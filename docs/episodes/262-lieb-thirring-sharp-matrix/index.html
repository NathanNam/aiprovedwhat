One bound state is the best?

Three AI preprints claim the one-dimensional Lieb–Thirring conjecture, for scalar and matrix wells

The claim, in plain words

Put a quantum particle on a line, in a potential well. It can sit in a few bound states, each with a negative energy. Lieb and Thirring bounded the sum of the sizes of those energies, each raised to a power γ, by a constant times an integral of the well’s depth to the power γ + 1/2. Their three-dimensional version gave a short proof that ordinary matter is stable.

What is the best constant? In one dimension, above γ = 3/2, a crowd of states wins; that was known by 1978. For γ between 1/2 and 3/2, Lieb and Thirring conjectured in 1976 that the best well holds a single bound state, a soliton-shaped well. The lower end was proved in 1998, but a 2020 survey by Frank called the middle range open. At γ = 1 the best proved constant was about 1.45 times the classical one, against a conjectured 1.15.

Result 262 is three OpenAI preprints, 73 pages in all. The first, 21 pages, dated September 23, 2026, claims the conjecture for every γ strictly between 1/2 and 3/2. The case γ = 1 had been posted on September 9 by Larry Read, then with Marvin Schulz, and the OpenAI paper credits it; Read’s own paper says a first version of its proof was developed by an AI model. The second, 24 pages, dated October 5, claims the same constant for wells whose depth is a matrix of any finite size, even when the matrices do not commute. The third, 28 pages, claims that equality holds only for independent soliton wells, one per channel, in one fixed basis.

What it doesn’t claim

Anything in higher dimensions, or values of γ outside the open range.

The scalar theorem in full (docs)

Last checked October 27, 2026. The preprints have not been revised. In Lean, the scalar theorem is formalized in full: about 13,700 lines with no unproved steps, which we did not build. The matrix and equality papers are not formalized. OSVerify has not reviewed them, and we found no specialist comment.

How the proofs go

  1. A path through matrices. The key move walks a symmetric matrix from one value to its negative along a differential equation, and a parity count shows such a path exists. Integrating along it gives each bound state exactly its single-state share.
  2. Matrix wells. The new ingredient is a trace inequality comparing two matrix quantities. It lets the scalar argument go through even when the matrices share no common basis.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: with exact soliton wells, the single-state well wins below 3/2, every reflectionless well ties at exactly 3/2, and at γ = 2 wells with many states beat it by about 7%. Of 3,000 random wells, none beat the single-state constant at five sampled values of γ below 3/2.
  • Ours: twisting the basis of a matrix well along the line lowers the ratio, to about 93% at one twist rate and 70% at four times that rate.
  • Ours: a naive optimizer seemed to beat the theorem by 27%, using a spike thinner than its grid. Resolved properly, at γ = 1, it reached 99.8% and never more.
