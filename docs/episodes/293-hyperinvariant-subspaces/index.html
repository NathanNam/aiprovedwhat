The claim, in plain words

Every complex square matrix has an eigenvector, so in finite dimensions every operator maps some line into itself. The invariant subspace problem asks whether every operator on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space has a nontrivial closed invariant subspace. In Banach spaces Enflo and Read showed the answer can be no; on Hilbert space it is still open (Enflo posted a claimed proof in 2023 that no one has confirmed). A stronger demand is a hyperinvariant subspace: one respected by every operator that commutes with the given one. Douglas and Pearcy studied it in 1972, linking it to transitive algebras, and Lomonosov proved in 1973 that every nonscalar operator commuting with a nonzero compact operator has one. A July 2025 survey still called the general question unknown. Inside the hyperfinite II₁ factor, Haagerup and Schultz proved in 2009 that an operator has invariant projections whenever its Brown measure is not concentrated at one point.

Result 293 is two OpenAI papers, 63 pages, dated September 27, 2026, and published October 6. Both prove what amounts to a negative answer to the hyperinvariant subspace problem: on every infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space, a nonzero quasinilpotent operator whose commuting operators share no nonzero proper closed invariant subspace. Its commutant is then a proper transitive algebra, closed in the strong operator topology, which would answer the transitive algebra problem negatively too. The longer paper’s main theorem lives in the hyperfinite factor: for every irrational rotation angle, a weighted rotation with no nontrivial invariant projection in the factor, answering a question of Zhu, Fang and Shi; that in turn gives the Hilbert-space result.

What it doesn’t claim

The invariant subspace problem itself. The shorter paper’s operator even has many ordinary invariant subspaces, and that problem stays open.

How the proof goes

  1. Rigid ladders. The shorter paper stacks ladders over the 2-adic odometer, a clock that adds one with carries. Each ladder is a two-sided weighted shift whose weights shrink so fast both ways that their running product falls like e−5n²; its only invariant subspaces are tails. The nth power has norm at most e−10⌊(n+1)²/4⌋, at most e−300 by n = 10.
  2. A staircase that always goes down. As the clock ticks the weights drift, and a commuting operator can carry each tail one rung down on the next ladder. A proper hyperinvariant subspace would need a tail label that drops by at least one at every tick, impossible on a clock that preserves measure and mixes.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: for the Volterra operator, n! times the norm of its nth power is 0.5085 at n = 15, settling toward 1/2.
  • Ours: for a weighted rotation whose weight has logarithm integrating to −∞, the nth root of the norm of the nth power is 7×10−6 at n = 100,000; for a positive weight it tends to 0.622.
  • Ours: on a 16-step clock, the forced labels come back at least 16 rungs lower; cut to finitely many rungs, every tail is hyperinvariant.

2:52How the proof goes

The shorter paper stacks ladders over the 2-adic odometer, a clock that adds one with carries. Each ladder is a two-sided weighted shift whose weights shrink so fast both ways that their running product falls like e^(−5n²), so rigid that its only invariant subspaces are tails. The operator’s powers collapse: the nth power has norm at most e^(−10⌊(n+1)²/4⌋), at most e^(−300) by n = 10. As the clock ticks, the weights drift, and a commuting operator can carry each tail one rung down on the next ladder. A proper hyperinvariant subspace would need a tail label that drops by at least one at every tick, impossible on a clock that preserves measure and mixes. Our computer runs the finite picture: a 16-step cycle comes back at least 16 rungs below where it started. Cut to finitely many rungs, the trick fails, as it must: in finite dimensions every nonscalar matrix has a hyperinvariant subspace.
