Mahler, solved?

The smallest volume products of convex bodies, and symplectic balls in polar products

The claims, in plain words

The polar of a convex body K symmetric about the origin is K° = {y : ⟨x, y⟩ ≤ 1 for every x in K}. A large body has a small polar, and the volume product |K| |K°| does not change under invertible linear maps. In the plane a disk gives π² ≈ 9.87, a regular hexagon 9 and a square 8. Ellipsoids give the largest value (the Blaschke–Santaló inequality). In the late 1930s Mahler asked for the smallest; the conjectured answer in n dimensions is 4n/n!, the value of a cube and its polar cross-polytope, shared by all Hanner polytopes, built from intervals by products and by convex hulls of copies in complementary directions. Mahler proved the plane inequality, Reisner showed that only parallelograms attain it, and Iriyeh and Shibata proved dimension three in 2020; Saint-Raymond (unconditional bodies) and Reisner (zonoids) settled special classes, and Bourgain and Milman proved the bound up to a factor exponential in the dimension.

Without symmetry, one minimizes over the position of the origin (the minimizer is the Santaló point), and the conjectured minimum is (n + 1)n+1/(n!)², attained by simplices: 27/4 = 6.75 for a triangle, 64/9 ≈ 7.11 for a tetrahedron. Mahler and Meyer settled the plane, Meyer and Reisner polytopes with at most n + 3 vertices, and a 2026 preprint of Chen, Li, Xi and Xu treats dimension three. From symplectic geometry, Artstein-Avidan, Karasev and Ostrover showed in 2014 that a conjecture of Viterbo would imply the symmetric Mahler conjecture.

Result 087 is three OpenAI preprints, all dated September 22, 2026. The first (26 pages) proves |K| |K°| ≥ 4n/n! for every origin-symmetric convex body in every dimension, with equality exactly for linear images of Hanner polytopes. The second (72 pages) proves that the product at the Santaló point is at least (n + 1)n+1/(n!)², with equality exactly for simplices. Both derive sharp functional versions for log-concave functions and their convex conjugates, with constants 4n for even functions and en in general. The third (19 pages) puts K in the position coordinates and K° in the momentum coordinates of phase space and proves, for n ≥ 2, that standard symplectic balls of every capacity below 4 fit inside and none larger: the Gromov width is 4. Since a ball of capacity 4 has volume 4n/n! and symplectic maps preserve volume, this proves the symmetric inequality a second time, without the equality cases.

What it doesn’t claim

Viterbo’s broader conjecture is false in general, as Haim-Kislev and Ostrover showed, and the papers claim nothing about it beyond these polar products. An embedding of a ball of capacity exactly 4 is not claimed. The symplectic route gives no equality cases.

How the proofs go

  1. Symmetric: a conformal lens. A conformal map from the disk onto a lens-shaped region turns uniform angles on the circle into a uniform vertical coordinate; it is a rotation of a map Gross used for a conformal Skorokhod embedding. For a body cut out by finitely many symmetric strips, random boundary angles determine complex points, and a mass estimate at an isolated holomorphic zero, proved with Stokes’ theorem, shows that certain probabilities add up to at least one. An exact identity turns that sum into an integral, over the body, of the volume covered by certain simplices inside the polar body, which gives the bound. For equality, every triple of points in the norm whose unit ball is the polar body has a metric median, and a classification of Hansen and Lima gives the Hanner polytopes.
  2. General: Gaussian projections onto cones. Klartag’s correspondence between a body and a pair of dual cones turns the volume product into a product of Laplace integrals. Gaussian vectors projected onto the two cones, with a carefully chosen bias and covariance, give maps into the cones, and their Jacobians with Jensen’s inequality reduce everything to showing that an error term is at most zero. The derivative matrices need not commute, so the paper compares them with spectral thresholds of a linear Gaussian matrix, using Hermite expansions and divided differences. Its scalar inequalities are proved with finite rational certificates, plus interpolation and tail estimates, and supplementary programs check them. Equality forces the matrices to commute, so the cone splits into half-lines and the body is a simplex.
  3. Symplectic: balls from holomorphic vanishing. If holomorphic functions vanish to order k at a single common zero, the region where their squared sizes add up to less than one, with a symplectic form built from those functions, contains balls of every capacity below πk. Using powers of the inverse lens map, the paper maps that region into the body times a polar scaled by about πk/4; dividing out leaves balls of every capacity below 4. A supporting cylinder and Gromov’s nonsqueezing theorem give the matching upper bound.

The numbers in the video

  • A short Python script computes volume products: π² ≈ 9.87 for a disk, 9 for a regular hexagon, 8 for a square, 27/4 = 6.75 for a triangle at its Santaló point and 64/9 ≈ 7.11 for a tetrahedron. The cube, the cross-polytope and the prism over an octahedron give exactly 4n/n!.
  • Among random origin-symmetric polytopes with at least 2n + 2 vertices, the smallest products found were 8.0003, 10.6746 and 10.6707 in dimensions 2, 3 and 4 (the conjectured minima are 8, 10.6667 and 10.6667). Among random general polytopes minimized over the center, the smallest were 6.7533 and 7.2706 in dimensions 2 and 3 (against 6.75 and 7.1111). No computation can test every body.
