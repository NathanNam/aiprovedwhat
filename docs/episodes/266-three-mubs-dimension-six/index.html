Only three in dimension six?

The claim, in plain words

Two ways of measuring a quantum system are mutually unbiased if, after one gives a definite result, every outcome of the other is equally likely, like position and momentum. For a system with d states, each measurement is an orthonormal basis, and unbiased means every vector of one basis overlaps every vector of the other with squared size exactly 1/d.

At most d + 1 bases can be pairwise unbiased, and that many exist whenever d is a prime power. Dimension 6, the first that is not a prime power, is different. Three bases are easy there, and Zauner conjectured in the 1990s that three is the maximum. Numerical searches never found a fourth: in our 100 random restarts none succeeded, and 79 stalled at 0.05125, the value Butterley and Hall reported in 2007. Before this release, all that was proved was that the number is 3, 4, 5 or 7.

Result 266 is two OpenAI preprints, 111 pages, dated September 24, 2026. The main paper, 76 pages, claims that there are no four mutually unbiased bases in dimension 6, so the maximum is exactly three. Its theorem is labelled computer-assisted, and holds under stated conditions on floating-point arithmetic and the compiler, for one documented run. The companion, 35 pages, proves by exact arithmetic a conjecture of Matolcsi, Ruzsa and Weiner: every 6 × 6 complex Hadamard matrix except those equivalent to Tao’s matrix satisfies a certain Fourier-vanishing condition. A second exact certificate rules out seven bases; with Weiner’s earlier theorem, the number is at most five, without relying on floating point.

What it doesn’t claim

Anything for dimension 10 or other non-prime powers, a classification of 6 × 6 Hadamard matrices, or anything about Zauner’s other conjecture, on symmetric measurements.

Update (October 26, 2026): OpenAI restored the main paper’s C++ source and saved numerical records for the computation that excludes a fourth basis, with a Python checker that verifies the consistency and coverage of the saved records. The folder holds the seven C++ programs and a small arithmetic probe, a build script, about 82 MB of saved records and recorded stage results, and the checker; OpenAI says the records match the October 4 run, and no compiled programs are included. OpenAI notes that the checker does not run the C++ programs and is not a proof of the C++ implementation or a check of compiler and hardware behaviour. We have not run the checker or the programs. Before this, the code and data were not in the public repository, as the video says; that is now out of date. Lean formalizes the companion’s exact results, at most five bases, in about 2.8 million lines with no unproved steps, which we did not build; it explicitly does not establish the bound of three. OSVerify has not reviewed them. An independent rebuild of the computation from the paper’s text, using interval arithmetic and itself produced with an AI model, also found no fourth basis. Apart from one brief enthusiastic blog comment, we found no assessment by specialists.

How the proofs go

  1. An exhaustive search. The computation splits the space of candidate bases into small regions, from about 152 million pairs of phase bins down to about 250,000 seed balls and then 22,000 smaller ones. In each region, a graph search finds no two orthonormal bases unbiased to each other and to the first two. 163 regions needed refining, and all were excluded.
  2. One run. The run took 75 minutes on October 4, ten days after the paper’s date, with ordinary floating point and hand-proved rounding margins, not interval arithmetic.
  3. A small example. Grassl showed that exactly 48 vectors are unbiased to both the standard basis and the Fourier basis. They form 16 bases, and no two of those are unbiased to each other (our computer confirms this).

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: we build 4 mutually unbiased bases in dimension 3, 5 in dimension 4 and 8 in dimension 7; in dimension 6 the same formula gives only two (other constructions give three).
  • Ours: the Fourier-vanishing condition holds for the Fourier family and other examples, and fails for Tao’s matrix.
