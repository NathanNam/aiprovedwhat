No more pieces than points, yet no choice?

A preprint claims that the Partition Principle does not imply the Axiom of Choice

The claim, in plain words

Cut a set into pieces. The Partition Principle says there are never more pieces than points: some one-to-one map sends each piece to a point. The Axiom of Choice gives such a map at once, by picking a point from every piece, but the Partition Principle asks for less: the point need not lie in its piece. For six points in pieces of sizes 1, 2 and 3 there are 120 one-to-one maps from pieces to points, and only 6 pick each piece’s point from inside it. For finite sets both always exist; the question is about infinite ones. Beppo Levi stated the principle in 1902, and Russell claimed, without proof, that it implies choice. Asaf Karagila has called the question the oldest open problem in set theory. Between November 2025 and January 2026 Frank Gilson posted three preprints claiming such a model; by September 2026 he had withdrawn the claims in all three.

Result 244 is one OpenAI preprint, 44 pages, dated September 24, 2026. It claims that if ZF is consistent, so is ZF plus the Partition Principle plus the failure of choice. Choice for ordinal-indexed families still holds there, but that already follows from the Partition Principle, as Pincus showed. A second deduction, from any countable transitive model with choice, gives a transitive symmetric extension with the same properties, the same ordinals, and no new countable sequences of old sets.

What it doesn’t claim

That choice is false or unneeded: choice still implies the Partition Principle. No atoms and no large cardinals are used.

How the proof goes

  1. A reduction. Following Ryan-Smith’s method: if every set is an image of one special set A times an ordinal, choice holds for ordinal-indexed families, and every image of A that cannot be well-ordered is the same size as A, then the Partition Principle holds.
  2. The model. Add many new subsets of the first uncountable cardinal, which serve as labels and form A, and keep only the sets that survive enough permutations of them. Supports are too small to list all the labels in order, so A cannot be well-ordered and choice fails. A second forcing lays copies of a custom-built monoid, called diagrams, over the labels; the monoid controls how groups of labels can overlap and combine.
  3. The heart. Theorem 6.3: every image of A that cannot be well-ordered is put in bijection with A, coherently across all symmetries. Choices inside the new model must respect every symmetry, so countably many bits of the new sets serve as fingerprints that pick out one of countably many candidates, and the pick moves along with every symmetry.
