One sample is enough?

A claimed matroid prophet inequality with one sample per element, against an adversary who sees everything

The claims, in plain words

A prophet inequality compares a gambler with a prophet. Nonnegative values arrive one at a time; the gambler must accept or reject each on the spot, while the prophet sees them all and takes the best. With independent values from known distributions and a single choice, the gambler can always get half of the prophet’s expected value, and one half is best possible (Krengel and Sucheston, crediting Garling). One rule accepts the first value above half the prophet’s expected maximum.

Matroids generalize the constraint: the accepted set must stay independent, like edges that never close a cycle in a graph. Kleinberg and Weinberg extended the one-half guarantee to every matroid when the distributions are known and the order reacts only to values already seen. Without the distributions, one sample from each distribution can stand in for knowledge, and for a single choice the largest sample is a good threshold. For general matroids, a one-sample 1/2 appeared in September 2026 for arrival orders fixed in advance; when the order may depend on the values, one sample gave guarantees shrinking like one over log log of the rank, and constants needed many samples per element.

Result 111 is one OpenAI preprint (27 pages) dated September 23, 2026. It claims a single rule, using one sample per element and no description of the distributions, that earns in expectation at least 2−310 times the prophet’s expected optimum on every finite matroid known in advance. The guarantee holds against an almighty adversary, who chooses the arrival order after seeing every sample, every value and the rule’s entire random seed. The values must be independent and nonnegative with finite expected optimum. The same argument gives a secretary rule that survives adversarial reordering after its observation phase.

What it doesn’t claim

No polynomial-time implementation is claimed, and the constant, about 5 × 10−94, is far too small to matter in practice. The point is that it is a constant: it does not depend on the matroid, its size or rank, or the distributions.

How the proof goes

  1. Fixed hidden weights. Start from fixed hidden weights. A random mask, drawn from the seed, reveals some of them before any arrival, and those labels are sacrificed. The target is the expected minimum over all arrival orders, so the adversary may pick a different order for every seed; the fixed-weight version reaches 2−293. A coupling transfers this to the sample model: use the samples on the mask and the true values elsewhere; whatever the seed, the glued vector has exactly the law of the values.
  2. Two branches. Weights are rounded down to powers of B = 232. With probability one half, a maximum branch accepts the first unmasked label that outranks every revealed one, by rounded weight and then by label; with probability one quarter the top label is unmasked and the second masked, so this branch averages a quarter of the largest rounded weight even in the worst order for each seed. Otherwise the main branch builds nested paths of flats, sets closed under the matroid’s span, from the revealed weights, group by group from low to high, and accepts greedily within the layers between alternate flats; nesting keeps the union independent in every arrival order.
  3. Three losses. Lighter arrivals may block a heavy group: independent guards enlarge each flat one step early, and exposing them backwards shows that a fixed fraction of the revealed labels stay safe. Those safe labels were revealed, so they are sacrificed: a counting bound on marked pivot patterns, with a union bound, shows that enough rank remains on unsacrificed labels. Enlarging the flats can reduce rank: a telescoping conditional-rank identity charges this only once along each path. After thinning, the remaining rank is a lower bound on the accepted count, for every order at once.
  4. The constant. When no single label dominates, the main branch earns tγ/(16κ) of the rounded optimum, with t = 2−140, γ = 2−16 and κ = 2100; otherwise the maximum branch gives more. Mixing the branches and undoing the rounding gives tγ/(32κB) = 2−293; the theorem states 2−310, with slack.

The numbers in the video

  • Known distributions, threshold half the expected maximum, worst arrival order: the gambler kept 0.65, 0.63 and 0.60 of the prophet’s value for exponential, uniform and Pareto families, and 1/(2 − 0.01) ≈ 0.503 on the two-point example where one half is tight.
  • The complete graph with 4 vertices has 38 forests and 16 spanning trees. With edge weights 1, 2, 3, 10, 20 and 30, a naive rule facing light edges first takes 6, against the prophet’s 53.
  • The maximum branch, computed exactly over all masks in the worst order, averaged at least 0.251 times the largest weight on 300 random weight vectors.
  • Gluing samples on the mask to values elsewhere: coordinate means within 0.4% of the values’.
  • 2−310 ≈ 4.79 × 10−94; 2−293 ≈ 6.28 × 10−89.
