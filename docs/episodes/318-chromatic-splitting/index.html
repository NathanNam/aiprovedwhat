Does it split?

Five AI preprints on Hopkins’s chromatic splitting conjecture: a filtration, and counterexamples

The claims, in plain words

At a prime p, stable homotopy theory sorts the sphere into chromatic heights, 0, 1, 2 and up. To rebuild everything up to height n, you glue height n onto the heights below it, along an overlap: the part of the height-n world that the lower heights can see. Around 1993 Hopkins conjectured that this overlap is as simple as it could be.

In the strong form, the overlap is a wedge of 2ⁿ shifted copies of lower-height spheres, one for each subset of the numbers 1 to n. Our computer lists them at height 3: 8 pieces, shifted by 0, −1, −3, −4, −5, −6, −8 and −9. In the weak form, at least the first piece, the lower sphere itself, splits off.

Height 1 can be done by hand, and height 2 was known at primes 5 and up and at 3. At the prime 2, Beaudry showed in 2017 that the strong form fails, and Beaudry, Goerss and Henn later showed the weak form still holds. In 2024 Barthel, Schlank, Stapleton and Weinstein showed the rational shadow is right at every height. At height 3 and above, the integral question was open, apart from one partial result posted this August.

Result 318 is five OpenAI papers, 372 pages, dated September 25 to 27 and made public October 6, 2026. They give five answers. First, for primes bigger than n + 1, the overlap is built from exactly the predicted pieces, glued in order in a filtration; a second paper does height 3 explicitly. Third, at height 3 and primes 5 and up, that gluing cannot be fully undone: the strong wedge form fails.

Fourth, at height 3 and the prime 3, the overlap cannot be built from lower spheres in finitely many steps at all. Fifth, the weak form, for the p-completed sphere, fails at heights p and p + 1, for primes 5 and up and 7 and up: the lower sphere does not even split off. Our computer maps the claims onto heights and primes, and the filtration and the weak failures never meet. Not claimed: the weak form at height 3, or at primes large compared with the height, where several authors expect the conjecture to hold, or anything at the prime 2.

How the proofs go

  1. A rational test at height 3. The filtration and the strong counterexample rest on p-adic geometry, Lubin–Tate and Drinfeld towers and the Fargues–Fontaine curve, building on Barthel, Schlank, Stapleton and Weinstein; the counterexample also builds on a human preprint from August 2026. The strong counterexample is a rational test: at height 3, a canonical map is nonzero in degree −3, where the wedge would force zero. Our computer’s bookkeeping shows there is room for such a test first at height 3, and it reproduces the Lie algebra ranks, 0, 8, 20 and 35, that the paper cites from a support script not included in the release.
  2. One class that cannot survive. The weak counterexample is short and classical. It follows one class, β₁ to the power (p − 1)². At p = 5 that is β₁¹⁶, in stem 608. The class is alive one height down, but a vanishing line kills it at height p, so the lower sphere cannot split off. Our computer checks where the prime bounds 5 and 7 come from.
