VP ≠ VNP and Valiant’s whole hierarchy?

OpenAI’s result 379, now seven papers, claims Euclidean prices for tensors, a strict hierarchy VF ⊊ VBP ⊊ VP ⊊ VNP over every field, and exp(c(log N)²/(log log N)²) circuit lower bounds for the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial and the permanent. Its Lean covers the headlines, not the rates.

The question

An arithmetic circuit builds a polynomial from variables and constants by adding, subtracting and multiplying. A huge sum can have a short circuit. Pick x or y from each of 3 pairs and multiply: the 8 products add up to (x₁ + y₁)(x₂ + y₂)(x₃ + y₃): 5 gates. The determinant of an m by m matrix has m factorial terms, and it too has small circuits. The permanent has the same terms, all with plus signs, and no comparably small circuits are known. Leslie Valiant’s class VP holds families of polynomial degree with circuits of polynomial size. VNP also allows a sum over all zero-one settings of polynomially many extra inputs. The permanent is complete for VNP outside characteristic two, and the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial over every field. Do the classes differ? It is the algebraic cousin of P versus NP.

OpenAI’s new witness lives on a grid. Label each edge of an n by n grid with one of n states. Give each vertex a table of variables, one for each way to label the edges that meet there. A labeling picks one entry at every vertex. Multiply them, then sum over all labelings: that is the grid’s state sum, and it is in VNP. For 3 by 3, with 3 states: 12 edges, over half a million labelings, 225 variables.

The claims, in plain words

Result 379 has a new title: Euclidean tensor geometry and its applications to complexity and combinatorics. On November 24, OpenAI added seven papers to it, and said two of them subsume its November 23 paper, VP and VNP are different, which stays available, unchanged, but is no longer listed. The first paper, 86 pages, prices tensors whose indices sit at points in space, over every field. A bit shared by two sites costs their distance. A bit shared by more sites costs the mean width of their convex hull: for a triangle, half its perimeter. In our example, a triangle with sides of length 1: a bit shared by all three corners costs 1.5; three separate wires cost 3. Such prices give lower bounds.

The second paper, 21 pages, claims that over every field, formulas, algebraic branching programs, circuits and VNP form a strict hierarchy: VF, VBP, VP, VNP, each strictly inside the next. The grid’s state sum needs circuits of size n^(c(log n)^(1/12)). On complete binary trees, the state sum needs branching programs of size n^(c√(log log n)). On paths, it needs formulas of size n^(c log log n), and so does the determinant. All three bounds allow limits, in border complexity.

The third paper, 80 pages, is about the classic complete polynomials. Over every field, circuits for the directed Hamiltonian cycle polynomial of order N need at least exp(c(log N)²/(log log N)²) gates once N is large, for an absolute constant c. The same holds for the permanent outside characteristic two; in characteristic two, the permanent equals the determinant. It holds even for circuits that only approximate the degree N coefficients, and carries over to determinants that represent these polynomials. The family also claims a matrix multiplication exponent of exactly 9/4, results on Fourier transforms and integer multiplication, and the sunflower conjecture of Erdős and Rado; matrix multiplication and sunflowers get episodes of their own. Not claimed, by our reading: anything about Boolean circuits, so not P versus NP. And no constants are given.

In the papers’ words. Euclidean prices (86 pages, abstract): “a bit shared by two sites costs exactly their distance”; “a bit shared among finitely many sites costs the mean width of their convex hull, normalized to give a segment its length. For a triangle this is half its perimeter.” Hierarchy (21 pages, Theorem 1.1): over every field F, VF ⊊ VBP ⊊ VP ⊊ VNP, witnessed by state sums on an n × n grid (circuit size n^(Ω((log n)^(1/12)))), a complete binary tree (ABP size n^(Ω(√(log log n)))) and a path (formula size n^(Ω(log log n))), all in degree-component border complexity; the determinant also needs formulas of size n^(Ω(log log n)). Hamiltonian cycle and permanent (80 pages, Theorem 1.1): for an absolute c > 0 and a threshold depending only on the characteristic, the degree-component border size of the directed Hamiltonian cycle polynomial of order N is at least exp(c(log N)²/(log log N)²) over every field, and the same for the permanent outside characteristic two.

The rest of the family, not covered in depth here: the matrix multiplication exponent is nine fourths (the matching upper bound is OpenAI’s earlier claim, our 107), finite Fourier transforms, integer multiplication on tapes and an exponential bound for sunflowers.

How the bounds compare, and Anthropic’s AN08

By our arithmetic, write each bound as e to a power of log N. The grid’s power is (log n)^(13/12), below the 5/4 of the November 23 paper, but for a different polynomial. For Hamiltonian cycle and the permanent, the new power is (log N)²/(log log N)². That also beats Anthropic’s November 23 claim, our Specials episode AN08. Anthropic makes Claude, which helps make this series. Anthropic states 2^(c(log m)^(4/3)), for exact and approximating circuits, and its Lean proof covers that bound. On November 23, Anthropic’s rate was the larger; now OpenAI’s is, eventually, whatever the constants. With both constants set to one, and base-two logs, it pulls ahead only when N has about 300 digits.

How the proofs go

How the grid proof goes, by the paper’s account. Give each edge an axis of its own, its two ends two units apart. The grid’s coefficient table is then a set of independent wires, priced at their total length times log n. A small circuit writes that table as a limited number of terms, each of low rank across some balanced cut. For each term, move a supply point a little toward the wire ends on one side. In our picture, three wires from the center to the ends of three axes have total length 3; moving the hub to the center of the ends cuts it to about 2.45. The saving grows like the square root of the number of crossing edges, while low rank costs only a thin wire. So each term, and their sum, is cheaper than the grid: a contradiction, unless the circuit is large. Every balanced cut of an r by r grid crosses at least r/3 edges; with r near (log n)^(1/6), the square root gives the 1/12.

The Hamiltonian cycle paper splits on a geometric question: are there bounded-degree graphs whose vertices, placed anywhere in these price spaces, stay about as close together as their edges are long? If not, one placement makes every low-rank term cheap at once, and an explicit tensor costs more than their sum. If so, the geometry compresses tensor networks, and a program that counts its own computation paths could simulate its count with a small circuit and add one: a contradiction. Either way, the bound holds. The paper builds on Strassen’s asymptotic spectrum and, among its precedents, cites Anthropic’s 3-SAT paper, our AN03, and OpenAI’s ETH paper, result 378.

The Lean proof, and what we read

OpenAI’s Lean, not run by us, is narrower. By its scope note, it proves the whole hierarchy for exact circuits, stated in a comparator file that imports only Mathlib. For the grid it rules out polynomial-size border circuits, but not the 1/12 rate. For Hamiltonian cycle and the permanent it proves superpolynomial bounds for exact circuits, as on November 23, not the new rate. By our reading, the final theorem takes its VP versus VNP step from the November 23 proof. Our text search of over 21,000 files finds no sorry or axiom. The pull request says the Lean build was not run.

Our reading (not a machine check): the hierarchy comparator imports only Mathlib and states VF F ⊂ VBP F ∧ VBP F ⊂ VP F ∧ VP F ⊂ VNP F for every field F, with formulas as expression trees (size = number of +, −, × nodes), branching programs with affine-linear edge labels (size = vertices plus edges), circuits with free variables and constants, and VNP as Boolean sums of VP families. In Strict.lean, the VP ⊊ VNP part comes from vp_ssubset_vnp, which calls the November 23 proof’s vp_ne_vnp; the grid route in SeparationFromFlats.lean is also proved, and both are in the import closure. The comparator configuration does not enable the nanoda kernel. We did not read the proofs.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours (the hierarchy paper’s opening example, n = 3): choosing x or y from each of three pairs gives 8 products, which add up to (x₁ + y₁)(x₂ + y₂)(x₃ + y₃): 3 additions and 2 multiplications, 5 gates.
  • Ours: the 3 × 3 grid with 3 states has 12 edges, 3^12 = 531,441 labelings and 225 variables (corner tables 9 entries, side tables 27, centre 81); the state sum has degree 9. A brute-force check on the 2 × 2 grid with 2 states: 16 monomials, each with coefficient 1.
  • Ours: a triangle with sides of length 1 has perimeter 3; a bit shared by its three corners costs half the perimeter, 1.5 (the mean width, checked numerically: 1.501), while three separate wires cost 3, as in the Euclidean paper’s Figure 1.
  • Ours, the hub picture: three unit wires from the centre to the ends of three axes have total length 3; moving the hub to the centre of the ends, (1/3, 1/3, 1/3), gives 3·√(2/3) = √6 ≈ 2.449; the first-order rate is √3, the square root of the number of crossing edges.
  • Ours, by brute force over all splits: the smallest balanced cut (each side more than a third of the vertices, at most two thirds) of a 2 × 2, 3 × 3 and 4 × 4 grid crosses 2, 3 and 4 edges; the paper’s general bound ⌈r/3⌉ gives 1, 1 and 2. With log₂ n = 4096, r = (log n)^(1/6) = 4 and √r = 2.
  • Ours, comparing exponents (logs base 2, every constant set to 1, illustration only): the Hamiltonian-cycle bound’s log₂ is (log₂ N)²/(log₂ log₂ N)²; it passes the November 23 paper’s (log₂ N)^(5/4) at log₂ N = 256 (N has 78 digits) and AN08’s (log₂ N)^(4/3) at log₂ N ≈ 982 (N has 296 digits). The ratio to AN08’s exponent grows without bound, so with any constants it is eventually larger. The grid bound is n^(c(log n)^(1/12)) = 2^(c(log n)^(13/12)), for a different polynomial.
  • Ours, text only: the import closure of the hierarchy comparator’s solution module (OAI.Hierarchy.Strict) has 21,408 OpenAI files and 2,492,880 lines; outside libraries Mathlib, Batteries, complexitylib, cslib, lemmata and fixed-point-theorems; Lean v4.34.1; no sorry, admit tactic, axiom declaration or native_decide in those files (the comparator challenge has its sorry by design). The November 23 VPVNP closure is now 3,189 files, 472,948 lines.
