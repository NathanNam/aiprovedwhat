The claim, in plain words

Take two functions F and G on the plane. Read F a distance t to the right and G the same distance up, multiply them, and integrate over t against 1/t, the Hilbert kernel. This is the triangular Hilbert transform; the question is whether it is bounded from L3 × L3 to L3/2. Pairing with a third function gives a symmetric form in which each of three functions sees one pair of the variables a, b and c, and the three pairs form a triangle. That cycle is what defeats energy methods that telescope across scales for bipartite arrangements; Kovač identified the triangle as an obstacle to his framework.

With the kernel cut to scales between ε and R, earlier bounds grew with the number of scales: Tao proved bounds growing slower than the logarithm for one-dimensional relatives, Zorin-Kranich extended this to the triangle, and Durcik, Kovač and Thiele proved power-type cancellation, which carried to the symmetric point still grows like the square root of log(R/ε). A bound uniform in the scales at the symmetric point is Problem 13 in Thiele’s section of a 2017 problem list, which also asks about a dyadic model.

Result 082 is three OpenAI preprints. The first (27 pages, September 24, 2026) proves the maximal estimate: the supremum over both truncation endpoints, chosen separately at each point, is bounded from L3 × L3 to L3/2 with an absolute constant, for complex inputs; this gives principal values almost everywhere and in L3/2, and the symmetric scalar estimate of Thiele’s problem. The second (37 pages, October 5) strengthens this to annular variation bounds for every r > 2, proving all the matrix and heat estimates it needs. The third (11 pages, October 5) bounds the dyadic model: for real inputs, the total of absolute local contributions over any finite set of scales is at most 40 times the product of the norms.

What it doesn’t claim

Only the symmetric point, three equal exponents 3, is claimed; other exponents in the expected range are not.

How the proof goes

  1. Matrices on a triangle. After a change of variables, the inputs become matrices around the triangle of coordinates a, b and c, and a localized piece of the form is a cyclic trace of their product. At each vertex the paper places the two incident matrices side by side as one matrix R and uses the energy tr((R*R)3/2), which scales like the form. A mixed trace inequality, with constants independent of the matrix size, bounds the cyclic trace by quadratic costs from the Hessians of these energies.
  2. Gaussian heat flow. Integrating the energies over Gaussian centers whose sum is zero, their decrease as the variance grows pays for the quadratic costs at every scale; the total is bounded by the initial energies, which are bounded by sums of cubed L3 norms, so one budget covers all scales.
  3. Switching scales. For the maximal estimate each output point picks its own truncation, so entries of the dual matrix switch on and off and the energy can jump. The total of the jumps is bounded by the L3 masses, through row and column norms and one-dimensional maximal functions; a kernel comparison passes to hard truncations. The variation paper adds a frequency-block estimate and groups endpoints by dyadic radius; the dyadic paper averages the energy over the four children allowed by the binary constraint.

The numbers in the video

  • A short Python script implements the dyadic form of the third paper on inputs over grids of 8, 16 and 32 cells, counting all scales. It checks the trace formula against a direct sum, gives a one-scale example worth exactly one unit (the product of the three L3 norms), and runs a gradient-ascent search from random starts: the largest ratios found were 1.99, 2.10 and 2.05, far below the paper’s bound of 40. A search is not a proof.

3:11Energy and heat

After a change of variables, the three inputs become matrices around the triangle of coordinates a, b and c, and a localized piece of the form becomes a cyclic trace of their product. At each vertex, the paper places the two incident matrices side by side as one matrix R, and uses the energy: the trace of R*R to the power 3/2, which scales like the form itself. A mixed trace inequality, with constants independent of the matrix size, bounds the cyclic trace by quadratic costs from the Hessians of these energies. Then comes Gaussian heat flow. The paper integrates the energies over Gaussian centers whose sum is zero; as the variance grows, their decrease pays for the quadratic costs at every scale. The total is bounded by the initial energies, which are bounded by sums of cubed L³ norms, so one budget covers all scales at once. For the maximal estimate, each output point picks its own truncation, so entries of the dual matrix switch on and off at their own scales, and the energy can jump. The key new estimate bounds the total of these jumps by the L³ masses: the derivative in one entry is controlled by its row and column norms, and one-dimensional maximal functions control those uniformly in the switching scales. A comparison of kernels then passes from smooth to hard truncations. The variation paper adds a frequency-block estimate and groups hard endpoints by dyadic radius. The dyadic paper averages the same energy over the four children allowed by the binary constraint.

4:49Where it stands

None of the three preprints is peer reviewed, and none has been revised. The maximal estimate has a Lean formalization, which we have not run; the variation and dyadic theorems do not. The dyadic paper takes its matrix inequalities from the first paper and reproduces real-matrix versions of their proofs. None of the three is among the papers OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If they hold, the triangular Hilbert transform is bounded at the symmetric point, with maximal and variation bounds and principal values, and the dyadic model is bounded at the same exponents. Other exponents in the expected range are not claimed.
