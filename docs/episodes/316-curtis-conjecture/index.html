Only the famous ones?

The claim, in plain words

The stable homotopy groups of spheres are famously hard to compute. Each class in them gives a map into an infinite loop space, QS⁰, and so casts a shadow in its mod 2 homology, called its Hurewicz image. That homology is completely known, and it is enormous: our computer counts 426 dimensions in degree 14, and about 77 trillion in degree 126. Which of these shadows are actually cast by maps from spheres?

Some are. The Hopf invariant one classes eta, nu and sigma, in degrees 1, 3 and 7, cast shadows, and so do the Kervaire invariant one classes, θⱼ, in degrees 2, 6, 14, 30, 62 and perhaps 126. Adams showed in 1960 that the Hopf classes stop at 7; Hill, Hopkins and Ravenel showed that the Kervaire classes stop by 126, and a 2024 preprint by Lin, Wang and Xu claims one in degree 126.

In 1975 Ed Curtis stated, as a theorem, that these are the only shadows. Two years later Peter May’s survey added a footnote: Wellington had found a gap in Curtis’s proof, and the result might be false. Since then it has been treated as an open problem, now called Curtis’s conjecture, with partial results by Zare and others.

Result 316 is one OpenAI paper, 37 pages, dated September 25 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims Curtis’s conjecture: in every positive degree, the mod 2 Hurewicz image is spanned by the images of eta, nu, sigma and the Kervaire classes that exist. With Hill, Hopkins and Ravenel’s theorem, that leaves at most 9 degrees, the last being 126; our computer counts 117 of the first 126 degrees where the image must vanish. As a corollary, it claims Eccles’s conjecture for every sphere of positive dimension.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: which Kervaire classes exist, Eccles’s conjecture beyond spheres, or anything at odd primes; that the listed classes do cast shadows comes from earlier work. It is a new argument, not a repair of Curtis’s.

On October 10, Nick Kuhn, whose lifting theorem the proof uses, wrote on the algebraic topology mailing list, about this paper and one other, “To the extent I have looked, I would not bet any money that either has a correct proof”, and that the punchline follows “a long rambling calculational bit which is precisely where plenty of humans (going back to Ed Curtis) have made errors in the past working in this area”. The message names no error, nobody replied, and we found no competing claim. OSVerify has not reviewed it.

How the proof goes

  1. Climb until the shadow dies. Take a class with a nonzero shadow, and push it up a ladder of adjoint maps until its homology image vanishes. At the last rung where it survives, the image must be a square. The mapping cone of the next map turns that square into a nonzero element of an algebraic object, built from Steenrod-algebra modules made of Dickson invariants.
  2. Only two ways out. Ten pages of algebra show that such an element can exist only in two patterns. One forces Hopf invariant one, so Adams’s theorem applies. The other uses Kuhn’s 2018 lifting theorem to cap the Adams filtration at two, and then Browder’s theorem gives Kervaire invariant one. Our computer spot-checks the algebra in small degrees: 492 cases with no violation, and the only ones that pass the key test sit at powers of two, from 2 up to 32, which correspond to the Kervaire degrees 2, 6, 14, 30 and 62. Running the Lambda algebra, Curtis’s own tool, our computer also finds that in each Kervaire stem up to 62 the relevant Ext group is a single class, hⱼ².
