Counting any graph?

A claimed fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme for perfect matchings in every graph, and an entropy bound for random perfect matchings

The claims, in plain words

A perfect matching pairs up all the vertices of a graph along its edges. Finding one is easy: Edmonds gave a polynomial-time algorithm in 1965. Counting them is another matter: Valiant showed in 1979 that exact counting is #P-complete, even for bipartite graphs, where the count is the permanent of a 0–1 matrix. For planar graphs, Pfaffian methods give exact counts.

The realistic goal is approximation: a fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS) gets within any relative error ε with probability at least 1 − δ, in time polynomial in the graph’s size, 1/ε and log(1/δ). Jerrum and Sinclair (1989) gave one for all matchings together, and for perfect matchings when near-perfect matchings, with two unmatched vertices, outnumber them at most polynomially. Jerrum, Sinclair and Vigoda removed that condition for bipartite graphs, approximating every nonnegative permanent. For general graphs the question stayed open, and Štefankovič, Vigoda and Wilmes showed that chains of the bipartite type fail on some graphs, whatever hole weights they use.

Result 113 is two OpenAI preprints, 45 pages each, dated September 23, 2026. The first claims an FPRAS for perfect matchings in every finite simple graph, in worst-case time polynomial in the input length, 1/ε and log(1/δ), returning zero with certainty when there is no perfect matching; the method also counts subgraphs with prescribed degrees and matchings of a given size. The second proves a pointwise entropy bound that settles a conjecture of Anari, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant: if x gives the probability of each edge in a random perfect matching of a graph on 2m vertices, the largest possible entropy H(x) of such a random matching satisfies F(x) − (2 − 2/m)·B(x) ≤ H(x) ≤ F(x), where F(x) = −Σ xe log xe and B(x) = −Σ (1 − xe) log(1 − xe).

What it doesn’t claim

The counting result is approximate and randomized: exact counting remains #P-complete. The entropy bound is pointwise and does not by itself give an algorithm; its optimized form gives log N ≤ max(F + B) ≤ log N + 3m − 2, the linear-error estimate Anari, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant asked for.

How the proof goes

  1. From the complete graph. The algorithm starts from the complete graph, whose perfect matchings are easy to count, and gradually lowers the weights of the non-edges of G. Up to a tiny bias, the count for G is the starting count times a product of ratios of successive partition functions, each estimated by sampling. Vertex scales keep the ratios with two vertices deleted balanced.
  2. An enlarged graph. Each edge becomes a weighted path, and weak clique edges are added in overlapping bags along a tree. A capacity bound built from bottleneck paths shows that, under balance, the extra edges at most double the partition function, so a sample uses only real edges at least half the time; a marked edge exposes the two-hole ratios for the next step.
  3. A sampler on pairs of matchings. The chain runs on a product of perfect-matching spaces, with local switches and exchanges of alternating cycles between two coordinates, avoiding the near-perfect states where the bipartite approach breaks down. A quadrangulation adapted to the bag labels gives an exact signed identity for a whole cycle, bounding the variance of sums of one-coordinate functions by their energy; replicating coordinates across a ladder of nearby weights gives a spectral gap for all functions. The bottom tier, at unit weights, is counted and sampled exactly by dynamic programming on the tree.
  4. The entropy bound. Shearer’s inequality gives H ≤ F. The lower bound uses Edmonds’ description of the perfect-matching polytope by odd cuts and the sharp estimate |supp x| − dim Fx ≤ 3m − 2, followed by a covariance calculation. A consequence: a graph on 2m vertices with k ≥ 2 edge-disjoint perfect matchings has at least km(1 − 1/k)2(m−1)(k−1) ≥ km/e2m−2 perfect matchings.

The numbers in the video

  • Exact counts: the Petersen graph has 6 perfect matchings, the three-dimensional cube 9, the four-dimensional cube 272, the complete graph on 10 vertices 945; on a random bipartite graph the count equals the permanent.
  • Fifteen ratios, each estimated from 4,000 samples while the weight of the Petersen graph’s non-edges inside the complete graph on 10 vertices was halved step by step, took 945 down to an estimate of 5.9 for the exact 6.
  • At the uniform point of the complete graph on 10 vertices (each edge with probability 1/9), the entropy bounds read 3.45 ≤ log 945 = 6.85 ≤ 10.99.
  • The counting corollary held for complete graphs up to 16 vertices (2,027,025 ≥ 3,435 for 16), and trivially for small cubes.

1:23The claims

Result 113 is two OpenAI preprints, of 45 pages each, dated September 23. The first claims an FPRAS for perfect matchings in every finite simple graph. For any relative error ε and failure probability δ, it returns an estimate within a factor 1 ± ε with probability at least 1 − δ, in worst-case time polynomial in the input length, 1/ε and log(1/δ). If there is no perfect matching, it returns zero with certainty. The same method counts subgraphs with prescribed degrees and matchings of a given size. The second preprint proves a pointwise entropy bound that settles a conjecture of Anari, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant. Fix the probability x of each edge in a random perfect matching. The largest possible entropy H of such a random matching is at most F(x) = −Σ x log x, and at least F − (2 − 2/m)·B, with B = −Σ (1 − x) log(1 − x), on a graph with 2m vertices. In our check, these bounds held at the uniform point of complete graphs. OpenAI published Lean formalizations: about 415 files and 66,000 lines for the counting algorithm, and 26 files for the entropy bound. We have not run the checks.

2:48How the proof goes

The counting algorithm starts from the complete graph, whose perfect matchings are easy to count, and gradually suppresses the non-edges of G by lowering their weights. The count for G is, up to a tiny bias, the starting count times a product of ratios of successive partition functions, and each ratio is estimated by sampling. In our check, 15 such ratios, each estimated from samples, took the 945 matchings of the complete graph on 10 vertices to an estimate of 5.9 for the Petersen graph’s 6. The difficulty is sampling. The algorithm keeps vertex scales so that ratios with two vertices deleted stay balanced, and it enlarges the graph: each edge becomes a weighted path, and weak clique edges are added in overlapping bags along a tree. A capacity bound built from bottleneck paths shows that, under balance, the extra edges at most double the partition function. So a sample uses only real edges at least half the time, and a marked edge exposes the two-hole ratios needed for the next step. The sampler runs on a product of perfect-matching spaces, with local switches and exchanges of alternating cycles between two coordinates, avoiding the near-perfect states where the bipartite approach breaks down. A quadrangulation adapted to the bag labels gives an exact signed identity for a whole cycle, which bounds the variance of sums of one-coordinate functions by their energy. Replicating coordinates across a ladder of nearby weights then gives a spectral gap for all functions. The bottom tier, at unit weights, is counted and sampled exactly by dynamic programming on the tree. For the entropy bound, Shearer’s inequality gives the upper side. The lower side uses Edmonds’ description of the perfect-matching polytope by odd cuts and a sharp rank estimate: the support of x exceeds the dimension of its minimal face by at most 3m − 2. A covariance calculation turns this into the entropy comparison. One consequence: a graph on 2m vertices with k edge-disjoint perfect matchings has at least k^m / e^(2m−2) perfect matchings. In our check, this held for complete graphs up to 16 vertices, and trivially for small cubes.

5:15Where it stands

The preprints are not peer reviewed and have not been revised. The Lean formalizations cover the counting algorithm, as a fixed randomized machine with its running time, and the entropy bound, which proves the face-dimension bound along the way; we have not run them. Neither paper is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, perfect matchings in every graph can be counted approximately in randomized polynomial time, answering the question Jerrum and Sinclair raised in 1989.
