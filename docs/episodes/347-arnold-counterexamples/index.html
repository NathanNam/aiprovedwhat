Fewer fixed points?

Five AI preprints claim counterexamples to strong forms of the Arnold conjecture

The claim, in plain words

Rotate a sphere about its axis and exactly two points stay put: the poles, the critical points of the height that drives it. In 1965 Arnold began to suspect this is no accident; his later, general form says a Hamiltonian diffeomorphism, the time-one map of an area-preserving flow driven by an energy that may change with time, should have at least as many fixed points as a smooth function must have critical points. On a torus, an upright doughnut’s height has four, and every Morse function needs four; but a degenerate function with a monkey saddle manages with three, which our computer confirms.

So the conjecture comes in strengths. The homological forms, counting Betti numbers over the rationals, finite fields or the integers, are proved for nondegenerate maps: by Fukaya and Ono and others, by Abouzaid and Blumberg, and by Bai and Xu and by Rezchikov. Stronger forms, for nondegenerate maps, compare with the fewest critical points of a stable Morse function allowed extra variables, or of a Morse function; a degenerate form compares with the fewest critical points of any smooth function. Golovko listed these forms in 2020, calling the Morse-number one still wide open. Positive results covered mainly symplectically aspherical manifolds, not the general case. On September 27, 2026, Hao Jiao posted a smooth Hamiltonian map of the product of two spheres with only two fixed points, found with help from ChatGPT, against the degenerate form there.

Result 347 is five OpenAI papers, made public October 6, 2026. The first, 12 pages, dated September 23, gives a Hamiltonian map of the quadric threefold with exactly three fixed points, while every smooth function there needs four; one fixed point is degenerate. The second, also dated September 23, gives nondegenerate examples in real dimension twelve that fall below the Morse number by any amount. Three papers dated October 5 break the stable Morse bound on simply connected Kähler manifolds. In dimension 22 the smallest example has stable Morse number 2,048, but the map has only 2,032 fixed points, and in families the gap grows without bound.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: anything against the proved homological bounds, which every example respects; one October paper even meets the integral bound of Bai and Xu exactly. The first counterexample claimed for the degenerate form is not theirs: the quadric paper cites Jiao’s. And only the quadric result is checked in Lean.

How the proof goes

  1. A symmetry trick. The trick is a symmetry. Take a Hamiltonian symmetry of finite order, and a short flow of an energy that the symmetry leaves unchanged. Composed, a point stays fixed only if the symmetry fixes it and the energy is critical there. Our computer shows this on a sphere: an energy with six critical points, of which only the two on the axis of a half-turn survive. In the quadric, the symmetry fixes a copy of the product of two spheres, and on it the paper writes down a function with exactly three critical points, one degenerate; our computer finds the three. Every function on the whole quadric needs four, because the cube of its basic class is twice a point.
  2. Topology the counts can't see. The nondegenerate examples use topology that ordinary counts cannot see. In the twelve-dimensional one, the fundamental group is a large power of the alternating group on five letters, which needs ever more generators. A related ideal needs only two, and handle moves turn that gap into missing fixed points. The October papers build simply connected Kähler manifolds from blow-ups, with torsion at the primes two and three. A half-turn fixes four copies of a smaller piece, and the counts come out at least sixteen short of the stable Morse number. Our computer matches every printed count.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: the quadric paper's function on the product of two spheres has exactly 3 critical points (values 0 and ±2.598), one degenerate; every function on the quadric threefold needs 4.
  • Ours: on a torus, an upright doughnut's height has 4 critical points; sin x sin y sin(x+y) has 3, one a degenerate monkey saddle.
  • Ours: the alternating group A5 has 2,280 ordered generating pairs, so at most 19 copies can share two generators (Hall).
