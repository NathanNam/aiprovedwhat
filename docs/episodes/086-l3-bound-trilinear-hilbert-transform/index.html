The claim, in plain words

Take three functions on the line. At each point x, multiply the first at x − t, the second at x − 2t and the third at x − 3t, and integrate against 1/t, the kernel of the Hilbert transform, as a principal value. This is the trilinear Hilbert transform T. Scaling pairs three inputs in L3 with an output in L1, since 1/3 + 1/3 + 1/3 = 1.

With two functions this is the bilinear Hilbert transform from Calderón’s work, bounded by Lacey and Thiele in 1997 and 1999 using time-frequency analysis. That transform is symmetric under matched linear waves. Three functions bring a quadratic symmetry: the coefficients −1, 3, −3, 1 cancel every constant, line and parabola along x, x − t, x − 2t, x − 3t, so matched quadratic waves pass through, and the output only picks up a quadratic wave. Wave packets built on linear frequencies are not adapted to this symmetry. Each scale on its own is easy; cutting the kernel to N scales, the elementary bound grows in proportion to N, and in 2015 Tao used higher-order Fourier analysis to show it grows more slowly than that. Hu and Lie proved bounds for curved versions; for straight lines with slopes 1, 2 and 3, boundedness remained open in their account.

Result 086 is one OpenAI preprint, 93 pages, dated October 5, 2026. Its theorem: there is a finite constant C such that ‖T(f1, f2, f3)‖1 ≤ C ‖f1‖3 ‖f2‖3 ‖f3‖3, so T extends to a bounded map from L3 × L3 × L3 to L1. The paper presents this as resolving the L3 case of the trilinear Hilbert transform conjecture for slopes 1, 2 and 3.

What it doesn’t claim

The conjecture asks for a broader range of exponents, and other slopes are not treated. The constant is not computed. Transforms with more functions are not claimed.

Last checked October 13, 2026. The preprint has not been revised, and OpenAI published no Lean formalization of this result. OSVerify refereed the paper (the October 5 version) and reported one major issue and two minor ones, each with a proposed repair. The major one: Lemma 8.6, a counting lemma for bad-density chains, states its nesting hypothesis only before the last position, but the proof uses it at the last one too; the proposed repair leaves the lemma’s bound unchanged, and where the lemma is applied both inclusions are available. The minor ones: an identity in the proof of Lemma 5.3 omits a conditional-expectation term for general inputs, and a convention in Section 9.1 is stated in the wrong order. The report says its findings “do not necessarily establish that the paper’s main theorem is false.” We have seen no other expert review.

How the proof goes

  1. Absorbing the scales. A fourth function turns T into a four-linear form, split into local pieces on dyadic intervals, with kernels that integrate to zero whenever any one of the four points is held fixed. A stopping argument with a maximal function reduces everything to one estimate: on a normalized tree, the length-weighted sum of the pieces is at most the root’s length times A + c·CN, where CN is the best constant for N scales. Since CN is finite (at most of order N), a small c can be absorbed, and the bound no longer depends on N.
  2. Structure at one scale. A large local form forces one of the inputs to correlate with a generalized quadratic phase. The paper uses the degree-two case of the quantitative inverse theorem of Leng, Sah and Sawhney for Gowers norms, and proves the continuous chart and localization statements the singular integral needs. Private orthogonal coordinates make the coefficients of each selected column recoverable, and a compression lemma controls products of inherited projections uniformly in depth, at the cost of a power of a logarithm.
  3. Gains against losses. Curvature is localized jointly in all four arguments, and a short-block comparison costs only a small power of the block length. Each matched column gets one fixed mode expansion; frequency graphs separate errors with square-summable energy from coherent components; and a counting lemma, applied with all four cancellations, bounds the total mass of progression tests on scales of small density with a power saving. The parameters are chosen so that every gain beats every loss, and the absorption closes.

The numbers in the video

  • A short Python script confirms that the coefficients −1, 3, −3, 1 cancel constants, lines and parabolas but not cubes, while 1, −2, 1 cancel only constants and lines.
  • With three Gaussian inputs, the trilinear output has L1 norm 2.9753. After matched quadratic waves of strength 0.5, 1 and 2 the output changes only by a quadratic wave, pointwise to rounding, so its L1 norm stays 2.9753. For the bilinear transform with two of the same Gaussians, matched linear waves leave the L1 norm at 4.33, while matched quadratic waves shrink it to 1.89, 1.34 and 0.95. No computation can test every input.
