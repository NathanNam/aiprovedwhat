The Hilbert transform along Lipschitz directions

The claim, in plain words

Give every point of the plane a direction, a unit vector v(x). At each point, integrate a function along the straight line through that point in its own direction, against the Hilbert kernel 1/t, for ε < |t| < a. If the field is constant, this is the one-dimensional Hilbert transform on parallel lines, and every truncation is bounded on L2 with norm at most 2 Si(π) ≈ 3.70. When the direction varies, nearby points sample different lines and the operator is no longer a Fourier multiplier.

Stein asked whether, for a Lipschitz field, this transform obeys a uniform weak L2 bound when the length a is at most a fixed multiple of one over the Lipschitz constant. Lacey and Li trace the question to Zygmund’s conjecture on averages over shrinking segments in direction v(x), and recall a Besicovitch obstruction: for the averaging operator, Hölder continuity of any exponent below one is not enough. Bourgain proved an L2 theorem for averages along analytic fields; Lacey and Li proved single-annulus estimates for measurable directions and a Lipschitz Kakeya maximal theorem; Guo proved single-annulus variation bounds for Lipschitz fields; Bateman and Thiele handled fields depending on one coordinate; Guo and Thiele, then Di Plinio and Parissis, handled lacunary roundings; and Di Plinio, Guo, Thiele and Zorin-Kranich showed that uniform bounds on vertical frequency bands would give the full short bound.

Result 083 is one OpenAI preprint, 87 pages, dated September 25, 2026. Its theorem: there are absolute constants a* and C* such that, for every unit vector field with Lipschitz constant at most one, every truncation at outer length a*, with any smaller inner length, has L2 operator norm at most C*. The principal value is therefore bounded on L2, which is stronger than the weak bound Stein asked for. By scaling, a field with Lipschitz constant L gets the same bound at length a*/L, and the field may depend on both coordinates.

What it doesn’t claim

Zygmund’s conjecture for averages is not claimed, and neither are longer lengths for varying fields or other Lp spaces.

It is not among the papers OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review.

How the proof goes

  1. Bands. After localizing and rotating, the field becomes a slope, and the operator integrates along (x − t, y − t·slope). The vertical frequencies are split into bands, which a Lipschitz commutator lets the paper reassemble. The core is a restricted estimate on one band, for functions bounded by indicators of sets F and E, with a gain of (1 + |log(|F|/|E|)|)−3, so that the sum over levels converges.
  2. Packets and popular tops. Where E is much larger than F, the band is cut into narrow bins and the operator into wave packets with direction labels. Size and density estimates remove the low-density packets; the rest are assigned to tops whose direction is popular on part of the test set, and Lacey and Li’s Lipschitz Kakeya theorem counts the tops at a polynomial cost in the logarithm. On every line, moment, square-function and variation bounds then, outside one small exceptional set, limit the number of separated significant directions to a power of the logarithm.
  3. A Fourier inequality with constant one. A sum of a few separated frequencies obeys a bound with a slightly improved exponent and a constant of exactly one, since any larger constant would accumulate at every angular level. The paper iterates this through nested angular intervals, predicting finer amplitudes from one good point, controlling prediction errors with a telescoping mass and leftover terms with variation bounds, and averaging over shifted angular grids. The strict gain beats every fixed polynomial loss.
