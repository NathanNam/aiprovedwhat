Only three shapes for every edge?

An AI preprint claims the interior Mumford–Shah conjecture in the plane, two days after a similar arXiv claim

Covers result 366 in OpenAI’s catalog: its one paper. See its connections to other results and the work it cites.

The claim, in plain words

How should a computer draw the edges in a picture? In 1989, Mumford and Shah proposed an energy: approximate the picture by a function that is smooth except across a set of edges, and charge for wiggles inside the regions, for the total length of the edges, and for distance from the picture. In a one-dimensional toy, our computer finds the trade-off: smoothing a step of height h costs about 0.23 h², a cut costs one, so a cut wins once the step is taller than about 2.08.

Their conjecture says the best edges are simple: finitely many curves that run through, stop at a crack tip, or meet three at a time at 120°. Our computer shows why the angle: crossing the diagonals of a unit square costs 2.83, while two junctions at 120° cost 2.73. Near a crack tip the gradient blows up; for the model tip, the area where it exceeds t is exactly 1/(4π t⁴), a borderline rate that De Lellis and Focardi tied to the conjecture. Their 2025 monograph calls it a long-standing open problem, to date still open.

Result 366 is one OpenAI paper, 20 pages, dated September 24 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims the interior part of the planar conjecture for reduced absolute minimizers with bounded data: near every edge point inside the domain, the edges form one regular arc, a crack tip, or three arcs at 120°, and only finitely many pieces meet any compact interior region. A weak L⁴ bound on the gradient then follows, via known work.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: anything at the boundary of the domain, or finitely many pieces overall. And it was not the first public claim. On September 22, Francesco Deangelis posted on the arXiv a claimed proof of the conjecture, including the same interior structure; his note says he obtained it using OpenAI’s ChatGPT Astra. The OpenAI paper cites it; both slide the floating piece, but they finish differently.

How the proof goes

  1. Zooming in, and the floating piece. Zoom in at an edge point. Decades of work, collected in the De Lellis and Focardi monograph, show the zoomed picture is a line, a half-line or a 120-degree junction, unless a bounded piece of edge floats in a connected background. The paper rules out that last case. It splits the gradient into a field carrying the piece’s jumps and a harmonic part that extends across it, then slides the piece by a small vector t. Lengths and the piece’s own energy do not change; a cross term does, and it is harmonic in t with its minimum at zero, so it must be constant. Our computer shows the reason: a harmonic function’s average on a circle equals its centre value, so if the centre is a minimum, the function is flat on the circle.
  2. Deleting the piece. Varying the jumps along an arc of the piece then forces the whole gradient to vanish. The function is constant everywhere off the edges, so deleting it costs nothing and saves its length, contradicting minimality. Results from earlier work then turn each zoomed picture back into one of the three shapes at small scale.
