Hyperbolic, but never CAT(0)?

An AI preprint claims a hyperbolic group with no geometric CAT(0) action, answering a question of Gromov

The claim, in plain words

Draw a group as a graph and step back. If every triangle in it is thin, each side staying close to the other two however large the triangle, the group is called hyperbolic. In the flat plane an equilateral triangle of side 16 has a point about 7 units from the other sides; in the hyperbolic plane that distance levels off below 1 (our computation).

A group is CAT(0) if it acts properly and cocompactly by symmetries on a space whose triangles are at least as thin as flat ones at every scale, not just from far away. Free groups, surface groups of genus two or more, and hyperbolic groups built from cubes have both properties. Gromov, who invented hyperbolic groups, called it “the basic foundational question” whether every hyperbolic group acts this way on a negatively curved space, and said in a 2009 interview that he had tried for about ten years. Brady and Crisp found obstructions in 2007, but only to two-dimensional spaces and only for certain groups. A 2026 preprint by Duchesne and Simon wrote that Gromov’s negatively curved version was expected to fail, but no counterexample was known.

Result 257 is one OpenAI preprint, 43 pages, dated September 25, 2026. It claims a hyperbolic group, the fundamental group of a finite two-dimensional aspherical complex, that has no geometric action on any proper complete CAT(0) space, in any dimension. So it cannot act that way on a negatively curved space either, which answers Gromov’s question.

What it doesn’t claim

An explicit group: the paper proves that a late enough member of a sequence of random presentations works, and gives no size. Nor anything about Gromov’s random groups, which are CAT(0) at low density, or about actions that are not cocompact or spaces that are not proper.

How the proof goes

  1. A template on the Fano plane. The relations follow a template built on the smallest projective plane, which has 7 points and 7 lines. Four points are doubled; at one of them an incidence is dropped and its twin edge left loose. That gives a graph with 18 vertices and 32 edges. Random tables of labels, among them 90 square tables, supply the relations, and a counting argument shows that, with high probability, the group is hyperbolic and its complex aspherical.
  2. Cubes appear by force. Suppose infinitely many groups in the sequence act on CAT(0) spaces. Normalize each so the average squared generator displacement is 1, average over all random matches, and pass to a limit. In the limit, certain hexagons are forced to have exactly the shape of the six-corner zigzag around a unit cube, all around one common center at distance √3/2.
  3. Zero or more, and minus one. Near that center, the Fano lines force one scalar product to be at least 0. The loose edge forces angles of 54.7°, 70.5° and 54.7°, adding to exactly 180°, and a flat unit square then forces the same product to be −1.

The relations follow a template built on the Fano plane, the smallest projective plane: 7 points, 7 lines. 4 points are doubled; at one of them an incidence is dropped and its twin edge left loose, giving a graph with 18 vertices and 32 edges. Random tables of labels, among them 90 square tables, supply the relations, and a counting argument shows that, with high probability, the group is hyperbolic and its complex aspherical. The loose edge forces angles of 54.7, 70.5 and 54.7 degrees, adding to exactly 180, and a flat unit square then forces the same product to be −1. That contradiction ends the proof. Our check: ordinary flat space would fail even sooner, since at most 4 of the 7 line directions fit, so the work is in handling branching spaces.
