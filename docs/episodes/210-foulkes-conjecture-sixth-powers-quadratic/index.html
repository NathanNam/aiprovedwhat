Does one stack fit in the other?

Two preprints claim Foulkes’ conjecture for sixth powers, and a quadratic bound beyond which it holds for every a

The claim, in plain words

There are two ways to stack symmetric powers: polynomials of degree a in polynomials of degree b, Syma(SymbV), or the other way around, Symb(SymaV). Both carry an action of the general linear group and break into irreducible pieces. In three variables, with a = 2 and b = 3, the first has dimension 55 and the second 56: the same two pieces, plus one extra, the square of the determinant.

In 1950 Foulkes conjectured that when a is at most b, the first always fits inside the second: every irreducible piece appears at least as often on the second side. As the papers tell it, Thrall’s formulas settle a = 2; a = 3, 4 and 5 were proved one case at a time, the last two with computer help, the last published in 2017. A natural map between the two spaces, studied by Howe in 1987, does not always have full rank, so it cannot simply do the job.

Result 210 bundles two OpenAI preprints, both dated September 25, 2026. Quadratic stabilization of the canonical Foulkes–Howe map (7 pages) proves that the natural multiplication map is onto whenever b ≥ a(a − 1), in every dimension, which gives Foulkes’ inequality in that range for every a. Foulkes’ conjecture for the sixth symmetric power (36 pages) finishes the case a = 6: the bound covers b ≥ 30, and computer checks cover 6 ≤ b ≤ 29.

What it doesn’t claim

Foulkes’ conjecture for a ≥ 7 below the bound; that the bound is sharp; or that the natural map itself is the embedding for a = 6. Our own plethysm code agrees with the inequality in 23 small cases, including a = 6 and b = 7, and reproduces three rows of the second paper’s first table.

How the proof goes

  1. Forms that vanish on products. The short paper turns surjectivity into a statement about forms: a symmetric form that vanishes on every product of b vectors must be zero, once b ≥ a(a − 1). It moves degree from one variable to another with derivatives, and a single positivity identity (Lemma 2) makes each step reversible. Over the complex numbers a surjection splits, giving the embedding in the other direction.
  2. The sixth power by computer. The sixth-power paper reduces the degrees 6 ≤ b ≤ 29 to a finite list of comparisons between plethysm coefficients: 145,435 base comparisons up to b = 25, then a band of coefficients, all checked by two C++ programs.
  3. A second route. Its own cubic bound covers b ≥ 150, with the programs run that far: about 57 million band coefficients in all. We compiled the first program and ran it once (about 10 minutes); its output matched the authors’ reference byte for byte.
