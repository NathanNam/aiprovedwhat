Sphere or tree?

Seven preprints claim the phase diagram of decorated random planar maps: Liouville quantum gravity spheres up to q = 4, a random tree above, and random walks that become Liouville Brownian motion

The claim, in plain words

A planar map is a graph drawn on a sphere, its edges cutting it into faces. Pick one at random with n edges, and as n grows it looks like a random fractal surface. Decorate it with the critical Fortuin–Kasteleyn model: choose a set of edges, and give each choice the weight q to the power of half the number of loops separating clusters from dual clusters. For q = 1 the map is uniform, q = 2 is the Ising case, and spanning-tree maps sit at the q-to-zero end of the family.

Physicists and mathematicians predicted a phase diagram. Up to q = 4 the decorated map should look like a Liouville quantum gravity sphere, with a roughness parameter γ set by q (γ = √3 for Ising, 2 at q = 4); above 4 it should collapse into a random tree. A word in hamburgers and cheeseburgers, due to Sheffield, encodes these maps; our decoder reproduces Tutte’s counts of rooted maps and the weight q to the power of half the loops.

Result 211 bundles seven OpenAI preprints, 762 pages, dated September 24 and October 5, 2026. For q below 4, and for spanning trees, the maps converge to the Liouville quantum gravity sphere as metric measure spaces; a second paper adds the conformal embedding and the loops, which converge to conformal loop ensembles; at q = 4 a third first builds the critical sphere, then proves convergence; above 4 a fourth shows convergence to the Brownian continuum random tree. Three more turn to motion: on Ising maps, every eigenvalue of the random walk, times n, converges to the matching eigenvalue of the Liouville Laplacian, and the walk itself, started at equilibrium and sped up by exactly n, converges to Liouville Brownian motion, in a theorem stated given the metric limit; on spanning-tree maps the walk converges too, with the clock exactly n.

What it doesn’t claim

Rates; an explicit distance scale for the surfaces; uniformity in q; the uniform vertex measure; walks from arbitrary starting points. The spectral result covers only the Ising case.

How the proof goes

  1. Surfaces. The burger word’s two stacks converge to correlated Brownian motions, and the mating-of-trees theorem turns those into a quantum-gravity sphere. Then come homeomorphisms with small cells, local laws for shortest paths given the continuum surface, and a rigidity argument: if the best upper and lower constants differed, a local change would create a shortcut.
  2. Trees. Above four, the map breaks into two-connected blocks forming a critical branching tree, and distances grow like the square root of n.
  3. Motion. The key step is electrical: the limiting conductivity is exactly one, by a duality between the map and its dual. That is why, in the papers’ conventions, the clock is exactly n.

As an illustration, our computer sampled spanning-tree maps with up to 2,000 edges and computed n times the walk’s eigenvalues: they grow along a line of slope near 3.2, close to π, the value a known Weyl law suggests, and half the flat value, which our flat-torus control reproduces. This is a picture, not evidence: the spectral theorem itself is for Ising maps, which we could only sample at a few hundred edges.

The numbers in the video

  • Map decoding (ours): Tutte’s counts 2, 9, 54, 378, 2916, 24057 and the FK weight reproduced exactly for small n; the word h c H h F c C F gives 3 vertices, 4 edges and 3 loops.
  • Walk spectra on spanning-tree maps (ours): slope of n·λj about 3.22 at n = 1000 and 3.18 at n = 2000; flat torus control 6.30. The Lean line count is ours.
