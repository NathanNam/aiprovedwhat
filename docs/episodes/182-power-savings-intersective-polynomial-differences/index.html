No square gaps?

Three preprints claim that sets of numbers with no two elements a square apart are smaller by a power of N, with extensions to polynomial differences and to values at primes

The claim, in plain words

Pick as many numbers from 1 to N as you can, so that no two differ by a perfect square (1, 4, 9, 16, …). For N = 10 the set {2, 4, 7, 9} works, and by our exact search no five numbers do. Repeating patterns fail: the numbers leaving remainder 1 or 3 on division by 5 have no square differences up to 25, but 1 and 26 differ by 5². Any pattern with period q meets the square q², since q² is a multiple of q. As the papers recount, the problem goes back to a question of Lovász, and Furstenberg and Sárközy proved independently in the late 1970s that the best fraction tends to zero. By our exact computations the best is 4 of 10, 10 of 30 and 24 of 100.

Upper bounds since then, by Sárközy, by Pintz, Steiger and Szemerédi, by Bloom and Maynard, and by Green and Sawhney (N·e−c√log N), all saved factors that grow more slowly than any power of N, and Green and Sawhney asked whether a fixed power saving holds. In the other direction, Ruzsa built such sets of size about N0.733, using residues modulo 65 with no square differences to restrict selected digits, and a later construction cited in the first paper passes the exponent 3/4. Our check of a digit construction of that kind (seven residues modulo 65 on every other base-65 digit; our reconstruction) gives 455 numbers below 65², against 308 for the greedy choice.

Result 182 is three OpenAI preprints. A power saving for square-difference-free sets (48 pages, dated September 24, 2026), Theorem 1.1: “There are absolute constants c > 0 and C < ∞ such that, for every integer N ≥ 1 and every square-difference-free set A ⊆ [N], |A| ≤ CN1−c.” A power saving for intersective polynomial differences with an exponent depending only on the degree (47 pages, October 5, 2026) extends this to every intersective polynomial h of degree at least two, one with a root modulo every positive integer (such as n² − 1): |A| ≤ ChN1−ck for N ≥ Nh, with ck depending only on the degree. The condition is needed: n² + 1 is never a multiple of 3, so the multiples of 3 avoid all its values. A Power Saving for Polynomial Differences at Prime Arguments (42 pages, October 5, 2026) forbids only the values h(p) at primes, such as (p − 1)², for polynomials of degree at least two with a root coprime to every modulus; it uses result 003’s claimed zero-free half-plane for Dirichlet L-functions (no zeros with real part above 7/8) as an input.

What it doesn’t claim

A value of the exponent. The first paper calls it “positive and absolute, but extremely small”. Its finite model has d = (24!)161 labels, a number with 3,831 digits, and c = a0/d; by our arithmetic on its formulas, c is below 10−7600, and far smaller once the prime threshold is included. Nothing about the true exponent, which lies somewhere between about 3/4 and 1 − c. The prime version is only as sound as result 003, a separate, unrefereed claim. The papers build no extremal sets and give no algorithm.

How the proof goes

  1. A signed lift. Keep the Fourier coefficients of the set at fractions whose denominators are squarefree products of chosen primes, up to a small power of N, and rebuild a function on a product of finite fields. Its average is exactly the density, but it can be negative.
  2. A huge reflection-positive model. For each large prime, a probability law on (24!)161 labels in which a designated cycle of 24 labels has nonzero square differences, apart from a part of mass at most p−4. Mirror cuts give positive semidefinite matrices (the chessboard method from mathematical physics), and repeated Cauchy–Schwarz gives (|A|/N)d ≤ Y(N, A).
  3. Zoom out. Restricting to a residue class and splitting it into progressions with square step D² keeps the rule, since D²x² = (Dx)². So Y at length N is controlled by Y at length about N/D².
  4. Cancel. For a fixed positive proportion of residue choices, cut the interval into pieces; unless all 24 cycle labels land in one piece, some cycle edge goes from an earlier piece to a later one, and there a signed kernel on actual squares, which matches the model up to a small error and which the set avoids, makes the contribution small.
  5. Contract. The two estimates give a recurrence that contracts, so Y decays like a power of N, and so does the density.

The polynomial paper runs the same argument along Lucier’s family of auxiliary polynomials, since passing to a progression changes the polynomial; the prime paper adds a count of primes in progressions with a power-saving error, where result 003 enters. The papers credit the chessboard method to Fröhlich, Lieb and their coauthors, and cite Bloom and Maynard and Green and Sawhney as precedents for the arithmetic side.

1:51The claim

Result 182 consists of three OpenAI preprints. The earliest, 48 pages long and dated September 24, 2026, claims exactly that: there are absolute constants c and C such that every square-difference-free set of numbers up to N has at most C times N to the one minus c elements. That would answer Green and Sawhney’s question. The second, dated October 5, 47 pages long, extends this to every intersective polynomial of degree at least two: one with a root modulo every positive whole number, like n squared minus one. Its exponent depends only on the degree. The condition is needed: n squared plus one is never a multiple of three, so the multiples of three avoid all its values. The third, also dated October 5, 42 pages long, forbids only the values at primes, such as p minus one, squared, for polynomials of degree at least two with a root coprime to every modulus. It uses, as an input, another claim from this release: result 3’s zero-free half-plane for Dirichlet L-functions. Not claimed: a value of the exponent. The first paper calls it positive and absolute, but extremely small. Its finite model has 24 factorial to the power 161 labels, a number with 3,831 digits, and by our arithmetic on the paper’s formulas, c is below 10 to the minus 7,600, and in fact far smaller. Nothing about the true exponent, which lies somewhere between about three quarters and one minus c. And the prime version is only as sound as result 3, a separate, unrefereed claim.

3:36How the proof goes

The first paper’s proof, in outline. Keep only the Fourier coefficients of the set at fractions whose denominators are small products of distinct primes from a chosen range, and rebuild a function on a product of finite fields, one coordinate per prime. Its average is exactly the density of the set, but it can take negative values, which rules out the usual positivity arguments. Then comes a huge finite model. For each large prime, there is a probability law on 24 factorial to the 161st power labels, in which a designated cycle of 24 labels has consecutive differences that are nonzero squares modulo the prime, apart from a part of mass at most one over p to the fourth. The law is reflection positive, an idea from mathematical physics: mirror cuts give positive semidefinite matrices. Repeated Cauchy-Schwarz across the mirrors then shows that the density, raised to the number of labels, is at most a single quantity Y. The goal is to show that Y shrinks like a power of N. Two estimates do it. First, zooming out: restricting to a residue class and splitting it into progressions whose step is a square, D squared, keeps the rule, because D squared times x squared is the square of D x. So Y at length N is controlled by Y at length about N over D squared. Second, cancellation: for a fixed positive proportion of residue choices, cut the interval into many pieces. Unless all 24 cycle labels land in the same piece, some edge of the cycle goes from an earlier piece to a later one, and on that edge the model matches, up to a small error, a signed kernel supported on actual square differences, which the set does not have. Together these give a recurrence that contracts, so Y decays like a power of N, and so does the density. The polynomial paper runs the same machine along Lucier’s family of auxiliary polynomials, since passing to a progression changes the polynomial. The prime paper adds a count of primes in progressions with a power-saving error, which is where result 3 enters. The papers credit the chessboard method to Fröhlich, Lieb and their coauthors, and cite Bloom and Maynard and Green and Sawhney as precedents for the arithmetic side.

5:55Where it stands

None of the three preprints is peer reviewed or has been revised. Only the first is formalized: OpenAI’s Lean files state its theorem exactly and prove it in 81 files and about 24,000 lines, roughly 2,000 of them near-copies, with no gaps we could find by search; we did not run the check. Like the paper, the proof shows that the exponent exists without computing it. The polynomial and prime papers have no Lean. Result 3’s zero-free half-plane is formalized in Lean according to OpenAI’s files; we did not run that check either. None of these papers is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, a set with no square differences is smaller than N by a fixed power of N, the first such saving, as the papers present it, after nearly 50 years of savings smaller than any power of N. The same holds for every intersective polynomial of degree two or more, and, given result 3, at primes.
