The claim, in plain words

Two players take turns claiming squares of an endless grid. Maker wants to own a copy of one fixed shape, shifted anywhere, turned or flipped; Breaker only wants to stop that. The shape is Snaky: six squares, four in a row and then a step up to two more. Turned and flipped, it has 8 different versions, and by our count 48 placed copies pass through any one square.

The paper tells the history starting from Sieben’s account, which credits these games to Frank Harary. Harary, Harborth and Seemann had Maker wins with a head start of two extra stones. Separately, Breaker has no pairing defense (one that pairs up squares and always answers in the partner square); Csernenszky, Martin and Pluhár later proved this without a computer. But ruling out one kind of defense does not give Maker a win. Sieben proved a win in 41 dimensions; Halupczok and Schlage-Puchta proved a win in 3 dimensions, a loss on an 8×8 board, and a planar win with a head start of one, as did Ito and Miyagawa; Sieben (2008) gave a finite proof for that head start and described the plain planar problem as undecided at the time. (Sieben’s 2004 paper, which we read, called Snaky “the only undecided 2-dimensional poliomino”.)

Result 187 is one OpenAI preprint, Snaky in 21 Maker moves (84 pages, dated September 25, 2026; most pages are printed certificates and code). Its Theorem 1: “In this game, there is one globally legal Maker policy that completes an allowed copy of S within at most 21 actual Maker claims against every legal continuation of Breaker.” That is a win by the 41st turn, on the empty infinite board with no head start. Corollary 6 gives the same bound on a 17×17 board, with Maker’s moves inside one fixed region of 251 squares; the appendices give separate 25- and 35-move strategies.

What it doesn’t claim

That 21 is best possible: “We do not assert optimal move counts or board sizes”, and no lower bound is stated. In our exact search, an all-knowing Breaker can make this particular strategy use all 21 moves, which says nothing about Maker’s best play. Together with the 8×8 loss, by our reading the smallest winning square board lies between 9×9 and 17×17. The strong game, where both players race for Snaky, is excluded, and the paper does not say how its certificate was found; it says only that checking it needs no search.

How the proof goes

  1. Cards. A card (the paper’s “valid claim”) names a few squares A, a region T and a number h: if it is Maker’s turn, Maker owns A and Breaker has nothing in T, Maker wins within h more moves, whatever else is on the board. The six starting cards: five squares of a copy owned, the sixth not Breaker’s, win in one.
  2. One rule. From some cards and a square p, Maker claims p; the rule arranges that afterwards every square in all the children’s regions is Maker’s, so Breaker’s single reply misses at least one child’s region, and that child takes over at the cost of one more move. A reply far away misses every region, so the infinite board needs no separate case.
  3. Forks. Card 6: Maker owns a column of five and plays beside its top square; two copies each need one more square, and Breaker can block only one. Card 7: from four in a column, Maker makes five, and a card-6 fork remains at whichever end Breaker does not block.
  4. The certificate. 722 lines build new cards from turned and shifted copies of earlier ones. The last, card 727, needs nothing, has a 251-square region inside a 17×17 box, and 21 moves; its 32 sub-cards all require only the centre, and the squares common to their regions shrink from 35 to none besides the centre.
  5. The strategy. Claim the centre; after each Breaker reply, follow the first sub-card whose region the reply missed. Each move lowers the remaining move bound, so Maker wins by its 21st move. The paper credits Sieben’s proof trees as a close antecedent.

The numbers in the video

  • Snaky has 8 distinct orientations and no symmetry; 48 allowed copies pass through any square; it is one of 35 hexominoes (216 counting all orientations separately). Our counts.
  • The paper’s checker, run by us: PASS, 728 cards, final card with no required squares, 251 squares in its region and height 21, in about 0.05 seconds.
  • Our own evaluator of the certificate: the same final card, Maker’s first move at the centre (8, 8), and the common region of the 32 sub-cards shrinking 35, 31, 21, 12, 4, 0 by group.
  • The worst case against this strategy, by our exact search: 21 Maker moves; the game in the video is one such line, with the first completed copy at Maker’s 21st move.
  • The Lean count: our count of the import closure.

0:00The question

Two players take turns claiming the squares of an endless grid. The first player, Maker, wants to own a copy of one fixed shape, shifted anywhere, turned, or flipped. The second player, Breaker, has only one job: to stop that. Breaker never needs a shape of its own. The shape here is Snaky: 6 squares, four in a row, then a step up to two more. Turn it and flip it, and you get 8 different versions; by our count, 48 placed copies pass through any single square. Snaky is one of the 35 hexominoes, the shapes made of 6 squares. The question is simple. On an empty endless board, with no head start, can Maker always force a copy of Snaky, and if so, how fast? The paper tells the history, starting from Nándor Sieben’s account, which credits these games to Frank Harary. Harary, Harborth and Seemann had winning strategies when Maker gets a head start of two extra stones. Separately, Breaker was shown to have no pairing defense, one that pairs up squares and always answers in the partner square; Csernenszky, Martin and Pluhár later gave a proof of that without a computer. But ruling out one kind of defense does not give Maker a win. Sieben proved that Maker wins in 41 dimensions. Halupczok and Schlage-Puchta proved that Snaky wins in 3 dimensions, loses on an 8 by 8 board, and wins in the plane with a head start of one extra stone; Ito and Miyagawa also gave a head-start-one construction. And in 2008, Sieben gave a finite proof for that head start, and described the plain planar problem as undecided at the time. Sieben’s earlier, 2004 paper, which we read directly, called Snaky the only planar polyomino whose game was still undecided.

1:47The claim

Result 187 is one OpenAI preprint, 84 pages, dated September 25, 2026; most of those pages are printed proof data and program listings. It claims: on the empty infinite square board, with no head start, there is one Maker strategy that completes a copy of Snaky within at most 21 of Maker’s own moves, against every legal play by Breaker. That means a win no later than the 41st turn of the game. A corollary gives the same bound on a 17 by 17 board, and in fact Maker can keep all its moves inside one fixed region of 251 squares. Appendices give two further, separate strategies, with 25 and 35 moves. Not claimed: that 21 is the best possible. The paper says it does not assert optimal move counts or board sizes, and it states no lower bound. Our own check makes the point: against this particular strategy, an all-knowing Breaker can drag the game out to all 21 moves, which says nothing about Maker’s best play. Combining the corollary with the 8 by 8 loss, by our reading, the smallest winning square board lies between 9 by 9 and 17 by 17. The strong game, where both players race for Snaky, is excluded. And the paper does not say how its proof was found; it only says checking it needs no search.

3:13How the proof goes

The infinite board is the obstacle: Breaker may play anywhere, so no finite game tree covers every reply. The paper works with conditional promises, which it calls valid claims, or cards. A card names a few squares Maker already owns, a region, and a number of moves. It says: if it is Maker’s turn, Maker owns those squares, and Breaker has nothing inside the region, then Maker wins within that many more moves, whatever else is on the board. The starting cards are simple. If Maker owns five of the 6 squares of a copy, and the sixth is not Breaker’s, Maker wins in one move. There are six of these, one for each missing square. One rule builds bigger cards from smaller ones. Take a few cards and a square. Maker claims the square. The rule arranges that, after this move, every square lying in all the children’s regions belongs to Maker. Then Breaker’s single reply must miss at least one child’s region, and that child’s promise takes over, at the cost of one more move. A reply far away misses every region, so distant play needs no separate case. Here is the paper’s card six. Maker owns a column of five. Maker plays beside its top square; now two copies each need just one more square, above and below, and Breaker can block only one. Card seven starts from four in a column: Maker extends it to five, and whichever end Breaker blocks, card six’s fork remains at the other end. The proof is a certificate: 722 lines, each building a new card from turned and shifted copies of earlier ones. It ends with card 727: nothing required, a region of 251 squares inside a 17 by 17 box, and 21 moves. Its 32 sub-cards each require only the centre, and the squares common to their regions shrink, group by group, from 35 to none besides the centre itself. So the strategy is: claim the centre, and after each Breaker reply, follow the first sub-card whose region the reply missed. Every step uses one move and lowers the remaining move bound, so Maker wins by its 21st move. Checking the certificate is pure set arithmetic. The paper’s own checker passed in a twentieth of a second when we ran it, and our independent re-implementation rebuilds the same final card. Here is a game in which a Breaker that knows the strategy holds out for all 21 moves. The paper credits Sieben’s proof trees as a close antecedent of its method.

5:47Where it stands

The preprint is unrefereed and has not been revised. OpenAI’s Lean files state the 21-move theorem on the infinite board and prove it, in 94 files and about 71,000 lines, nearly all of it card data; every card is checked by Lean’s kernel, with no compiled-code shortcuts and no gaps we could find by search. We did not run the check. A separate Lean proof covers the 35-move strategy. The 17 by 17 corollary is not formalized. The paper’s own appendix describes a narrower formal supplement than the one in the repository. The paper is not among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, Snaky is a win for Maker on the empty plane, within 21 of Maker’s moves, and on a 17 by 17 board; how few moves best play needs is not claimed.
