Do eigenvalues repel?

Two preprints claim random-matrix eigenvalue statistics for random regular graphs of every fixed degree, and under weak disorder

The claim, in plain words

Take a random graph in which every vertex has exactly three neighbours. Its adjacency eigenvalues follow the Kesten–McKay density, worked out from walks on an infinite tree. But how are neighbouring eigenvalues spaced? The Petersen graph’s ten eigenvalues sit on just three values, 3, 1 and −2: symmetry piles them up.

For a random graph, Jakobson, Miller, Rivin and Rudnick predicted in 1999 that the spacings follow random-matrix statistics, the Gaussian orthogonal ensemble: eigenvalues repel. Our computer checks random three- and four-regular graphs with up to 4,000 vertices: the spacing ratio is 0.53, as for GOE matrices, against 0.39 for independent points. Proofs existed only when the degree grows with the size.

Result 219 bundles two OpenAI preprints, 44 and 60 pages, dated September 23 and October 5, 2026. The first claims that for every fixed degree of at least three, including cubic graphs, and every energy strictly inside the Kesten–McKay band, the local eigenvalue point process of a uniformly random regular graph converges to the GOE one; it says this proves a conjecture stated by Bourgade and Huang in July 2026. The second adds weak random potentials on the vertices and claims the same GOE statistics well inside the band, averaged over the graph and the potential.

What it doesn’t claim

The spectral edges, handled for graphs without disorder by Huang, McKenzie and Yau; growing degree; strong disorder; any particular graph. In our simulation, at disorder strength 12 the spacings look like independent points.

How the proof goes

  1. Gaussian eigenvectors. Sample a random edge and read an eigenvector’s values at its two ends. An entropy count over graphs, plus a strict inequality that needs degree at least three and energy inside the band, shows these values are Gaussian, to polynomial accuracy. The spectral input, rigidity and delocalization at every fixed degree, comes from Huang and Yau.
  2. A symmetry that injects noise. Swapping the ends of a few sampled edges is an exact symmetry of random regular graphs. In the limit, with Gaussian values at the edge ends, it extends to all rotations, and many small rotations act like adding a little GOE noise. So the limiting process equals its own points plus GOE noise, and a known Dyson Brownian motion result identifies it as GOE.
  3. With disorder, small law-preserving shifts of single vertex potentials play the role of the swaps.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: Petersen spectrum 3 (×1), 1 (×5), −2 (×4); spacing ratio 0.530 for random 3-regular graphs (n = 2,000), 0.530 for GOE matrices, 0.386 for independent points; eigenvector fourth moment 2.996 (Gaussian 3).
  • Ours, with disorder (d = 3, n = 2,000): GOE-like up to strength 4, close to independent points from strength 12.

0:00The question

Take a random graph in which every vertex has exactly three neighbours. Its adjacency matrix has eigenvalues, and their overall density follows the Kesten–McKay law, worked out from walks on an infinite tree. But what about the fine structure: how are neighbouring eigenvalues spaced? Here is a small cubic graph, the Petersen graph. Its ten eigenvalues sit on just three values: three, one, and minus two, with large multiplicities. Symmetry makes them pile up. A random graph is different. Our computer checks this on random three- and four-regular graphs with up to 4,000 vertices: the spacing ratio comes out at 0.53, matching GOE matrices and far from the 0.39 of independent points. It is striking at fixed degree, where the graph still looks locally like a tree. Proofs existed only when the degree grows with the size.

The proof works through eigenvectors. Sample a random edge and read an eigenvector’s values at its two ends. An entropy count over graphs, plus a strict inequality that needs degree at least three and energy inside the band, shows these values are Gaussian, to polynomial accuracy. Our check: eigenvector entries have fourth moment 2.996, against three for a Gaussian. The spectral input, rigidity and delocalization at every fixed degree, comes from Huang and Yau. Then the key idea. Swapping the ends of a few sampled edges is an exact symmetry of random regular graphs. In the limit, with Gaussian values at the edge ends, that symmetry extends to all rotations, and many small rotations act like adding a little GOE noise. So the limiting process equals its own points plus GOE noise, and a known Dyson Brownian motion result identifies it as GOE. With disorder, small law-preserving shifts of single vertex potentials play the role of the swaps.

3:14Where it stands

Both preprints are unrefereed and have not been revised. Neither is formalized in Lean. They share about half of their closing sections nearly word for word, the shared endgame. Neither paper is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we found no expert reaction or earlier proof. If they hold, a random cubic graph, which looks locally like a tree, has eigenvalue spacings indistinguishable from those of a random matrix, and weak disorder does not change that.
