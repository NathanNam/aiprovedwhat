Infinite enough?

The claim, in plain words

In Hilbert’s hotel two copies fit inside one. In a C*-algebra, a positive element is properly infinite when two copies of it fit inside it, in the sense of Cuntz comparison; in matrices comparison is just rank comparison, so nothing nonzero there is properly infinite. For simple algebras there is one clean notion of pure infiniteness. For algebras with ideals, Kirchberg and Rørdam found three: weak, where for one fixed n, n copies of every positive element are properly infinite; ordinary, where every positive element is properly infinite; and strong, where any two positive elements can be pulled apart, each keeping its shape, approximately. Strong implies ordinary, and ordinary implies weak. In 2002 they asked whether the arrows reverse for all C*-algebras; Schafhauser, Tikuisis and White list it as Problem LXXII. Many special cases were known, including simple algebras, real rank zero and topological dimension zero.

Result 303 is one OpenAI paper, 52 pages, dated September 25, 2026, and made public October 6. It claims that ordinary pure infiniteness implies strong for every complex C*-algebra, and that for exact algebras weak implies ordinary, so all three notions agree. Combined with Kirchberg’s absorption theorem, every separable nuclear weakly purely infinite algebra absorbs the Cuntz algebra 𝒪∞, with no unit or simplicity assumed.

What it doesn’t claim

Whether weak implies ordinary for algebras that are not exact. The paper leaves that case outside its conclusions, and other recent surveys still call it open.

How the proof goes

  1. Ordinary to strong. A transport lemma moves whole corners of the algebra along a single partial isometry. If two corners could not be pulled apart, finitely many freely chosen entries would be forced to equal values fixed in advance. Four entries, chosen using proper infiniteness, break that prescription, and repeating the step makes the cross term as small as you like.
  2. Weak to ordinary: halving. An element x with x² = 0 gives two orthogonal, equivalent halves inside a piece, and halving enough times produces n copies inside one piece. Exactness gives one error bound that works in every quotient at once, and that is what lets the paper find such an x across a whole slice of the algebra.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: in the strong test with the identity matrix taken twice, the best possible error in matrices is exactly 1/2; in the hotel it is 0, and a cross term pushed down the corridor shrinks from about 0.24 to 5 × 10⁻¹⁰.
  • Ours: halving in 16 × 16 matrices gives ranks 8, 4, 2, 1 and stops; in the hotel it never ends.
