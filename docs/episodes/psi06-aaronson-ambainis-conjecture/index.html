The claim, in plain words

This is PSI06. Some quantum algorithms need exponentially fewer looks at their input than any ordinary algorithm. Shor’s factoring algorithm is one, and it works because its input hides a repeating pattern: structure. The looks are called queries. Beals, Buhrman, Cleve, Mosca and de Wolf showed that after T queries, a quantum algorithm’s chance of saying yes is a polynomial in the input bits, of degree at most 2T, with values between zero and one. Here is PSI’s own example: a polynomial of degree two on 4 bits. Each corner of these two cubes is one of the 16 inputs, shaded by its value. The influence of a bit is the change in value when that bit is flipped, squared and averaged over all inputs. By our count, bit 4’s influence is a quarter; the other three have 0.02, 0.02 and 0.01.

Is there always an influential bit? The question goes back to a 2008 lecture by Scott Aaronson. With Andris Ambainis, in a paper first posted in 2009, he stated it as a conjecture. If a polynomial of degree at most d stays between zero and one on every input, and its variance is at least ε, then some bit has influence at least (ε/d) to a fixed power, however many bits there are. They showed this would let an ordinary algorithm imitate any quantum one on most inputs, with only polynomially more queries. So exponential speedups would need structure. Earlier bounds for general polynomials shrank exponentially with the degree. In 2019, Nathan Keller and Ohad Klein posted a claimed proof, then withdrew it, writing: our proof contains a serious flaw. In August 2026, a Stanford group proved its prediction in a special case: quantum algorithms that query in a fixed number of parallel rounds.

On November 6, 2026, the company PSI, Physical Superintelligence, announced: the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture is now a theorem. With the post came a public repository, holding a 60-page paper by David Turturean and a Lean proof. In Aaronson and Ambainis’s own terms, the main result says some bit has influence at least a constant c, times ε^(5/2)/d⁶. And that is at least (ε/d)⁷. The constant is 8√15/(125π⁴): about 0.0025. Through the known reduction, the paper says, a quantum algorithm making T queries can be imitated on most inputs by an ordinary one making at most (4T)⁸ queries, times factors for the allowed error and for the fraction of inputs allowed to fail. Who did the work? The paper says its mathematical statements were produced by AI models: instances of a non-public Anthropic model, in a research harness that Turturean built at PSI. He prompted the first draft, reviewed and edited it, and is responsible for its correctness. We cannot check which model was used. AI Proved What? is itself made with Claude, Anthropic’s AI.

How the proof goes, and what Lean checked

  1. The outline. The proof, which the paper calls elementary, follows a plan of Shachar Lovett and Jiapeng Zhang. Slice the function at every level. At every input, give each bit a weight, so that flipping any group of bits changes the value by at most the group’s total weight. Their plan left one inequality as a conjecture. The new proof puts an all-pairs inequality in its place, proved with a one-to-one pairing of inputs that comes from a determinant. That pairing refines David Reimer’s Butterfly Lemma. By the paper’s timeline, the first complete proof came on the third day of the run, with exponent 22 in place of 7, and was checked in Lean. Later work in the run brought it to 11, then 7, each checked in Lean.
  2. The statement. Now the Lean proof. The statement it proves is called PrintedConjecture. It imports only Mathlib, Lean’s community library, and is written with ordinary polynomials and finite sums, with the bound (ε/d)⁷. The README says a reader needs to read only this definition to check what the theorem proves. There are four public theorems; one assumes an unformalized result of Agarwal and Ben-David, only to improve the constant. The repository says there is no placeholder and no added axiom, only Lean’s three standard ones. Our own count: 95 Lean files, about 9,600 lines. PSI reports 761 declarations.
  3. The checks. Then the checks, as PSI records them. Every declaration was replayed by Lean’s own kernel checker, and again by lean4lean, an independent implementation of Lean’s type checker. A tool called comparator matched the four theorems against the plain statements, with a third kernel, nanoda, written in Rust. Controls are included: a false file that an ordinary build accepts but both replays reject, and four wrong comparator solutions, all rejected. But PSI ran all these checks itself, on rented machines and in private GitHub repositories, first on October 22 and again on November 6. Only copied logs are public, and the public repository shows zero runs so far. And the checks cover the influence bound only. The quantum consequences, in Section 1.3 of the paper, have no Lean counterpart, and neither do its survey, overview and closing remarks.

The numbers in the video

  • PSI’s example f(x) = 0.1 + 0.5x4 + 0.2x1x2 + 0.1x3 (from its post). Ours: values 0.1 to 0.9; influences (squared change, averaged over the 16 inputs) 0.02, 0.02, 0.01, 0.25; variance 0.0725.
  • The constant c = 8√15/(125π⁴) = 0.002545, “about 0.0025” (post); the paper’s Theorem 1.1 uses √15/(500π⁴) ≈ 7.95e-05 on the cube with values ±1, which is c/32.
  • Exponents during the run (paper, Appendix A): 22, then 11, then 7, each checked in Lean. Query bound (paper, Section 1): (4T)⁸ε⁻¹⁴δ⁻⁸.
  • Ours: 95 Lean files, 9,625 lines in the repository at commit 2a6ceae. PSI’s count: 761 declarations (648 theorems, 113 definitions) in 84 modules.
  • Keller and Klein’s 2019 claimed proof (arXiv:1911.03748) was withdrawn on December 2, 2019: “Unfortunately, our proof contains a serious flaw.”
