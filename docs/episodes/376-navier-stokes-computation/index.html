A fluid that computes?

Nine AI preprints claim universal computation in forced Navier–Stokes flows on a fixed flat domain

The claim, in plain words

Can a fluid compute? In 1991 Cris Moore wrote that no one knows whether hydrodynamics, the Navier-Stokes equation for example, is capable of computation. Computing here means: any Turing machine and its input can be encoded into the setup so that some feature of the flow reveals whether the machine ever halts, a question that is undecidable in general. Our computer runs small machines: the two-state busy beaver halts after six steps, a four-state one after 107, while a machine that walks right forever never halts, and no finite run can certify that.

Ideal fluids came first: in 2021 Cardona, Miranda, Peralta-Salas and Presas built a steady, Turing-complete flow of an ideal fluid on a specially shaped three-sphere. For viscous flow, a July 2025 paper by Dyhr, González-Prieto, Miranda and Peralta-Salas built unforced, stationary, Turing-complete Navier-Stokes flows on specially deformed spaces, writing that the Navier-Stokes case had remained open until then. The open questions in that literature concerned flows without external forcing.

Result 376 is nine OpenAI papers, 260 pages, dated September 27 and made public October 6, 2026, with one revised the next day. The headline: on the flat three-dimensional torus, at any fixed positive computable viscosity, an algorithm turns any Turing machine and input into a smooth, divergence-free force. The flow that starts at rest under that force is unique, and a particle starting at a fixed point enters a fixed region exactly when the machine halts. So predicting that particle is undecidable.

What it doesn’t claim

The companion papers vary the setup: a force that becomes constant in time, one that is periodic from the very start, one that dies off faster than any power of time in ordinary space, or detection through the velocity itself. Not claimed: anything about unforced or natural flows, or robustness, since everything rests on exact positions. The force is tailored to each machine, and the papers say that making a chosen velocity a forced solution is elementary; the work is in the compiler and in tracking the particle at every moment.

How the proof goes

  1. A reversible machine, coded as a point. First the machine is made reversible with a seven-row recorder, in the style of Bennett. Our computer runs it on the two-state busy beaver: 84 microsteps, each one undoable, matching the paper’s step count. Then the tape is coded in base B as a point in a square, and each machine step becomes an exact affine move: a head move stretches one direction and squeezes the other. Our computer checks all six steps exactly, and shows how fragile the coding is: in our base-five version, tapes that differ only twenty cells from the head have codes about 4 × 10⁻¹⁵ apart.
  2. Shear flows that are exact solutions. Each rule gets its own height: lift, apply four shears that compose to the stretch, translate, and lower. Our computer checks exactly that four shears give the stretch, with no point straying from the rectangle’s center by more than twice its half-width. Shears carry no self-advection, nor do the lifts and slides, so the force defined as the time derivative minus viscosity times the Laplacian makes them exact Navier-Stokes solutions, and an energy argument gives uniqueness. Codes that never halt stay to the left, and the halting square sits inside the detector.
