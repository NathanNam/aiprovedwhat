The claims, in plain words

Scatter points across the plane, one per unit of area on average, and give every pair an energy that depends only on their distance. Which arrangement has the least energy per point? The papers allow any potential that is completely monotone in the squared distance: never negative, never increasing, convex, with derivatives alternating in sign forever. Every Gaussian and every inverse power qualifies. With the Gaussian e−πr² at density one, the triangular lattice has energy 0.160 per point, the square lattice 0.180, and the honeycomb 0.272.

Among lattices the answer was classical: Rankin, Cassels, Ennola and Diananda settled the inverse powers whose sums converge, and Montgomery (1988) settled every Gaussian. Cohn and Kumar conjectured in 2007 that sharp Fourier bounds would prove the triangular lattice universally optimal among all configurations, along with the E8 and Leech lattices, and Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko and Viazovska later proved those two higher-dimensional cases. In the plane only restricted competitors had been handled. Two neighboring problems point the same way: Cohn and Elkies conjectured that their Fourier bound for packings is exact in dimensions 2, 8 and 24, and Sandier and Serfaty, studying vortices in superconductors, conjectured that the triangular lattice minimizes a renormalized energy for logarithmic repulsion against a uniform background. Brauchart, Hardin and Saff conjectured the constant in the linear term of the least logarithmic energy of points on a sphere.

Result 090 is four OpenAI preprints. The first (70 pages, September 23) claims that for every completely monotone potential of squared distance, and every configuration whose density in growing centered disks tends to one, the energy per point, in the lower limit, is at least the triangular lattice’s, even when the energies are infinite. It also claims triangular minimality for renormalized Riesz energies with exponent between 0 and 2, where the raw sums diverge. The second (31 pages, September 26) proves the same energy theorem with a smaller, independent construction. The third (49 pages) claims the Sandier–Serfaty conjecture and, with a formula of Bétermin and Sandier, the Brauchart–Hardin–Saff constant, 2 log 2 + ½ log(2/3) + 3 log(√π/Γ(1/3)) ≈ −0.0556. The fourth (49 pages) builds an exact Cohn–Elkies function in the plane, with f(0) = 2/√3.

What it doesn’t claim

The claims identify minimum values, not every minimizer: no uniqueness is claimed for the energies. Cohn and Kumar’s stronger request, one sharp Fourier function for each potential, is not claimed; the Gaussian inequalities are added up instead. The packing function does not meet the extra zero-set condition of a further Cohn–Elkies conjecture. Nothing is claimed at Riesz exponent 2 or for quantum crystallization.

How the proofs go

  1. The Fourier route. For each Gaussian G, the energy papers build a radial function f with f ≤ G, a nonnegative Fourier transform, f = G at every nonzero lattice point, and a transform vanishing at every nonzero point of the dual lattice, which is the triangular lattice turned a quarter turn. For any configuration of density one these signs bound the energy below by f̂(0) − f(0), and Poisson summation shows this is exactly the lattice energy. Every completely monotone potential, shifted slightly, is a positive mixture of Gaussians and a constant (Bernstein–Widder), so the inequalities add up.
  2. Building the functions. Rescaled squared lengths of lattice vectors are the numbers j² + jk + k², which leave remainders 0, 1, 3, 4, 7 or 9 on division by 12. The 70-page paper puts a node at every such positive integer, multiplies by a product of squared sines with a double zero at each node, and builds columns prescribing a value and a slope at every node. Finite blocks of 168 coordinates are certified with interval arithmetic, the infinite tail is solved because its couplings are small, and the signs between nodes are certified with Bernstein polynomial bounds over whole ranges of the Gaussian parameter. The atomic paper uses nodes modulo 36 and blocks of 20. The packing paper built this machinery first, for one function.
  3. The Coulomb route. On square tori the minimizing points stay a definite distance apart. Their Voronoi cells split into sectors, at most six per point on average. With one trial potential glued across all sectors, each sector contributes at least a negative constant plus fixed multiples of its area and angle; areas and angles add up exactly, giving a bound calibrated to equal the triangular value. Two scalar inequalities are verified with interval arithmetic. Sandier and Serfaty’s periodic approximation carries the bound to the whole plane, and Bétermin and Sandier had shown that the sphere constant takes the conjectured value exactly when the triangular lattice wins.

The numbers in the video

  • A short Python script computes energies at density one. With the Gaussian e−πr²: triangular 0.1596, square 0.1803, honeycomb 0.2716. Sixteen random points on a torus that fits the triangular pattern descended to the triangular energy from all 40 starts; on a square torus of the same area the best found was 0.1654.
  • The Cohn–Elkies linear program with 20 Laguerre–Gaussian functions, sign conditions imposed on a fine grid, gives 0.906906, against π/(2√3) = 0.906900. A numerical search only approaches the bound; it cannot show that a function attains it.
  • Local minima of logarithmic energy on the sphere, found by descent, give (energy − (½ − log 2)n² + (n/2) log n)/n of −0.0478 at 50 points and −0.0525 at 800, drifting toward −0.0556. True minima can only be lower.

4:31How the proofs go

Both energy papers reduce everything to Gaussians. For each Gaussian they build a radial function f that stays below it, whose Fourier transform is never negative, that touches the Gaussian at every nonzero lattice point, and whose transform vanishes at every nonzero point of the dual lattice, which for the triangular lattice is the same lattice turned a quarter turn. For any configuration of density one, the two sign conditions bound the energy below by f̂(0) − f(0), and Poisson summation over the lattice shows this number is exactly the lattice energy. Finally, every completely monotone potential, shifted slightly, is a positive mixture of Gaussians and a constant, by a classical theorem of Bernstein and Widder, so the Gaussian inequalities add up to the full theorem. The hard part is building f for every Gaussian at once. Rescaled squared lengths of triangular lattice vectors are the numbers j² + jk + k², which leave only certain remainders on division by 12: 0, 1, 3, 4, 7 or 9. The 70-page paper puts a node at every such positive integer, uses a product of squared sines with a double zero at each node, and builds columns that prescribe a value and a slope at every node. Finite blocks of 168 coordinates are certified with interval arithmetic, the infinite tail is solved because its couplings are small, and the signs between nodes are certified with Bernstein polynomial bounds over whole ranges of the Gaussian parameter. The atomic paper uses nodes modulo 36 and blocks of 20. The packing paper built this machinery first, for one function. The Coulomb paper takes a direct route, with no universal-optimality input. On a square torus, minimizing points stay a definite distance apart. Their Voronoi cells, the regions nearest each point, split into sectors, one per edge, and on average a point has at most 6. With one trial potential, glued continuously across all sectors, each sector’s contribution is at least a negative constant plus fixed multiples of its area and angle. Areas and angles add up exactly, so at most 6 sectors per point gives a bound calibrated to equal the triangular value. Two scalar inequalities behind this are verified with interval arithmetic. Sandier and Serfaty’s periodic approximation carries the bound to the whole plane, and Bétermin and Sandier had shown that the sphere constant takes the conjectured value exactly when the triangular lattice wins.

7:11Where it stands

None of the four preprints is peer reviewed, and none has been revised. OpenAI’s Lean formalizations cover the energy theorem, the atomic paper’s Gaussian functions and the packing certificate; we have not run them. The Coulomb paper and the sphere constant rest on that paper and its interval arithmetic, and the Riesz step is unformalized analysis. The packing paper mentions a 2026 thesis abstract announcing a related function built from modular forms. None of the papers is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If they hold, the plane largely joins dimensions 8 and 24: no configuration of density one beats the triangular lattice, for any completely monotone potential; it minimizes the Coulomb and long-range Riesz renormalized energies, fixes the Brauchart–Hardin–Saff constant, and has an exact Cohn–Elkies function. Whether the minimizers are unique, and whether every single potential has its own sharp Fourier function, are not settled here.
