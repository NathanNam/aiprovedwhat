Result 379, November 2026: a companion episode on two of the result’s seven papers (see also result 379; sequel to 130 and 109)

N√(log N), not N log N?

Two OpenAI preprints claim matching bounds: a finite abelian Fourier transform needs N(log N)^(1/2 + o(1)) binary linear gates, and one multitape machine multiplies n-bit numbers in n(log n)^(1/2 + o(1)) time, optimal if Weak ETH holds. The lower bounds rest on a new tensor-geometry paper.

A companion episode of result 379 in OpenAI’s catalog, on two of its 7 papers: The logarithmic exponent of finite Fourier transforms and The logarithmic exponent of integer multiplication on tapes. The question

A Fourier transform turns a list of N samples into N frequencies, each one a weighted sum of all the samples. Computed one sum at a time, that takes about N² operations. In 1965, Cooley and Tukey published a fast Fourier transform: split the list in half, transform the halves, and combine them with butterflies. For 8 samples, that is 3 stages of 4 butterflies. Count each weighted sum of two values as one gate, and you get 24 gates: N·log₂ N, as our script checks against the 8-point transform. How much of that log N per sample is really needed, if a circuit may use any coefficients and reuse anything it has computed?

The same question for multiplying whole numbers. The school method for two 8-digit numbers takes 64 digit products. In 1962, Karatsuba and Ofman needed three half-size products instead of four: 27 for 8 digits, by our script. Fourier methods followed, and Harvey and van der Hoeven reached n log n, in a paper published in 2021. All of this on a multitape Turing machine: a fixed program with tape heads that move one cell per step, so moving digits around costs time too.

Two of our episodes covered OpenAI claims from September that beat n log n: episode 109 for multiplication, with log n raised to 1 − 2^−182, and episode 130 for Fourier transforms, with an exponent about 1 − 10^−13. Lower bounds were even further away. Morgenstern proved about ½ N log N, but only when no coefficient is larger than one, and the other bounds the paper lists also need restrictions. With arbitrary coefficients, the sources we checked call any superlinear lower bound for an explicit matrix a central open problem. For multiplication, the paper lists only bounds for restricted models, or under conjectures.

The surveys: S. V. Lokam, Complexity lower bounds using linear algebra (2009), and M. Kumar and B. L. Volk, Lower bounds for matrix factorization (2019), which calls a superlinear lower bound for an explicit family of matrices a central open problem. That background is ours, not the paper’s.

The claims, in plain words

Result 379, added on November 24, 2026, is a family of seven OpenAI papers. This episode covers two. The first, The logarithmic exponent of finite Fourier transforms, 41 pages, opens: The minimum number of binary linear gates for a finite abelian Fourier transform of order N is N(log N)^(1/2 + o(1)). Its Theorem 1.1: at least c·N√(log N) gates, and for every ε, at most C_ε·N(log N)^(1/2 + ε). Each gate forms a·u + b·v, with any coefficients. This holds for every finite abelian group, over every field in which N is invertible and which has the needed roots of unity, and per transform when one circuit handles many inputs, even mixing them. Polynomial multiplication gets the same exponent.

In the paper’s words (Theorem 1.1): for finite abelian groups G₁, …, G_J of common order N ≥ 2, with N invertible in K and K containing a primitive root of unity of order exp(G_j), and T the direct sum of their forward or inverse transforms, “c JN√(log N) ≤ C_K(T) ≤ C_ε JN(1 + log N)^(1/2+ε)” for an absolute c > 0. “Theorem 1.1 leaves open an upper bound with ε = 0.” Its Theorem 1.2 extends the lower bound to systems with feedback, a low-rank error and a sparse error, and to their limits.

The second, The logarithmic exponent of integer multiplication on tapes, 43 pages. One fixed multitape machine multiplies two n-bit numbers exactly in time n(log n)^(1/2 + o(1)), visiting only a linear number of tape cells. The lower bound, at least c·n√(log n), is conditional. It assumes variable-count Weak ETH: no fixed delta lets a deterministic algorithm decide 3-SAT on v variables in time 2^(v^(1 − δ)). Time 2^(v/log v) is still allowed, and so, the paper notes, are the recently claimed subexponential 3-SAT algorithms. Under that hypothesis, the best exponent is exactly 1/2. Division and square roots get the same bounds.

In the second paper’s words: Theorem 1.1, “There is a deterministic multitape Turing machine that computes the full product of two n-bit integers in worst-case time at most n(1 + log n)^(1/2+o(1)), visiting O(n) tape cells.” Theorem 1.2, under variable-count Weak ETH, every exact deterministic multitape multiplier needs at least c·n√(log n) steps on some pair of n-bit operands, for each sufficiently large n. “Whether O(n√(log n)) time is attainable remains open.” The paper says the subexponential 3-SAT algorithms of Anthropic’s Claude (our AN03) and of OpenAI (our 378) refute the original Exponential Time Hypothesis and are compatible with this weaker one; both are unreviewed claims. Anthropic makes Claude, which helps make this series.

What is new, and what they don’t claim

What is new: both exponents drop from nearly one to 1/2 + any ε, with lower bounds that match up to that epsilon. If the Fourier lower bound holds, it would be, by our reading of those sources, the first superlinear lower bound for an explicit matrix in this unrestricted model. Not claimed: the case ε = 0, which both papers leave open, any constants, or practical speed. The Fourier count charges only for gates, not for preparing coefficients. And both lower bounds rest on the family’s main paper, Euclidean geometry in the asymptotic spectrum of tensors, 86 pages; the multiplication bound also on the family’s circuit paper for Hamiltonian cycle. All are just as new and unreviewed.

Compared with our earlier episodes: 130 claimed Fourier circuits below N log N along a subsequence and an explicit algorithm with exponent 1 − 2.1 × 10⁻¹³ that also charged for indexing and preparing coefficients; 109 claimed exponent 1 − 2⁻¹⁸² on one fixed tape machine. The new upper bounds reuse result 109’s tape network; the tape paper also uses the Fourier paper’s algorithm.

How the proofs go

How the proofs go, by the papers’ account. The Fourier lower bound starts with a test that sees rank and sparsity at once. Put a matrix’s row label at the two left corners of a rectangle and its column label at the two right corners, and price it with the main paper’s Euclidean prices, where sharing a label costs distance. Flatten the rectangle, and the price reads the log of the rank. Make it thin, and it reads the log of the number of nonzero entries. At width one and height L, a character table costs (L + √(1 + L²))·log N, the most any matrix can. A permutation matrix, also of full rank, costs only (L + 1)·log N. With N 2^16 and L = 1/4: 20.49 against 20, by our arithmetic. Then a circuit is unrolled along about √(log N) time steps. Cutting the long paths costs one unit of rank per gate, and the short paths left reach few inputs. The test then forces at least c·N√(log N) gates.

The upper bound starts with the Walsh Hadamard transform, the plus and minus one transform on binary digits. Written as a product of m commuting operators, it acts at each frequency by a sign, set by the parity of how many operators give minus one. That parity is a polynomial of degree m. The trick: run many independent inputs together, each with its own known shift, all shifts used equally often. At each frequency the shifted count is binomial, almost always near the middle, and there a polynomial of degree about √(m log m) gives the parity exactly. With three operators, the paper’s linear predictor is exact on 6 of every 8 shifts, as our script confirms. The rare exceptions are repaired exactly through a few combinations sent to the frequency side. Recursion then gives the exponent 1/2 + ε, and small field extensions reach other groups and fields.

On tapes, the hard part is movement. Transposing an array, rows into columns, brings far-apart digits together, and doing it one address digit at a time costs a full pass each. The multiplication paper applies the same parity trick to these swaps on many streams at once, then runs short Fourier transforms of coprime lengths, precise enough that rounding gives the exact product. The lower bound runs backwards. Cut a run on an N × N bit array into epochs of N steps, and the tapes into blocks of N cells, and a fast run becomes a small network. If expander graphs could be placed with short edges in the main paper’s geometry, 3-SAT would get a power saving, against Weak ETH. Without that, there is a placement with short network edges but rows far from columns, so transposition needs about N²√(log N) steps. A reduction by Harvey and van der Hoeven carries this to multiplication.

The Lean proofs

OpenAI’s Lean proofs, not run by us, cover both Fourier bounds, including the batched and robust versions, but not the polynomial multiplication lower bound. For tapes, they cover the upper bound, the Weak ETH lower bound and the optimal exponent 1/2; by our reading, the Lean lower bound is stated more broadly than the paper’s theorem, also for randomized machines that may get up to 1% of the low bits wrong. Division and square roots are not formalized. The imports are large: about 8,100 files and a million lines for Fourier, and about 5,600 files for the Weak ETH bound. Our text search finds no sorry or axiom. The pull request says the Lean and LaTeX builds were not run, and no build log is published.

From the Fourier comparator, which imports only Mathlib: a gate evaluates to g.leftCoeff * v g.left + g.rightCoeff * v g.right, complexity is the least number of such gates, and the theorem states lower ≤ complexity (fourierMatrix G K) and (complexity (fourierMatrix G K) : ℝ) ≤ upper, where lower is c·N·√(log₂ N) and upper is C·N·(1 + log₂ N)^(1/2 + ε) (our notation for the Lean expressions), for every admissible group and field, and the same for the inverse transform. The Weak ETH comparator states its lower bound for machines with private random choices that succeed with probability at least 2^(−n/100), allowing n/100 wrong low bits; by our reading this is broader than the paper’s Theorem 1.2, and we found no manuscript stating it that way. The configurations permit only the axioms propext, Quot.sound and Classical.choice and do not enable the nanoda kernel. We did not read the proofs.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: the textbook radix-2 FFT for 8 samples has 3 stages of 4 butterflies, each butterfly two binary linear gates (u + wv and u − wv), so 24 gates = N log₂ N; our script builds this circuit and checks it against the 8-point DFT matrix (also for N = 2, 4, 16 and 1024). For comparison, N√(log₂ N) is 13.86 at N = 8 (constants ignored; the theorem is asymptotic), and N log N is √(log₂ N) times larger than N√(log₂ N): 3.16 at N = 2¹⁰, 4.47 at 2²⁰, 8 at 2⁶⁴.
  • Ours: the school method for two 8-digit numbers takes 64 digit products; Karatsuba’s recursion takes 3³ = 27 (checked by a counting implementation on random inputs; for 1,024 digits 59,049 vs 1,048,576).
  • Ours, from the Fourier paper’s Theorem 2.2: at width 1 and height L the character table’s rectangle price is (L + √(1 + L²))·log N and a permutation matrix’s is (L + 1)·log N; for N = 2¹⁶ and L = 1/4 that is 20.492 vs 20 (gap 0.492, close to L²·log N/2 = 0.5). At L = 1/√(log N) the gap is 1/(1 + √(1 + 1/log N)), about one half for every N.
  • Ours: the paper’s predictor for three binary operators, (−1)^|β|·(2c − 3), gives the right sign on 6 of the 8 shifts β for every sign pattern k (exceptions 000 and 111 at k = 000, 001 and 110 at k = 001, as the paper says).
  • Ours, from the paper’s window formulas (Section 4): the predictor degree is at most 80, 1,096 and 12,962 for m = 100, 10,000 and 1,000,000 operators; the exact binomial mass outside the window is far below the paper’s bound 1/(2m³).
  • Ours, text only: Lean import closures (OpenAI files) of the solution modules: Fourier 8,115 files, 1,010,494 lines; tape upper bound 3,225 files, 425,187 lines; Weak ETH lower bound 5,571 files, 801,040 lines (including the upper-bound code). Outside libraries (Mathlib d13f23b and others) not scanned; Lean v4.34.1; no sorry, axiom, native_decide or unsafe outside comments (‘admit’ appears only as a variable name; one ‘opaque’ definition that carries its own proof).
