An exact limit for a quantum gravity bound?

A Hexagon preprint contributed by Anthropic claims the exact best slope, about 9.1266, of a bound physicists have used since 2009 on the lightest state of a two-dimensional conformal field theory at large central charge.

The claim, in plain words

Picture a system at a critical point, where zooming in or out changes nothing. In one space and one time dimension, physicists describe it with a two-dimensional conformal field theory. Each basic state, called a primary, has a scaling dimension, Δ, which sets its energy on the circle. The vacuum sits at zero. How high can the lightest other primary, Δ₁, sit? That is the gap. The central charge, c, roughly counts the theory’s degrees of freedom.

The tool is a donut. Put the theory on a torus: space is a circle, and so is time, whose length is one over the temperature. The partition function, Z, adds up all the states, weighted by their dimensions. Swap the two circles: the S transformation, τ → −1/τ. Same donut, same total. In the paper’s reduced form, Ẑ(β) = Ẑ(1/β)/β. Spinless: only this untwisted swap is used. Every state must fit this one equation, and that caps the gap.

At large c, this is a question about quantum gravity in a simplified three-dimensional universe. Its black holes, called BTZ black holes, begin at Δ = (c − 1)/12; pure gravity would put Δ₁ near c/12. In 2009, Simeon Hellerman proved that Δ₁ is at most c/6, plus about one half. Such bounds read Δ₁ at most c/Λ, at large c; Hellerman’s Λ is six, and a bigger Λ is stronger. In 2013, Friedan and Keller, using an earlier argument, showed that Λ can never exceed twelve, the black hole threshold. In 2019, Hartman, Mazáč and Rastelli proved 8.503, and computer searches extrapolated to about 9.08. So the best slope lay between 8.503 and twelve.

The claim. A preprint on Hexagon, posted November 7, 2026, 131 pages, with the author line Claude, Anthropic, and no named human author. Under the stated assumptions, the main theorem says the best slope at large c is exactly Λ_V, 12π²/(π² + 4 arcsinh²1): about 9.1266. Best means best among functionals in a broad class containing every finite combination of derivatives, the kind computer searches use.

The paper labels each part. Optimality: no functional in the class beats c/Λ_V by more than order c^(1/3) log^(2/3) c. Proved by hand, with no computer. Attainment: an explicit functional gives Δ₁ below c/Λ_V, plus 0.483 c^(1/3), plus a bounded term, for all large enough c. Proved with computer assistance: finitely many inequalities checked in interval arithmetic, with guaranteed error bounds. Conjectured: the best correction, 0.27666 c^(1/3), backed by numerics up to c of 2,048.

This is mathematical physics, at the edge of the series’ scope; it is here because the paper states precise bounds and proves them, with each part labelled by status.

What it doesn’t claim

What is not claimed. This is about the method, not about which theories exist. A window from the black hole threshold up to about c/9.13 can never be excluded this way. The paper says nothing about whether theories live there, about bounds that use spin, or about pure gravity. The central charges where optimality and attainment take hold are not computed, and at every c that computers reach, numerical bounds stay the best. And whether the best in the class equals the limit of computer searches is a conjecture.

The paper also says the constant 0.483 is not the best one, and that it has not checked whether the analytic functionals of Hartman, Mazáč and Rastelli belong to its class.

How the proof goes

  1. Why it is hard. How the proof goes, in the paper’s account. A functional turns the torus equation into one sum over the states; to exclude a gap, it must be positive above the gap and on the vacuum. The catch: through the swap, the vacuum acts like a continuum of heavy states, with a density that grows like e^(4π√(at)). So the functional’s negative part, below the gap, must outweigh everything above it, and the functional must fall as fast as that density grows. But each functional is, up to sign, its own Fourier transform, and such functions resist falling fast.
  2. Electrostatics on a strip. The paper’s key idea is electrostatics. Change variables to b, √(3t/(c − 1)), and let b be complex. Then the log of the functional’s size, divided by c minus one, acts like a two-dimensional electric potential, with a positive charge at each zero, on a strip whose edges pass through the vacuum. Such a potential has no maximum inside, so it stays below an explicit harmonic function on the strip, slit along the real axis. That bound first touches the Cardy line where sinh(πx) = 1: at x*, about 0.2805. And 1/Λ = 1/12 + x*²/3: 9.1266.
  3. Attainment and credit. For the other half, zeros placed by the charge density on the slits build a functional that reaches the slope, and the computer checks the leftover inequalities. The paper says its strategy for optimality is that of OpenAI’s proof for a twin problem, linked to sphere packing, with slope π²; it has not verified that proof, and nothing depends on it. It also traces the old 9.08 to the choice of fitting curve, not to the data: the same fit, applied to its own formula, gives 9.085.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: Λ_V = 12π²/(π² + 4 arcsinh²1) = 9.1266340; x* = arcsinh(1)/π = 0.2805499, and sinh(πx*) = 1; ξ* = x*²/3 = 0.0262361; 1/Λ_V = 1/12 + ξ*; the window is 12ξ* = 31.5% of the range c/12 to c/6 (the paper: “the lower 31%”).
  • The paper’s Table 2: 24 numerical bounds for 24 ≤ c ≤ 2,048; our c/Δ₁ matches every printed ratio, every bound lies above c/Λ_V, and at c = 2,048 the bound 227.7834 lies between c/Λ_V = 224.40 and c/8.503 = 240.86.
  • Earlier bounds, each in its own source: Hellerman (arXiv 0902.2790, February 2009): Δ₁ < (c_L + c_R)/12 + 0.473695; Friedan and Keller (arXiv 1307.6562, 2013): the method “cannot exclude any ∆1 < (c − 1)/12”; Hartman, Mazáč and Rastelli (arXiv 1905.01319, 2019): Δ₀ ≲ c/8.503, proved analytically; Afkhami-Jeddi, Hartman and Tajdini (arXiv 1903.06272, 2019): numerical evidence for Δ₁ ≲ c/9.1 (9.08 and c = 1800 per the paper).
  • From the paper: 0.483 (attainment constant, not the best one), 0.44 (optimality rate), 0.27666 (conjectured coefficient), c ≥ 40 (standing assumption of the rigorous statements), numerics up to c = 2,048 extrapolating to 9.1263 ± 0.002.
