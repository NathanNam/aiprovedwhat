The claim, in plain words

Put an angle at every point of a square grid, and let neighbours prefer to agree, with strength β. In one dimension the memory is short: our exact calculation gives a correlation length a little under 2β. In two dimensions, Berezinskii, Kosterlitz and Thouless found a sharp transition: below a critical temperature, correlations fade only like a power of the distance; above it, vortices unbind and correlations die exponentially. Fröhlich and Spencer proved in 1981 that the power-law phase exists.

Physics predicted far more: at the critical point, correlations fall like r−1/4(log r)1/8; just above it, the correlation length explodes like e to a constant over the square root of the distance to the critical point; and the stiffness jumps from 2/π to zero. In our simulations at the critical point, on grids up to 512 across, the decay looks like a power near −0.21, not −1/4: the logarithm disguises itself.

Result 216 bundles six OpenAI preprints, 461 pages, dated September 24 and October 5, 2026. For the square-lattice XY model with nearest neighbours, they claim the critical exponent is exactly 1/4; the logarithmic correction, with power 1/8; the essential singularity of the correlation length; the critical spin field as a continuum object with stiffness 2/π; and the magnetization at the centre of a critical square. A 169-page foundation paper claims that integer height models become Gaussian fields up to and including their roughening point, for every finite symmetric range; it says this covers a conjecture of Bauerschmidt, Park and Rodriguez, who had called short-range cases out of reach.

What it doesn’t claim

The value of the critical temperature, which no paper computes; the constants in front, which the papers call model-specific; any other lattice for the spin results.

How the proof goes

  1. A squeeze. Expand each bond in Fourier modes and the angles become integer heights: is a random landscape rough? Its roughness coefficient a sets the spin exponent, 2π/a. Comparisons built on a lattice Gaussian inequality from computer science show a is either zero or at least 8π. At the critical point it cannot be zero, because a known lower bound forbids fast decay; it cannot exceed 8π, because that phase is stable and would extend below the critical point. So a = 8π and the exponent is 1/4.
  2. The logarithm. With a at exactly 8π, vortices are marginal. A renormalization group tracks them scale by scale; their weight, and the stiffness correction it causes, dies like one over the number of scales, and summing that slow decay gives (log r)1/8.
  3. The singularity. Just above the critical point, the same flow follows the Kosterlitz equations; their cotangent solution hits a pole after a number of scales proportional to one over the square root of the distance, which gives the essential singularity.

The other four spin papers build on the exponent paper, which builds on the foundation.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours, exact: the 1D chain’s correlation length 1.24, 2.78, 8.87, 18.95 at β = 1, 2, 5, 10.
  • Ours, simulated on tori (βc ≈ 1.1199 is an outside estimate): local decay slope −0.21 at the critical point on a 512 × 512 grid; the stiffness crosses 2/π between β = 1.05 and 1.10 for sizes 16 to 128.
