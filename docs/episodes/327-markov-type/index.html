Walks reveal the shape?

An AI preprint claims that nontrivial Markov type forces superreflexivity

The claim, in plain words

Run a stationary, reversible random walk on a finite graph and watch it through any map into a Banach space. Markov type p says that after t steps the walk has moved, on average, at most a constant times t^(1/p) times a typical single step. In a Hilbert space, Ball showed in 1992 that p can be two: the walk diffuses. In ℓ₁ it cannot: on the Hamming cube our computer finds the ratio for p = 2 growing past 26 at 128 dimensions, while in Hilbert space it never exceeds 1.

A space is superreflexive when it has an equivalent norm whose unit ball is uniformly round. In 2006 Naor, Peres, Schramm and Sheffield showed that smooth norms give Markov type above 1; with Pisier’s renorming, every superreflexive space has it. In 2012 Naor asked the converse: can a space have Markov type above 1 without such a norm? The question was listed as open, and ordinary Rademacher type is known not to be enough.

Result 327 is one OpenAI paper, 14 pages, dated September 23 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims that every real Banach space with Markov type p for some p above 1 is superreflexive. Together with the known converse, nontrivial Markov type is exactly superreflexivity.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: any relation between the exponents, any explicit constant beyond the proof’s own, or anything about general metric spaces. The walks the proof builds are finite but astronomically large.

How the proof goes

  1. Partial sums that swing between two levels. Suppose a space is not superreflexive. Then it contains, almost isometrically, every finite piece of a norm in which merging neighbouring entries never increases the length, like the size of the largest partial sum. In such a norm, a sum whose partial sums swing many times between two levels a distance l apart has length at least l/6, measured in single steps. The proof needs a reversible walk whose labels do exactly that, with l proportional to the number of steps.
  2. The free group does it. The free group supplies it. A random word of length n keeps, on average, at least half its letters after cancellation; our computer gets 64.75 at 128. Commuting letters would only diffuse. Ping-pong maps let the free group act on the line, and our computer confirms the paper’s two lemmas, for one choice of arcs, on 13,120 reduced words. A finite Ramsey argument lets this motion run inside a finite stationary, reversible walk, and a lattice trick turns edge labels into positions. Then, with probability at least 3/20, the walk moves at least n/24 step lengths, which contradicts Markov type p above 1 for large n.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours, exact: on the Hamming cube, the Markov-type ratio for p = 2 grows to 26.6 at 128 dimensions in ℓ₁ and stays at most 1 in Hilbert space.
  • Ours, exact: a random free-group word of length n keeps on average at least n/2 letters (64.75 at n = 128); commuting letters only diffuse.
  • Ours: the paper’s ping-pong lemmas hold on all 13,120 reduced words of length up to 8, for one choice of arcs.
  • The paper’s constants: the walk moves at least n/24 step lengths with probability at least 3/20.
