The claim, in plain words

The classical Brunn–Minkowski inequality says that blending two convex bodies K and L, by averaging every point of K with every point of L using fixed weights, gives a body whose volume is at least the weighted geometric mean of their volumes. Describe a convex body around the origin by its support function, how far it reaches in each direction; the classical blend averages support functions. The logarithmic version takes the geometric mean of the two support functions instead, and keeps the points that stay within that reach in every direction. That body sits inside the classical blend, so a volume bound for it is stronger. For a square of side 2 and the same square turned 45 degrees (areas 4), the classical midpoint is a regular octagon of area 4.83, and the logarithmic midpoint a slightly smaller one of area 4.69, still above 4.

Böröczky, Lutwak, Yang and Zhang conjectured in 2012 that for bodies symmetric about the origin the logarithmic blend always has volume at least the geometric mean, and proved it in the plane. Without symmetry it can fail: shifting the square sideways by 0.6, with the origin still inside, makes the logarithmic midpoint of the square and its shifted copy only 3.79. Earlier work settled bodies with extra symmetries (Saroglou; Böröczky and Kalantzopoulos; Rotem for complex norms) and local versions (Colesanti, Livshyts and Marsiglietti; Kolesnikov and Milman; van Handel for zonoids; Putterman showed local implies global). Stancu announced a proof in 2018, with details of a flow argument deferred. Nearby is the (B)-conjecture: for an even log-concave measure μ and a symmetric body K, log μ(etK) should be concave in t, proved for the Gaussian measure by Cordero-Erausquin, Fradelizi and Maurey in 2004.

Result 091 is one OpenAI preprint dated September 23, 2026 (20 pages). It claims the logarithmic Brunn–Minkowski inequality for every pair of origin-symmetric convex bodies in every dimension, with no smoothness assumption. As corollaries it claims the symmetric Lp Brunn–Minkowski inequality for every p between 0 and 1 and, through a transfer theorem of Saroglou, the logarithmic inequality for every even log-concave measure, and with it the (B)-conjecture.

What it doesn’t claim

The claims are for symmetric bodies only. The paper does not determine the equality cases in higher dimensions (in the plane they are dilates and parallelograms with parallel sides), and the (B)-conjecture it proves concerns dilations by a single scalar.

How the proof goes

  1. From slabs to a variance. Finitely many symmetric slabs cut out a symmetric polytope. Replace its indicator by the smooth weight exp(−ε|x|²/2 − Σ(⟨pi, x⟩/hi)q); as q grows and ε fades, the integral becomes the polytope’s volume. It is enough that the logarithm of the integral is concave when each slab width changes geometrically. Its second derivative is a variance minus an average, so everything reduces to one bound: the variance of Σ biψi is at most Σ bi² E ψi, where ψi are the power terms.
  2. Moment coordinates. Following the moment-measure theory of Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag, with a theorem of Berman and Berndtsson and a Hessian estimate of Klartag, the paper writes the probability as the image of e−φ under the gradient of a convex function φ whose Hessian is positive and bounded above. A differential operator then produces test functions dense among centered even functions; the only obstructions are affine functions, constants removed by centering and linear functions by evenness, which is the key place symmetry enters. A tensor calculation leaves a sum of explicit squares, and two Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities give the bound.
  3. Limits and transfers. Letting q grow and ε vanish gives polytopes, and dense slab directions give every symmetric body. The Lp inequality follows by the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality after normalizing volumes. Saroglou’s transfer theorem and a reduction for measures living on a subspace give every even log-concave measure, and two dilates of one body give the (B)-conjecture.

The numbers in the video

  • Over 300 random pairs of symmetric polygons the smallest ratio of the logarithmic blend’s volume to the geometric mean was 1.0253; over 80 pairs of symmetric polyhedra, 1.1754. Dilates and parallel rectangles give exact equality.

Cordero-Erausquin, Fradelizi and Maurey proved it for the Gaussian measure in 2004.

3:52How the proof goes

The proof begins with slabs. Finitely many symmetric slabs, each between two parallel hyperplanes, cut out a symmetric polytope. Replace the polytope’s indicator by a smooth weight: e to the minus a small Gaussian term, minus a sum of even powers of the slab coordinates. As the power grows and the Gaussian term fades, the integral of this weight becomes the polytope’s volume. So it is enough that the logarithm of the integral is concave when each slab width changes geometrically. Its second derivative is a variance minus an average, so everything reduces to one variance bound: the variance of a weighted sum of the power terms is at most the sum of the squared weights times their averages. In our tests on 120 random planar cases, the largest ratio was 0.9975, never above one, and in one dimension, where the volume inequality is an equality, it creeps toward one as the Gaussian term vanishes. To prove the variance bound, the paper changes coordinates. Following the moment-measure theory of Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag, and using a theorem of Berman and Berndtsson and a Hessian estimate of Klartag, it writes the probability as the image of a measure e^(−φ) under the gradient of a convex function φ, whose Hessian is positive and bounded above. In these coordinates, a differential operator produces test functions that are dense among centered even functions. The only obstructions are affine functions: centering removes constants, and evenness removes linear ones. This is the key place symmetry enters. Then a tensor calculation leaves a remainder that is a sum of explicit squares, and two Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities give the bound. The rest is limits and known transfers. Letting the power grow and the Gaussian term vanish gives the inequality for polytopes, and adding slab directions densely gives every symmetric body. The Lᵖ inequality follows by the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality after normalizing volumes. For measures, Saroglou showed that the volume inequality transfers to every even log-concave density in the same dimension, and a reduction handles measures living on a subspace; applying it to two dilates of one body gives the (B)-conjecture. For the density e^(−|x₁| − |x₂|), and a tilted square, our computed log measure of eᵗ times the square is concave in t, as the theory predicts.

6:27Where it stands

The preprint is not peer reviewed and has not been revised. OpenAI’s Lean formalization covers the main theorem; we have not run it. The Lᵖ corollary is a short argument in the paper, while the measure results rest on Saroglou’s transfer theorem and Borell’s description of log-concave measures. The paper acknowledges Stancu’s earlier announcement. It is not among the papers OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, the logarithmic Brunn–Minkowski conjecture is settled for symmetric bodies in every dimension, together with the symmetric Lᵖ inequalities for p between 0 and 1, and the (B)-conjecture for even log-concave measures. The equality cases in general are not settled here.
