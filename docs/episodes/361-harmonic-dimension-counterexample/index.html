More harmonic than flat space?

Two AI preprints claim counterexamples to integer-degree harmonic dimension comparison

Covers result 361 in OpenAI’s catalog: all 2 papers. See its connections to other results and the work it cites.

The claim, in plain words

A harmonic function has zero Laplacian: in flat space, its value at each point is the average over any sphere around it. In ordinary three-space, the harmonic polynomials of degree at most k form a space of dimension (k + 1)²: our computer counts 1, 4, 9, 16 by exact linear algebra. Yau asked whether, on a complete manifold with nonnegative Ricci curvature, the harmonic functions growing at most like the k-th power of distance can ever outnumber those of Euclidean space.

Li and Tam settled linear growth in 1989, and Colding and Minicozzi proved these spaces are always finite-dimensional. Donnelly beat the Euclidean count in 2001, but at a growth rate strictly between one and two, in dimension five and up. For integer degrees the question stayed open: two preprints from this summer, by Lin, Wang and Xu and by Cai and Lai, each proved the bound in a special case; Cai and Lai described the integer case as still open.

Result 361 is two independent OpenAI papers, dated September 25 and 26 and made public October 6, 2026. The 35-page paper claims a complete metric with nonnegative Ricci curvature in 16 dimensions with more harmonic functions of growth at most 50,000 than Euclidean space, by about one part in 8,000. The 39-page paper claims, in three dimensions and for every large enough k, metrics with nonnegative Ricci curvature and at least c times (k + 1)² such functions, for any c below 9/4 of the volume ratio.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: one metric for every k; each metric is built for its own k, and the three-dimensional bound needs k beyond an unspecified threshold. Nonnegative Ricci curvature is all they assume; this summer’s special cases also needed nonnegative sectional curvature or conformal flatness, so they are not contradicted. We found no competing claim.

How the proof goes

  1. A shape that keeps changing. The trick in both papers is that the shape of the space at large distances keeps changing. On a fixed cone with a shrunken cross-section, harmonic functions grow faster, so fewer fit under degree k: our computer counts 81 against Euclidean space’s 121 at k = 10. But if the cross-section keeps changing, a harmonic function only needs its average growth rate to stay below k, and the papers make many modes take turns being slow.
  2. Two constructions. The three-dimensional paper steers eigenvalue crossings on nearly round spheres so that a whole band of modes cycles; each mode’s average rate is about two thirds of the band edge, divided by the cone’s opening, which is where 9/4 of the volume ratio comes from. Our computer reproduces that limit with round-sphere bands. The 16-dimensional paper uses Berger spheres and exact counting; our computer recomputes its selected dimension, a 59-digit number, exactly from the paper’s description.
