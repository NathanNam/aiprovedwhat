The claims, in plain words

Shine a light on a convex body from every direction and record the area of its shadow. Those areas define a second convex body, the projection body ΠK: how far it reaches from its center in each direction is the area of the shadow cast by light shining in that direction. The ratio |ΠK|/|K|n−1, where n is the dimension, does not change under affine maps. In three dimensions a ball gives about 7.40, a cube 8, a regular octahedron 9 and a tetrahedron 18.

In 1971 Petty conjectured that, in every dimension from three on, ellipsoids give the smallest value, and only ellipsoids. (A related inequality of Petty for the polar of the projection body was already known.) Lutwak reformulated the conjecture; Saroglou and Zvavitch, and Ivaki, proved local versions near the ball; Mielke-Sulz treated bodies of revolution; and a 2026 preprint of Chen, Feng, Li, Xi and Xu proved dimension three. In 1996 Brannen conjectured that simplices give the largest value. In dimension three that is true, by the same preprint, but a 2026 preprint of Feng, Hu, Liu and Xu found counterexamples in every dimension from nine on.

Result 088 is two OpenAI preprints dated September 24, 2026. The first (30 pages) proves Petty’s conjecture in every dimension n ≥ 4: for every convex body, |ΠK|/|K|n−1 ≥ κn−1n κn2−n, the ellipsoid value, with equality exactly for ellipsoids. Together with the three-dimensional preprint the conjecture would hold for every n ≥ 3, and the paper derives the Lutwak–Petty lower-degree projection inequalities, a sharp affine Sobolev-type inequality, and Holmes–Thompson isoperimetry in normed spaces. The second (7 pages) shows that the product of two ten-dimensional simplices beats the twenty-dimensional simplex by the factor 22,355,476/22,020,096, about 1.5%, and that repeating ten-dimensional factors makes the excess grow exponentially with the dimension.

What it doesn’t claim

In dimension four and up, the largest value of the ratio, and which bodies attain it, are not determined. The three-dimensional case of Petty’s conjecture is a separate preprint, not this one.

How the proofs go

  1. A measure on vectors. After normalizing the volume, the first variation of volume gives a probability measure on vectors: integrating the support function of any symmetric body against it gives at least a root of that body’s volume. The same measure represents the projection body through the absolute dot product. The core theorem bounds from below the double integral of the absolute dot product for any two measures with that property; applied to the body and its rescaled projection body, it gives the inequality.
  2. Spherical harmonics and affine position. A new inequality bounds the higher harmonics of a power of any norm by a difference of its spherical means, strictly unless the norm is Euclidean. A sign test bounds the pairing by a bilinear form in the cosine and Funk transforms, and the norm estimate controls every harmonic of degree four and up. The degree-two part is removed by choosing affine coordinates: a family of variational minimizers is extended continuously to singular matrices, an inward-pointing field appears at the boundary, and Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem gives the position. Equality then forces an ellipsoid.
  3. The product counterexample. For a polytope, the projection body is a sum of segments, one for each facet, of length its area. For a product of two polytopes the facets split into two families, and the ratio multiplies. A simplex in dimension d gives (d + 1)dd/d!, and the value for ten, squared, exceeds the value for twenty.

The numbers in the video

  • A short Python script computes projection bodies of polytopes as sums of facet segments. In dimension 3 the ratio is 7.40 for a ball, 8 for a cube, 9 for a regular octahedron and 18 for a tetrahedron; in dimension 4, 12.64, 16, 20 and 53.33. Hulls of 20, 60, 180 and 540 random points on the sphere give 9.68, 8.07, 7.64 and 7.48, approaching the ball. 600 random polytopes in dimension 3 and 200 in dimension 4 all lie between the ball and simplex values.
  • The product rule checks numerically, the exact ratio for two ten-dimensional simplices is 22,355,476/22,020,096 ≈ 1.0152, and among products of two simplices (both of dimension at least two), dimension 20 is the first where one beats the simplex. No computation can test every body.
