The claims, in plain words

When you braid three strands, the moves obey one rule: crossing 1, 2, 1 equals crossing 2, 1, 2. Artin groups generalize this. For each pair of generators, alternating words of some length m are equal: m = 2 means the generators commute, 3 gives the braid rule, and ∞ means no rule at all. Adding the rule that each generator squares to one gives a reflection group. Each Artin group comes with a space built from simple pieces, the Salvetti complex; for three-strand braids it is a hexagon with its sides glued. The K(π,1) conjecture says this space has no higher-dimensional holes beyond those the group itself forces: its universal cover is contractible.

The braid case goes back to Fadell, Fox and Neuwirth in 1962, and Deligne proved all finite reflection types in 1972. Van der Lek wrote down the general form, crediting Arnold, Pham and Thom. Many families followed: the affine ones in 2021 and dimension three in 2025.

Result 254 is three OpenAI preprints, of 62, 55 and 29 pages, all dated September 23, 2026:

  1. The first claims the K(π,1) conjecture for every Artin group with finitely many generators.
  2. The second claims that intersections of parabolic subgroups are again parabolic. It also claims that the word problem is decidable for all these groups.
  3. The third claims an explicit Artin group with 116 generators that has no geometric action on any CAT(0) space.

What they don’t claim

  • Anything about Artin groups with infinitely many generators.
  • Which Artin groups are CAT(0). Many are, including a triangle subgroup inside the counterexample.
  • That 116 generators are needed.
  • How fast the word-problem algorithm runs. The word-problem claim is also missing from the catalog’s summary and from the Lean.

How the proofs go

  1. One engine for the first two papers. The group acts on complexes over a small algebra, and each group element gets a profile by counting generators layer by layer. Harmonic averaging, with weights 2 cos(π/m), turns these profiles into heights on cosets of the spherical subgroups. Two local estimates then let the paper build the space of cosets one vertex at a time without ever creating a hole. The same machine shows that stabilizers are parabolic, and gives a membership test that decides the word problem. The two papers share their method and more than 500 Lean files, so they are not independent confirmations.
  2. Angles that do not add up. In a CAT(0) action, the translation lengths of commuting elements behave like Euclidean vectors. Forty-strand braid blocks squeeze six angles around a triangle subgroup below 60°, so the six flat sectors cannot close up. A winding argument turns this into a contradiction in every dimension.

Our computer built these and computed their homology; for 4-strand braids a 2-torsion class appears in degree 2, matching the known answer. Our census: with labels 2, 3, 4 and ∞, every diagram on up to 4 generators was already covered, but on 5, up to 140 of 10,688 were not covered by the theorems we checked.

1:29The claims

Result 254 is three OpenAI preprints, of 62, 55 and 29 pages, all dated September 23, 2026. The first claims the K(π,1) conjecture for every Artin group with finitely many generators. The second claims that intersections of parabolic subgroups, the conjugates of subgroups generated by some of the generators, are again parabolic; it also claims the word problem is decidable for all of these groups. The third claims an explicit Artin group with 116 generators that has no geometric action on any CAT(0) space. Not claimed: Artin groups with infinitely many generators; which Artin groups are CAT(0), since many are, including a triangle subgroup inside the counterexample; that 116 generators are needed; or how fast the word-problem algorithm runs. The word-problem claim is not in the catalog’s summary or in the Lean. By our count, that 116-generator group’s Salvetti complex has about 1.65 trillion cells, in 39 dimensions.

2:41How the proofs go

The first two papers share one new engine. The group acts on complexes over a small algebra, and each group element gets a profile by counting generators layer by layer. Harmonic averaging, with weights 2 cos(π/m), turns these into heights on cosets of the spherical subgroups. Two local estimates, one from a positive definite form and one ruling out an isolated balanced layer, let the paper build the space of cosets one vertex at a time, never creating a hole. The parabolic paper uses the same machine to show that stabilizers are parabolic, which gives the intersection theorem, and a membership test that decides the word problem. These are not independent confirmations: the two proofs share their method, and more than 500 Lean files. The third paper is separate. In a CAT(0) action, translation lengths of commuting elements behave like Euclidean vectors. 40-strand braid blocks squeeze six angles around a triangle subgroup below 60°; our arithmetic gives at most 58.1° each, at most 349° in total, so the six flat sectors cannot close up. A winding argument turns that into a contradiction in every dimension. Our check: in the triangle subgroup’s own flat picture, six 60° corners meet around each point; the braid blocks are what bend it.

4:07Where it stands

The preprints are unrefereed and unchanged since release. In Lean, all three main theorems are formalized, about 91,000 lines with no unproved steps, though not the word-problem corollary, and the parabolic paper is missing from the repository’s formalization list; we did not build it. OSVerify has not reviewed them. Giulio Tiozzo wrote that, using OpenAI’s own model, he got a 12-generator example in 10 minutes; that is unpublished and unverified, and no expert check has appeared. If they hold, every finitely generated Artin group has a classifying space we can write down, and the groups are tame in that sense; yet one of them, with 116 generators, refuses every non-positively curved geometry.
