One small patch sees everything?

Three AI preprints claim uniqueness for the smooth anisotropic Calderón problem from one boundary patch, and nonuniqueness for rough conductivities

The claim, in plain words

Can you tell what is inside a body from voltages and currents measured on its surface? Calderón posed the mathematical version in 1980. On a line, a toy version, the answer is no: our computer builds four different layered conductivities on an interval with exactly the same end-to-end response. And a change of coordinates that fixes the boundary never changes the measurements, so for anisotropic materials the inside can at best be recovered up to such a change.

On a disc the measurements carry one number per mode, but the inside fades fast: our computer finds an inclusion’s effect falling from about 0.3 in the first mode to about 10⁻⁵ by the tenth. For smooth anisotropic metrics in dimension three and up, uniqueness was known only in special cases: real-analytic metrics, by Lee and Uhlmann and by Lassas and Uhlmann, and special geometries or metrics near special ones. Papers this summer still called the smooth problem open, and a 2024 claim to solve it was withdrawn three days later as incorrect.

Result 365 is three OpenAI papers, made public October 6, 2026. The September 24 paper, 43 pages, claims that for smooth metrics in dimension three and up, zero-frequency measurements on any one boundary patch, with inputs and outputs on that same patch, determine the metric up to a change of coordinates fixed on the patch. The October 5 paper, 50 pages, the headline, claims the same while also recovering a unitary connection on a trivial rank-two bundle, up to gauge.

What it doesn’t claim

The September 23 paper gives the contrast: in three dimensions, two different scalar conductivities, bounded above and below but not smooth, with identical full boundary data. Not claimed: stability or a reconstruction method, nonzero frequencies, where smooth counterexamples exist, or inputs and outputs on different patches. Specialists who cite the smooth papers describe them as an announcement.

How the proof goes

  1. Growing the matched region. For the smooth result, equal patch data first give equal boundary jets. A small cap glued outside the patch makes the two Green kernels agree there, and harmonic functions from sources in the cap label interior points, defining a matched region. At its frontier, harmonic functions are moved across shrinking spheres; the crux is a uniform bound on this transfer: it makes harmonic differences of order r², so the metric mismatch is of order r^(3/2), better than the assumed order r, and the region can grow. Finally a leftover conformal factor is forced to be one, because in dimension three and up such a factor changes which functions are harmonic; our computer shows the same effect on the energy, which vanishes in dimension two.
  2. Hiding in rough conductivities. The nonuniqueness paper builds a block from box-shaped tori, one outer and two inner, and nests copies inside each other forever; our computer checks its printed numbers, like two s cubed equals 0.3 seven, below one, so the nested volume shrinks to zero. The block’s flux rule forces every solution to share one surface average, and a hidden potential then changes the conductivity without changing any measurement.
