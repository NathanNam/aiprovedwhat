Squarefree, 3 in 4

Squarefree values of quartics, and power-free values of polynomials in every degree from 4

The claim, in plain words

An integer is squarefree if no prime divides it twice. The values of n4 + 2 are 3, 18, 83, 258, 627, …; 18 = 2·32 is not squarefree, the others are. One prime at a time, p2 divides f(n) for ρ(p2) of the p2 residue classes of n (for n4 + 2 and p = 3, the classes 2 and 7 mod 9). If the primes acted independently, the fraction of squarefree values would be cf = ∏p (1 − ρ(p2)/p2), which is 0.7567 for n4 + 2. The squarefree-values conjecture says this is right for every irreducible integer polynomial with no fixed prime-square divisor.

Result 020 (55 pages) claims it for quartics, and more generally: for every irreducible integer polynomial f of degree d with 4 ≤ d ≤ 8, with no prime p such that pk divides all its values, where k = d − 2, the number of n ≤ X with f(n) free of k-th powers is cf·X + o(X), with cf = ∏p (1 − ρf(pk)/pk) > 0. Combined with Browning’s theorem for degree 9 and up, this covers every degree from 4 up.

What it doesn’t claim

The theorem gives the density with no stated rate of convergence, and no uniformity is claimed as the polynomial varies. It concerns one fixed polynomial at a time, at positive integers. Two other recent preprints claim n4 + 1 (one of them also n4 + 2); this paper uses neither.

What was known

  • Ricci (1933): the asymptotic for exponents at least d. Hooley (1967): exponent d − 1, which includes squarefree values of cubics.
  • Erdős (1953) wrote that he could not even prove that n4 + 2 is squarefree infinitely often.
  • Exponent d − 2: Nair (1976) for degree 24 and up, Heath-Brown (2006) for degree 10 and up, Browning (2011) for degree 9 and up. Heath-Brown (2013) handled binomials xd + c at this exponent for d ≥ 6.
  • Granville (1998): the abc conjecture would give the predicted squarefree density for every separable polynomial.

How the proof goes

  1. The large-prime estimate. Small primes act independently by the Chinese remainder theorem, and primes up to X are rare enough. Everything reduces to showing that, among X < n ≤ 2X, at most X1−δ inputs have pk | f(n) for some prime p > X.
  2. The surface. Writing f(n) = ypk puts each bad input on the surface f(x) = yzk. For very large p, the determinant method (Bombieri–Pila, Heath-Brown, Salberger) traps the integer points in a box on relatively few auxiliary curves.
  3. Number-field factorization. For the harder range, with θ a root of f, ideal factorization gives αkβ = μ(n − θ) with α, β of balanced size. Their coordinates fill two boxes, cut into small paired boxes; in each pair a determinant gives a polynomial equation, and lattice reduction plus a uniform counting theorem with adaptive auxiliary primes finish the count.
  4. Quartics. Surfaces and curves need sharper geometry. In the limiting curve case, a polynomial of degree 5 with four prescribed critical values, only finitely many polynomials occur up to a change of variable, so their values form one set of size O(X1/5), removed once.

0:00Squarefree values

Plug n = 1, 2, 3, and so on into n⁴ + 2. You get 3, 18, 83, 258, 627. 18 is 2 × 3²: a square divides it. The others are squarefree: no prime appears twice. Up to 30,000, about 75.7 percent of these values are squarefree. That matches, almost exactly, a number you can predict from the primes alone. In 1953, Paul Erdős wrote that he couldn’t even prove n⁴ + 2 is squarefree infinitely often. Result 020 in OpenAI’s math repository claims that every quartic like it has squarefree values with exactly the predicted density, and more.

0:46The prediction

Where does the prediction come from? Take one prime at a time. 3² divides n⁴ + 2 exactly when n leaves a remainder of 2 or 7 when divided by 9. That’s 2 classes out of 9, so a fraction 7/9 survives. Do the same for every prime, and multiply the surviving fractions. For n⁴ + 2, the product is about 0.7567. The conjecture says this is right for any irreducible polynomial, as long as no fixed prime square divides all its values. Small primes are easy: finitely many of them act independently, by the Chinese remainder theorem. The trouble is primes larger than n itself. For a quartic, a prime as large as n² could have its square divide the value, and the sieve has nothing to say about it. For example, 97⁴ + 2 is divisible by 179².

1:43What was known

In degree d, the next exponent to reach is d − 2: for a quartic, that’s squares. Exponents of d or more were handled by Giovanni Ricci in 1933. Christopher Hooley reached d − 1 in 1967, which settles squarefree values of cubics. The exponent d − 2 was the frontier. Mohan Nair reached it in degree 24 and up, Roger Heath-Brown in degree 10 and up, and Tim Browning, in 2011, in degree 9 and up. Degrees 4 through 8 stayed open in general. Assuming the abc conjecture, Andrew Granville proved everything in 1998.

2:23The claim

The preprint, 55 pages, dated September 24, claims degrees 4 through 8. For every irreducible integer polynomial of degree d in that range, with no fixed prime power of exponent d − 2 dividing all its values, the values free of such powers have exactly the predicted density. For quartics, that’s squarefree values. With Browning’s theorem, every degree from 4 up is covered. OpenAI also published a Lean proof of the statement for every degree from 4 up: 63 files, about 23,000 lines, not counting Mathlib. It’s listed in OpenAI’s formalization catalog, and we haven’t run the check.

3:05How the proof goes

Everything reduces to one estimate. Write k for d − 2. Among the inputs n between X and 2X, at most X^(1−δ) have a value divisible by pᵏ, for some prime p larger than X. That’s a vanishing fraction. Write f(n) = y·pᵏ. That puts each bad input on a surface. When p is very large, the determinant method handles the surface directly: it traps all the integer points in a box on relatively few auxiliary curves. For the harder middle range, the paper factors in the number field of a root θ: αᵏβ is a fixed multiple of n − θ. The coordinates of α and β fill two boxes. Cut into small paired boxes, a determinant forces the points in each pair onto a polynomial equation, and a uniform counting theorem finishes the count. Quartics need extra geometry, on surfaces and curves where the counts are tight. The worst case is a polynomial of degree 5 with four prescribed critical values. Up to a change of variable, only finitely many such polynomials exist, so their values form one small set, at most about X^(1/5), which is removed once.

4:23Where it stands

This is a preprint, not peer reviewed. The result gives the density, with no stated rate of convergence, and no uniformity is claimed as the polynomial varies. Two other recent preprints claim n⁴ + 1, one of them also n⁴ + 2; this paper uses neither. 3, 18, 83. About three in four are squarefree, just as the primes predict. If the proof holds, that’s now a theorem.
