Walk to infinity?

The Gaussian moat conjecture

The claim, in plain words

Gaussian primes are the Gaussian integers a + bi that cannot be factored. Off the axes they are exactly those with a² + b² prime; on the axes they are ±p and ±pi for primes p ≡ 3 (mod 4). In 1962 Basil Gordon asked whether one can walk to infinity on Gaussian primes with steps of bounded length (Erdős problem #952).

Result 028 (29 pages) claims the Gaussian moat conjecture in a strong form: for every step length D there is a finite B(D) such that every connected cluster of Gaussian primes, joining primes at distance at most D, has at most B(D) primes, wherever it is and including the axes. So every walk through distinct Gaussian primes with steps at most D has at most B(D) terms.

What it doesn’t claim

The bound B(D) is not explicit. The proof gives no estimate of how far a walk from the origin can get, so it does not replace the computer searches for the width of the moat around the origin.

What was known

  • Gethner and Stark trace the question to Basil Gordon at the 1962 International Congress of Mathematicians; Erdős credited Gordon and Motzkin. Jordan and Rabung ran early computer searches (1970).
  • Gethner and Stark (1997) formulated a bound uniform in the starting point, built periodic obstructions for steps √2 and 2, and proposed sieving by small Gaussian primes for larger steps. Vardi (1998) studied the periodic coprimality graphs and the passage to a uniform bound.
  • Gethner, Wagon and Wick (1998) showed that prime-free disks can be as large as you like, and found a moat of width √26 around the origin. Tsuchimura (2005) showed that steps up to √32 reach 2,106,442 + 1,879,505i, about 2.8 million from the origin, and that steps up to 6 stay within about 80 million.

How the proof goes

  1. A periodic sieve. For primes p ≡ 1 (mod 4), split p into two conjugate Gaussian primes and remove all their multiples. Every Gaussian prime survives except the chosen factors and their associates, and the surviving set repeats with period Q, the product of the chosen primes.
  2. Periodicity gives uniformity. If the survivors have no infinite walk with steps at most D, a cluster cannot contain two points a period apart, so every cluster has at most Q² points; adding back the removed factors gives a bound for all Gaussian primes (also in Vardi).
  3. Avoidance costs information. Suppose an infinite walk avoids the removed points for a long list of prime batches. Sampling differences along the walk and choosing one Gaussian factor of each prime at random, the paper shows that, for each batch of primes near T, each step must reveal on average at least c/log T about the walk’s residues modulo those primes.
  4. The budget. A step has at most KD possible values, so it carries at most log KD. With batches at geometrically growing sizes, the costs add up like the harmonic series, so enough batches exceed the budget. An entropy telescope, related to Tao’s entropy-decrement argument, makes the costs add.

The numbers in the video

  • A short Python script checks the primality rule by brute-force division in the Gaussian integers, and follows every walk from the origin (added as a starting point). Over the whole plane, steps up to √2 reach 100 primes, farthest 11 + 4i; up to 2, 720 primes, farthest 42 + 17i; up to √8, 2,996, farthest 84 + 41i; up to √10, 249,508, farthest 976 + 311i.
  • Tsuchimura’s count of about 1.04 × 1011 primes for steps up to √32 is for one eighth of the plane; the whole cluster has about 8 × 1011.
  • The toy sieve removes the multiples of 2 ± i and 3 ± 2i (the factors of 5 and 13). The survivors have density (4/5)²(12/13)² ≈ 0.545 and period 65; with unit steps they form finite islands, the largest of 580 points.

0:00Gaussian primes

Gaussian integers are the points a + bi of the square grid. Some of them factor: five is 2 + i, times 2 − i. The ones that don’t are the Gaussian primes. Off the axes, a + bi is prime exactly when a² + b² is an ordinary prime. On the axes, the primes are ordinary primes that leave remainder three when divided by four, like three, seven and eleven, and their turns by i. In 1962, Basil Gordon asked: can you walk to infinity, stepping only on Gaussian primes, with steps of bounded length? Along a line, no: there are arbitrarily long gaps between primes. But in the plane, a walk can go around a gap. The Gaussian moat conjecture says the answer is still no: for every step length, some moat stops you.

0:49Moats

Computers can follow every walk from the origin. With steps up to √2, you never get farther than about 12: the reachable primes are walled in. Steps up to two reach 42 + 17i, about 45 away. √8 reaches about 93. √10 reaches about 1,000, through a quarter of a million primes. In 2005, Tsuchimura went much further. With steps up to √32, the walk reaches about 2.8 million from the origin, through about 800 billion primes. With steps up to six, it’s walled in within 80 million. But a computation only surrounds one starting region. Gethner, Wagon and Wick showed that prime-free disks can be as large as you like. But a walk can go around a disk. Gethner and Stark built periodic walls for the smallest steps, and suggested sieving by small primes for larger ones.

1:45The claim

Result 028 in OpenAI’s math repository claims to settle the question. The preprint, 29 pages, dated September 26, claims: for every step length D, there’s a number B(D) such that every cluster of Gaussian primes joined by steps of length at most D has at most B(D) primes, wherever it is, including on the axes. So every such walk that never revisits a prime ends after at most B(D) primes. B(D) isn’t computed. OpenAI also published a Lean proof: 41 files and about 12,700 lines, not counting Mathlib. We haven’t run the check.

2:24How the proof goes

The proof starts with a sieve. Take ordinary primes that leave remainder one when divided by four, like five and thirteen. Each splits into two Gaussian primes: five into 2 + i and 2 − i. Remove every multiple of these factors. Every Gaussian prime survives, except those factors and their turns by i, and the survivors repeat with period Q, the product of the chosen primes. Suppose the survivors have no infinite walk. Then a cluster can’t contain two points a period apart: shifting by the period would map the finite cluster into itself, which is impossible. So each cluster has at most Q² points, wherever it starts. Here’s a toy case, with steps of length one: after sieving by the factors of five and thirteen, the survivors break into islands, the largest with 580 points. The hard part is showing that enough primes trap every walk. Suppose an infinite walk with steps at most D avoids every removed point. The paper shows that dodging costs information: for each batch of primes near a size T, each step must reveal, on average, at least c/log T about where the walk sits modulo those primes. But each step is one of a bounded number of moves, so it carries at most a fixed amount of information. Over batches at sizes that grow geometrically, the costs add up like the harmonic series, without bound. So enough batches exceed the budget, and no such walk exists. The tools include sampling differences along the walk, picking one Gaussian factor of each prime at random, and an entropy telescope related to Tao’s entropy-decrement argument.

4:02Where it stands

This is a preprint, not peer reviewed. OpenAI’s Lean proof covers both the cluster bound and the walk statement, and we haven’t run it. The bound isn’t explicit, so it doesn’t say how far a walk can actually get. If the proof holds, there’s no walk to infinity on Gaussian primes with bounded steps: every cluster is finite, with a size limit that depends only on the step length.
