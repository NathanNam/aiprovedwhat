Taxicab maps extend?

An AI preprint claims metric Markov cotype two for ℓ₁, settling Lipschitz extension from Hilbert space into ℓ₁

The claim, in plain words

Given a Lipschitz map on part of a space, can you extend it to the whole space without stretching distances much more? Into the real line, always. From Hilbert space into Hilbert space, also, with no loss. But into ℓ₁, distance measured taxicab style, nobody knew whether the loss could always be kept bounded. Extending one coordinate at a time can lose a lot: on a simplex of 64 points our computer finds a loss of 32, growing with the number of points.

In 1992 Ball linked extension to how random walks behave in a space. Later work by Mendel and Naor showed that a metric version of Ball’s property, called metric Markov cotype two, for the target, would give extension from Hilbert space with a universal loss. In 2013 they asked whether ℓ₁ has it, noting that a yes would settle a long-standing question attributed to Ball. As late as 2022, a research paper by Naor and Young called the ℓ₁ case a major open problem.

Result 332 is one OpenAI paper, 9 pages, dated October 5 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims that ℓ₁ has metric Markov cotype two, with constant squared at most 3,024. Combined with Mendel and Naor’s theorem, every Lipschitz map from any subset of a Hilbert space into ℓ₁ extends to the whole space, losing at most a universal factor.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: an explicit extension constant, optimal constants, every subspace of ℓ₁, or Ball’s original linear version of Markov cotype, which ℓ₁ still fails. There is no Lean formalization for this item.

How the proof goes

  1. Smooth the points with an S-curve. Metric Markov cotype lets you move the points: the cost of moving them, plus the time times their leftover one-step jitter, must stay within a fixed multiple of how far the walk spreads the original data. The paper first writes ℓ₁ distances through cuts, so each point becomes a 0-1 vector. It then averages along a walk stopped at a random time, and rounds each coordinate with the S-curve 3r² − 2r³. That curve is the chance that at least two of three coins land heads when each lands heads with chance r, so each new point is the expected median of three independent endpoints of the walk. Our computer confirms this median formula exactly on a test example.
  2. A martingale pays for both. A martingale and a fourth-power potential then pay for both the move and the remaining jitter at a factor of 108; swapping the random stopping time for a fixed time costs another 28. The S-curve’s flatness at 0 and 1 is essential to this argument: our computer finds the key ratio stays below 18 with it and grows without bound without it. On 239 random examples the whole inequality holds with ratio at most 1.21, while leaving the points unmoved reaches 135. A similar S-curve trick appeared four weeks earlier, for a different question, in a paper by Cheng, Wang and Xiang.
