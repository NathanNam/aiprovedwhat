What does a random landscape look like?

The claim, in plain words

Put a height on every point of a triangular lattice, with one rule: neighbouring heights differ by at most one. Think of a terrain of terraces whose cliffs are never more than one step high, and pick such a terrain at random. What does it look like from far away? Its contour lines form loops, which ties it to the loop O(2) model of statistical physics. In 2006 Oded Schramm asked two questions about such height models: one with odd integer heights, steps of 0 or 2, and boundary +1 on one arc and −1 on the other; one with real heights, steps at most 1, and boundary ±λ. For each he asked whether the height converges to the Gaussian free field, and whether the interface between positive and negative converges to SLE4 (for the real heights, at some value of λ). Before this release the variance of the integer heights was known to grow like the logarithm of the size (Glazman, Manolescu, Lammers and others), and the Gaussian limit was a conjecture.

Result 232 is three OpenAI preprints, 261 pages in their second editions, dated October 6, 2026 (first editions September 25). The first claims that integer heights with zero boundary, weighted by x to the number of height changes, converge to σ(x) times the Gaussian free field, for every fixed x from 1/√2 to 1. The second claims that Schramm’s two-arc odd model converges to its harmonic boundary profile plus a multiple of the free field. The third claims that uniform real heights converge to a Gaussian field for every boundary amplitude up to 1/2, and that at one tuned amplitude, defined only implicitly, the interface converges to SLE4. That would resolve Schramm’s real-height question.

What it doesn’t claim

For the integer two-arc model, only the field half is claimed; whether its interface tends to SLE4 stays open. None of the scaling constants is computed, and the papers do not claim that σ(x) is the same for every x. Nothing is said about the real-height interface at other amplitudes.

How the proof goes

  1. Integer heights. The height, modulo four, is encoded by two ±1 spins, and a suitable circuit, given what it fixes, splits the system exactly into conditionally independent inside and outside. Reflection positivity makes the correlations a positive mixture of simple kernels, and the six-fold symmetry of the lattice forces one angular term to vanish on average, so every limit has the free field’s spectrum and the moments satisfy Wick’s rule.
  2. Real heights. A reflected Brownian heat bath samples the uniform law; two copies with the same noise pull together at the walls. A cell problem defines a stiffness, which fixes the field. For the interface, the paper adapts Schramm and Sheffield’s method for the discrete free field: the explored curve carries boundary values ± a gap, and matching them to the field’s scale selects the one amplitude where the curve is plain SLE4.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours, exact: Schramm’s two-arc model on hexagons of radius 1 to 4 has 2, 176, 1,301,984 and 836,798,460,630 configurations; the variance at the centre is 1.00, 1.36, 1.50, 1.62.
  • Ours, heat-bath simulation up to radius 32: the centre variance grows in line with the log of the size. Integer slopes 0.094, 0.104 and 0.111 sit near 1/π² ≈ 0.101 (one universal constant would fit; the papers do not claim it). For real heights the slope suggests a tuned amplitude of roughly 0.3 to 0.4, inside the claimed range.
