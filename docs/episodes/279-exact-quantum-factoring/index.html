An AI preprint claims exact quantum factoring over one fixed, finite gate set

The claim, in plain words

Shor’s algorithm factors integers on a quantum computer in polynomial time, but each run is a gamble: for 21, one run finds the hidden period with probability about 31%. Repeating drives the chance of failure down, but never to zero. Can factoring be done exactly, with probability one, in guaranteed polynomial time? Brassard and Høyer posed this in 1997, and in 2003 Mosca and Zalka called it a challenge that remains. Mosca had sketched a way in 2002, but with gates whose rotation angles are computed during the run. What stayed open was one fixed, finite set of gates. That restriction bites: Hadamard and S alone make only 24 distinct rotations, and these gates offer only 8th roots of unity as phases, so Shor’s fine Fourier phases can be approximated but never made exact.

Result 279 is one OpenAI preprint, 24 pages, dated September 25, 2026. It claims a uniform family of quantum circuits, built only from NOT, CNOT, Toffoli, Hadamard and S gates and their controlled versions, that outputs the complete prime factorization of every integer from 2 up with probability exactly one, with gate and qubit counts polynomial in the input length, and no measurement until the end.

What it doesn’t claim

Practical speed, as the paper says plainly; noise or fault tolerance; or any classical algorithm. At the smallest size the paper allows, our rough count is about 2137 gates, against about 221 for textbook Shor. “Probability one” is for an ideal, noiseless circuit.

The theorem, constructing the circuit (docs)

Last checked October 30, 2026. The preprint has not been revised. The theorem is formalized in Lean, about 54,000 lines that construct the circuit itself; our scan finds no unproved steps, but we did not rebuild it. OSVerify has not reviewed it, and we found no comment from specialists.

How the proof goes

  1. Throw luck away. One step of amplitude amplification turns a success probability of exactly 1/4 into exactly 1; with plain sign flips, 3/4 or 1/2 never get there. So every stage is made exactly predictable, and a final top-up sets the total at exactly 1/4.
  2. Exact odds, by bookkeeping. The Fourier transform is replaced by one that runs straight between 1, i, −1 and −i, so every probability is a power-of-two fraction. The order trial is deliberately diluted with coin flips so it succeeds with a small probability fixed by an exact formula (exactly 1 in 4,096 in our toy for 21). The run also factors p − 1 for each prime it finds, computes each step’s exact odds afterwards, and filters them all to the same rate.
