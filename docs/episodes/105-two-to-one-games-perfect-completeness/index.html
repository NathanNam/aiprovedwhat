The claim, in plain words

A projection game labels the vertices of a bipartite graph: left vertices from a larger alphabet, right vertices from a smaller one. Each edge carries a map from left labels to right labels and is satisfied when the right label is the image of the left label; the value is the largest fraction of edges one labelling satisfies. In a 2-to-1 game the left alphabet is twice the right, and under every map each right label has exactly two preimages.

Projection games are where most hardness-of-approximation proofs begin. The PCP theorem and Raz’s parallel repetition make it NP-hard to tell games of value 1 from games of tiny value, but only with many preimages per label. When every map is one-to-one (unique games), a satisfiable game is easy: fix one label and follow the constraints. In 2002 Khot conjectured that 2-to-1 games stay hard with perfect completeness: telling value 1 from value at most δ is NP-hard for every δ. Austrin, O’Donnell, Tan and Wright proved perfect completeness against soundness about 23/24; the 2-to-2 theorem of Khot, Minzer and Safra (2018, with Dinur and Kindler) gave arbitrarily small soundness with completeness only near 1; Fei, Minzer and Wang proved perfect completeness for 4-to-1 games. Repeating a 2-to-1 game k times gives 2k preimages, so the usual amplification does not help.

Result 105 is one OpenAI preprint (53 pages) dated September 23, 2026. For every fixed rational δ in (0, 1) it gives an alphabet size q and a deterministic polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to 2-to-1 games over alphabets of 2q and q labels: satisfiable formulas give value exactly 1, unsatisfiable ones value at most δ. The output is an explicit unweighted list of edges, and every map has exactly two preimages for each right label.

What it implies, and what it doesn’t

Guruswami and Sinop’s conditional bound, through their reduction, becomes unconditional: for every large enough k it is NP-hard to tell k-colorable graphs from graphs where every k-coloring properly colors at most 1 − 1/k + O((log k)/k²) of the edges, close to what a random coloring achieves. It also recovers known results: Håstad’s hardness for satisfiable Not-Two constraints and the hardness of telling 3-colorable graphs from graphs that are not c-colorable. The alphabet size and running time depend on δ through a long chain of constants; this is a hardness statement, not a practical construction.

How the proof goes

  1. The source game. One player receives a clause and answers with a satisfying assignment of its variables; the other receives a variable and answers with a bit. A PCP theorem with perfect completeness and parallel repetition make its value tiny for unsatisfiable formulas, while a satisfying assignment wins every round.
  2. A tree of function spaces. Independent copies of the questions sit at the leaves of a finite tree. Each leaf carries all binary functions of its answers, and each node above carries the span of products of two functions from the same child.
  3. Questions are partitions. A question shows a list of functions at every node; together they cut the space of all leaf answers into parts by their joint values, and a legal answer is one nonempty part, so a satisfying assignment answers every question consistently. Deleting one function merges each part with at most one other: that gives the 2-to-1 maps, and value exactly 1 for satisfiable formulas.
  4. Soundness. If a labelling satisfies a fixed fraction of the edges, an inverse theorem for the shortcode, derived from Khot, Minzer and Safra’s Grassmann expansion, finds a linear form z whose matrix z(stsu) on the displayed functions has rank at most one, as it does for evaluation at a point. Further steps show that low-rank forms carry part of the success; they pick short odd lists of answers at each leaf, decoders read off answers for the source game, and parallel repetition rules that out. Finally every map is completed to exactly 2-to-1 and the weights are replaced by an explicit edge list.

The numbers in the video

  • A 2-to-1 game with labels 1–4 on the left and 1–2 on the right, three vertices on each side and seven edges, built around a planted labelling: value exactly 1 by brute force.
  • Following constraints along a path from one fixed label leaves one candidate in a unique game, and 2m−1 in a 2-to-1 game with m left vertices: 549,755,813,888 for 40.
  • Repeating a 2-to-1 map k = 1, 2, 3, 4 times gives 2, 4, 8, 16 preimages.
  • Four random binary functions cut 64 points into 16 parts; deleting one leaves 8, each the union of 2.
  • For 8 functions on 32 points, evaluation at a point always gave a matrix of rank at most 1; random forms gave median rank 7 (200 trials).

5:35Where it stands

The preprint is not peer reviewed and has not been revised. The Lean formalization covers the main theorem and proves its own inputs, with a level inequality in place of Grassmann expansion; we have not run it. It is not among the papers OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. The alphabet size and running time depend on delta through a long chain of constants; this is a hardness statement, not a practical construction. If it holds, 2-to-1 games are hard even when one labelling satisfies every edge, and Khot’s conjecture from 2002 is a theorem.
