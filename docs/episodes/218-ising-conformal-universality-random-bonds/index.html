Same curve, new rules?

Six preprints claim the critical Ising picture survives weak extra interactions and weak random bonds

The claim, in plain words

The Ising model puts a spin, up or down, on every square of a grid, and neighbours prefer to agree. At one special temperature it is critical, and since work by Smirnov and collaborators between 2010 and 2015 its large-scale picture is known exactly: spin and energy correlations have explicit conformally covariant limits, and the boundary between plus and minus regions becomes a random fractal curve, SLE3. But all of that rests on exact solvability, which only special nearest-neighbour models have.

Physics expects universality: add a weak extra interaction, or make the bonds slightly random, and the critical point moves but the large-scale picture stays. Our exact transfer-matrix computation shows it: a weak diagonal coupling moves the critical temperature by up to 14%, yet the spin and energy scaling dimensions stay within about 0.2% of 1/8 and 1. Random bonds are subtler: in two dimensions they are marginal, so physicists predicted logarithmic corrections.

Result 218 bundles six OpenAI preprints, 329 pages, dated September 23 and October 5, 2026. For weak, even, finite-range, square-symmetric extra interactions, they claim the critical bulk spin and energy correlations converge to the exact nearest-neighbour limits, up to two unknown constants, and that the plus–minus interface converges to SLE3. For weak independent random bonds of any bounded, mean-zero law, they claim the critical interface converges to the same SLE3, in probability over the environment. A sixth paper, conditional on the bulk paper’s estimates, recovers the predicted log correction for fair plus–minus bonds: E[C²]/E[C]² grows like (log r)1/4.

What it doesn’t claim

Any explicit size for the allowed perturbations; other lattices; strong disorder; correlation limits for random bonds. The correlation paper and the interface paper each construct their own critical temperature, and they are not shown to be the same. Energy correlations alone were proved earlier, by Giuliani, Greenblatt and others.

How the proof goes

  1. A renormalization group in spins. Zoom out, replace every small interaction by its average over the exact critical model given the boundary, and check that it shrinks. Square symmetry cancels one dangerous term, so leftovers shrink like the cube of the scale ratio, which beats the two powers lost by summing over a block.
  2. The critical point. Only the temperature direction grows; choosing the temperature on the stable manifold fixes the critical point. For curves, a four-arm exponent of 21/8, greater than two, keeps rare changes from bending the interface.
  3. Random bonds. The disorder’s variance is marginal: it shrinks only like one over the log of the scale, which is enough for the curve to converge. For fair two-valued bonds, duality pins the critical point exactly; two papers’ formulas agree to 12 digits in our check.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours, exact transfer matrices up to circumference 14: critical β 0.4407 (pure), 0.3866 and 0.5144 (diagonal ±0.1), 0.4194 and 0.4650 (four-spin ±0.1), with ξ/L amplitudes within 0.2% of 4/π and 1/(2π).

2:15How the proof goes

The engine is a renormalization group written in the spins themselves, a different route from the earlier fermion methods. Zoom out by a factor, replace every small interaction by its average over the exact critical model, conditioned on the boundary, and check that it shrinks. Square symmetry cancels one dangerous term, so leftovers shrink like the cube of the scale ratio, which beats the two powers lost by summing over a block. Only the temperature direction grows, and choosing the temperature on the stable manifold fixes the critical point. For curves, a four-arm exponent of 21/8, greater than two, keeps rare changes from bending the interface. With random bonds, the disorder’s variance is marginal: it shrinks only like one over the log of the scale, slowly, but enough for the curve to converge. For fair two-valued bonds, duality pins the critical point exactly, and our check shows two papers’ formulas agree to 12 digits. One small inequality, from the toolkit paper, is formalized in Lean; we also tested it on 3,000 random graphs, with no violations.

All six preprints are unrefereed and have not been revised. Only that toolkit inequality is formalized in Lean: about 3,000 lines, with no unproved steps; none of the universality, interface or random-bond theorems is.
