Only after stretching?

A preprint claims tensor saturation for every even spin group: if an invariant appears after stretching the labels, it was there all along

The claim, in plain words

The irreducible representations of a Lie group are labelled by highest weights: points of a lattice inside a cone. Take three of them and ask whether their tensor product contains an invariant, a nonzero vector that the whole group leaves fixed. Stretching all three labels by the same whole number N keeps an invariant that was already there; saturation asks for the converse: if an invariant appears after stretching, was it there all along?

There is a parity test: the three labels must add up to a point of the root lattice. Even then saturation can fail. Our example: for the rotations of 5-dimensional space, three copies of the 5-dimensional representation pass the test, yet no invariant combines three vectors into a number; double the labels and an invariant appears. Knutson and Tao proved saturation for the unitary groups in 1999, and Kapovich and Millson conjectured it for all simply laced groups, those whose roots all have the same length. For the even spin groups it was known only up to Spin(12) (Spin(8) by Kapovich, Kumar and Millson; Spin(10) and Spin(12) by Kiers; Spin(4) and Spin(6) via the unitary case).

Result 204 is one OpenAI preprint, Tensor saturation for even spin groups (46 pages, dated September 24, 2026). Its Theorem 1.1: for Spin(2n), the double cover of the rotation group in 2n dimensions, with n at least 2, and dominant weights λ, μ, ν whose sum lies in the root lattice, an invariant at (Nλ, Nμ, Nν) for some positive whole number N gives one at (λ, μ, ν). The spin representations, with half-integer labels, are included. In Kapovich and Millson’s conjecture, this is the type D part.

What it doesn’t claim

The exceptional groups E6, E7 and E8, the remaining simply laced cases; any formula for how many invariants there are; stretching by fractions, which the paper shows would break the statement (Remark 6.6). Our scans agree with the theorem: among 76,615 small triples for Spin(6) through Spin(16), none fails at the stretches we tried, while the odd rotation and symplectic groups show 226 failures, all at a stretch of two. These ranks are small, and most were already known.

How the proof goes

  1. Forget the integers. An invariant at stretch N gives three skew-symmetric matrices, with eigenvalues set by the stretched labels, that add up to zero (Lemma 2.5). Divide by N, and the stretch disappears.
  2. A real path. From this real solution the paper builds a schedule: start at one label, follow a polygon shaped like the second, reflect in mirror hyperplanes at certain moments, and end at a mirror image of the third (Definition 2.1, Proposition 2.2). Choosing the path of largest area makes the mirror directions independent.
  3. Earn the integers back. The jump at each reflection should be a whole number, but in type D it can be a half. The bad case is a parity defect, exactly two odd pieces of a signed graph (Lemma 3.3); the paper repairs it by sliding along half of one linear relation while keeping the reflections in order (Lemma 3.5, Figure 1, Proposition 3.6 and Section 4, about 12 pages).
  4. Build the invariant. An integral schedule is turned into an actual invariant, one fundamental column at a time (Proposition 5.1). Labels that all end in two or more zeros pass through a smaller spin group at double the stretch (Proposition 6.1), which the paper says introduces no saturation factor.
