Square the staircase: all?

Two preprints claim Saxl’s conjecture, that the square of every staircase representation of a symmetric group contains every irreducible, and its general form for every symmetric group except three small ones, with Lean formalizations of both

The claim, in plain words

Shuffle n objects in every possible way: that is the symmetric group. Its irreducible representations are labelled by Young diagrams with n boxes; for six objects there are 11. Multiply one of them by itself, a tensor square, and it breaks into irreducible pieces, counted by Kronecker coefficients. For the staircase with rows 3, 2 and 1, of dimension 16, our computer finds all 11 irreducibles in the square, with multiplicities from 1 to 5.

In 2012 Jan Saxl guessed that this always happens for staircases. Pak, Panova and Vallejo conjectured more broadly that every symmetric group, except for 2, 4 and 9 objects, has some representation whose square contains everything. Those exceptions are real: for 2 objects no diagram is symmetric, and only symmetric diagrams can work; for 4, the only symmetric diagram’s square misses two irreducibles; for 9, the best candidate misses only the 3-by-3 square. As the papers tell it, many families were known, and the staircase’s cube was known to contain everything, but not its square.

Result 205 bundles two OpenAI preprints, both dated September 24, 2026. A Cyclic Polytabloid Proof of Saxl’s Conjecture (17 pages, no computer) claims Saxl’s conjecture: for every staircase, the tensor square contains every irreducible representation. Universal Tensor Squares for Symmetric Groups (37 pages) claims the broader conjecture: for every number of objects except 2, 4 and 9, one fixed diagram whose square contains everything.

What it doesn’t claim

Any formula for the Kronecker coefficients: only that they are positive. Nor is the diagram unique: in our census up to 28 objects, 7 diagrams work for 21 objects and 14 for 28. Away from the staircase sizes, the second paper adds cells symmetrically to a staircase (for odd n from 45 and even n from 66) and uses a computer search up to 64 objects.

An earlier claimed proof of Saxl’s conjecture, posted to arXiv in December 2025, was withdrawn by its author.

How the proof goes

  1. One vector. The first paper proves something stronger: fill the staircase with positions; in one copy alternate along the rows, in the other along the columns. Their product is a single vector in the square, and every irreducible appears among its shuffles (Theorem 3.1).
  2. Two halves. Shapes below the staircase in the dominance order come from an explicit map onto a permutation module (Proposition 3.2). For the rest, the paper peels a band of width one or two off the triangle, so that the smaller triangle and the band split apart (Proposition 4.2).
  3. Two-by-two matrices. The band becomes a product of 2×2 matrices; in one example the product is 12 times a matrix whose corner is zero, and a change of basis turns that corner into 12 (Example 5.4). So every shape with at most four rows appears in the band (Proposition 5.3), and induction, removing at most four horizontal strips, finishes the proof (Lemma 6.1).
  4. Every degree. The second paper reuses the one-vector statement, adds two tests and a counting argument, partly checked by computer for middle sizes, and exact character arithmetic up to 64 objects.

The numbers in the video

  • Exact Kronecker coefficients (ours) from Murnaghan–Nakayama character tables: staircases up to 28 objects contain every irreducible; the staircase (3,2,1) squared has multiplicities 1, 2, 3, 4, 2, 5, 4, 2, 3, 2, 1.
  • Census (ours): for every n up to 28, every symmetric diagram tested against every irreducible; none works only at 2, 4 and 9; 1, 2, 3, 7 and 14 diagrams work at 6, 10, 15, 21 and 28 objects.
  • Example 5.4 of the first paper reproduced (ours). The Lean line counts are ours.
