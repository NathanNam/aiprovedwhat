Yes or no, undecidable?

An AI preprint claims the separable quotient problem is independent of ZFC, relative to a measurable cardinal

The claim, in plain words

Every infinite-dimensional Banach space contains a separable infinite-dimensional subspace: just take the closed span of a sequence. Quotients are another matter. A quotient squashes a space along a subspace, and to be separable it has to forget almost everything while staying infinite-dimensional. So: does every infinite-dimensional Banach space have a separable infinite-dimensional quotient?

The question is traditionally attributed to Banach and Mazur, and its earliest known explicit statement is Rosenthal’s, in 1969. Many classes were settled: spaces containing c₀, reflexive spaces, and, in 2008, every dual space. Small spaces were settled too, up to a set-theoretic threshold called the bounding number, which the axioms do not fix. But in 1972 Johnson and Rosenthal wrote that it is an open question, and papers still called it open at the end of September 2026.

Result 323 is one OpenAI paper, 39 pages, dated September 23 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims the standard axioms of set theory, ZFC, cannot decide the question, assuming a measurable cardinal is consistent. Under the continuum hypothesis, there is a counterexample. If instead the continuum carries a measure on all of its subsets that gives single points zero and is extremely additive, every such space has a separable quotient. Both hold over the real and the complex numbers.

What it doesn’t claim

The two sides cost different amounts. The counterexample world needs only that ZFC is consistent; the world where every space has a quotient uses a measurable cardinal, through a theorem of Solovay. Not claimed: an answer in ZFC, or that the large cardinal is really needed. For spaces of the smallest nonseparable size, ℵ₁, it was already known that a yes answer is consistent, with no large cardinal; the new part there is the counterexample.

How the proof goes

  1. One test, and a random sample. Both halves use one test: a space has such a quotient exactly when some infinite-dimensional piece of its dual sees the space separably. For the positive half, suppose a counterexample exists. The measure on the continuum lets the proof pick functionals at random, one from each of a sequence of rows of nearly orthogonal vectors; the rows have 16, 256, then 4,096 vectors, so the average contribution shrinks fast. That shows the space looks huge through every separable piece of its dual. Almost surely the next round of random picks forms an unconditional sequence, which a result going back to Hagler and Johnson turns into a separable quotient: a contradiction.
  2. A tree of paths in every subspace. The negative half is 23 pages. It builds a dual space on the first uncountable ordinal, with Gowers–Maurey style paths whose weights code their whole history. Inside every infinite-dimensional subspace, the proof finds vectors that tell a binary tree of paths apart by a fixed amount. Under the continuum hypothesis there are only ℵ₁ paths, so each gets its own coordinate, and a small tilt of the predual makes every infinite-dimensional separable piece of the dual see too much. Our computer checks the coding on a finite tree: in 44,850 pairs of branches, shared weights stop at the fork.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: quotienting ℓ∞³ by one coordinate line gives ℓ∞², exactly.
  • The paper’s rows have 16ⁿ vectors (16, 256, 4,096) and average bound 2/√N (1/2, 1/8, 1/32); our script reproduces the bound.
  • Ours: on a finite tree of depth 12, in 44,850 sampled pairs of branches, shared weights stop at the fork.
  • Lean: 51 files and 14,178 lines in the CH half’s import closure (counted by us; not run by us).
