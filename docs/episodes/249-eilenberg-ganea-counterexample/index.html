Dimension two, or three?

An AI preprint claims a counterexample to the Eilenberg–Ganea conjecture

The claim, in plain words

A classifying space for a group is a space whose loops form the group and whose universal cover is contractible: a circle for the integers, a torus for pairs of integers. The smallest possible dimension of one is the group’s geometric dimension. Algebra gives a second number, the cohomological dimension: the length of the shortest projective resolution of the integers over the group ring, which never exceeds the first. In 1957 Eilenberg and Ganea showed the two agree from three up, and Stallings and Swan settled dimension one. That left dimension two: does every group of cohomological dimension two have a two-dimensional classifying space? In 1997 Bestvina and Brady built candidates, and showed that for those built from the Poincaré homology sphere, at least one fails: this conjecture, or Whitehead’s asphericity conjecture.

Result 249 is one OpenAI preprint, 25 pages, dated September 23, 2026. It claims a finitely generated, residually finite group of cohomological dimension two and geometric dimension three, which would disprove the Eilenberg–Ganea conjecture: the group has no two-dimensional classifying space, however many cells one allows. The group is a Bestvina–Brady group built from a flag triangulation of the complex with two loops, x and y, and two discs for x² = y⁵ and x² = (xy⁻¹)³.

What it doesn’t claim

A finitely presented example (this group is not finitely presented); anything about Whitehead’s conjecture, which stays open; or the versions with families of subgroups, which were already known to fail.

Last checked October 19, 2026. The preprint has not been revised. In Lean, the theorem is formalized for this exact complex: about 34,000 lines with no unproved steps. We did not build it, and it is missing from the repository’s formalization list. OSVerify has not reviewed the paper. A commenter on Scott Aaronson’s blog said it is the Bestvina–Brady group long thought to be a counterexample. Wikipedia edits mentioning the claim were reverted pending independent sources, and no error has been reported.

How the proof goes

  1. The test. Put a tiny rotation, a unit quaternion near 1, on every edge of the two-dimensional slice the group acts on, so that the product around each triangle is 1. Commuting labels would give 1 around every loop, because the homology vanishes. With quaternions, a loop could still twist.
  2. If a two-dimensional classifying space existed. It could be of any size; the paper turns it into a presentation lined up with the triangles. The first-order error cancels exactly, leaving only commutators. A degree count on products of copies of SU(2), going back to Gerstenhaber and Rothaus, solves the remaining equations. Finite quotients and compactness then force every small enough flat labelling to give 1 around every loop.
  3. But labels that twist exist. A direction map sends each element of the Artin group to a point of the seed complex and changes slowly. Pulling back the 120-element quaternion representation gives tiny labels, flat on every triangle, with a loop that twists. So no two-dimensional classifying space exists, while a three-dimensional one does.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: the seed complex has zero reduced homology, yet its fundamental group has 120 elements. The paper’s map sends them onto 120 unit quaternions, the vertices of the 600-cell, and the complex’s second homotopy group is 119 copies of the integers.
  • Ours: the Lean version’s flag complex has 103 vertices, 372 edges and 270 triangles, zero homology and a fundamental group of order 120.
  • Ours: the direction map’s step bound held in 14,854 random steps and was reached exactly. Signed solution counts of small word maps on SU(2) (3, −2, 3, −4, 2) matched their determinants numerically.

2:25How the proof goes

The test: put a tiny rotation, a unit quaternion near one, on every edge of the 2-dimensional slice the group acts on, so that the product around each triangle is one. With commuting labels, every loop would give one, because the homology vanishes. With quaternions, a loop could still twist. Suppose a 2-dimensional classifying space existed, of any size. The paper turns it into a presentation lined up with the triangles; the first-order error cancels exactly, leaving only commutators. A degree count on products of copies of SU(2), going back to Gerstenhaber and Rothaus, where the degree equals a determinant, solves the remaining equations; our numerical checks gave signed counts 3, −2, 3, −4 and 2, matching. Finite quotients and compactness then force every small enough flat labelling to give one around every loop. But the paper builds small labels that twist. A direction map sends each element of the Artin group to a point of the seed complex, changing by at most 2 divided by the word length per step; in our 14,854 random steps the bound was never exceeded, and it was reached exactly. Pulling back the quaternion representation gives tiny labels, flat on every triangle, with a loop that twists. So no 2-dimensional classifying space exists, while a 3-dimensional one does.

3:52Where it stands

The preprint is unrefereed and unchanged since release. In Lean, the theorem is formalized for this exact complex, about 34,000 lines with no unproved steps; we did not build it, and it is missing from the repository’s formalization list. If it holds, algebra can be fooled: a group that looks 2-dimensional to algebra needs three dimensions to be built, 69 years after Eilenberg and Ganea.
