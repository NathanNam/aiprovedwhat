Rebuild a graph from its cards?

A paper attributed to Google’s Cogentic agent claims Harary’s 1964 edge reconstruction conjecture for every graph with at least 1000 times as many edges as vertices, replacing Müller’s n log n bound from 1977. By Müller’s own bound, which the paper uses as its first step, the theorem is new only for graphs with more than about 10³⁰¹ vertices. Its Lean proof is claimed but not released, and no human check is named.

The question

This is GO05, the fifth of our Specials on papers from Google’s Cogentic. On November 25, 2026, Google Research added six research papers to its public GitHub repository. Each README says that the paper and a Lean formalization were entirely obtained by Cogentic, an agent system built on Gemini 4, with no human interaction other than the initial prompt. The Lean files have not been released. Google is not affiliated with this series. This episode takes the fifth paper in our series, on a graph puzzle from 1964: can you rebuild a network from all its copies with one link missing? The paper claims yes, for every network with at least 1,000 times as many links as dots. So what is the question?

A graph is a set of dots, called vertices, joined by lines, called edges. Delete one edge, then erase all the labels, so only the shape remains. That is a card. Do this once for every edge, and the pile of cards is the edge deck. Here is a triangle with a tail: 4 edges, so 4 cards. A triangle beside a lone dot, two paths of 3 edges, and a star with 3 arms. The question is whether the deck always determines the graph you started from. Not always. A star with 3 arms, and a triangle beside a lone dot, both give the same 3 cards: a path of 2 edges, plus a lone dot. Two separate edges, and a path of 2 edges beside a lone dot, also share a deck. In 1964, Harary conjectured that small cases like these are the only failures: every graph with at least 4 edges is determined by its edge deck. As an illustration, we checked all 1,253 graphs with up to 7 vertices. The only collisions are those two pairs, plus extra lone dots. By Brendan McKay’s account, a computer search has checked the edge version up to 12 vertices.

There is an older sibling. Kelly and Ulam’s reconstruction conjecture deletes vertices instead of edges. It is still open, and has been checked by computer up to 13 vertices. By a 1971 result of Greenwell, a proof of the vertex version would settle the edge version too, while the reverse is not known, and this paper is only about edges. For dense graphs, there is a counting method. In 1972, Lovász proved the edge conjecture for graphs with more than half of all possible edges. In 1977, Müller sharpened the count: with n vertices and m edges, it holds whenever 2^(m−1) exceeds n!, roughly when there are more than log₂ n edges per vertex. Nash-Williams turned the idea into a general lemma. Later work covered special families: bidegreed graphs, claw-free graphs, large Hamiltonian graphs. As the paper tells it, Müller’s n log n stood for nearly five decades as the best density bound for all graphs.

The claim, in plain words

The claim. The paper has 47 pages and no author line; a footnote credits Cogentic. Its main theorem: every finite simple graph on at least 4 vertices, with at least 1,000 times as many edges as vertices, is determined by its edge deck. Simple means no loops and no repeated edges. A corollary: so is every graph in which each vertex has at least 2,000 neighbors. Unconditional, here, means that no extra structure is assumed: no connectivity, no Hamiltonian path, no gaps between degrees. Where Müller needs about log n edges per vertex, this needs a constant: 1,000. The paper notes that below 2,001 vertices the statement is empty, since no simple graph that small has that many edges. What is not claimed: Harary’s conjecture itself, which also covers sparse graphs, with fewer than 1,000 edges per vertex; the vertex version; or any algorithm. The paper even argues, in Remark 1.3, that counting with a spanning tree, as here, cannot reach below about 3.7 edges per vertex. The sparse case needs new ideas.

In the paper’s words (Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2, p. 2): every finite simple undirected graph G₀ on n₀ ≥ 4 vertices with m = |E(G₀)| ≥ 1000 n₀ edges is edge-reconstructible, that is, every simple graph H with the same number of vertices and the same edge deck D_E(H) = D_E(G₀) is isomorphic to G₀; and every finite simple graph with minimum degree δ(G₀) ≥ 2000 is edge-reconstructible. The paper notes that the theorem is vacuous for 4 ≤ n₀ ≤ 2000 and “takes effect for n₀ ≥ 2001”.

How much does it add?

How much does it add? Put the two thresholds side by side, as edges per vertex. Müller’s grows with the logarithm of n: about 20 for a million vertices, 40 for a trillion, and about 266 for 10⁸⁰ vertices, roughly the number of atoms in the observable universe. The new threshold is flat, at 1,000. The two meet only when log₂ n reaches 1,000: at 2¹⁰⁰⁰, a number with 302 digits. Below that, any graph with 1,000 edges per vertex already has more than n log₂ n edges, so Müller’s theorem already covers it. The paper’s own proof makes this its first step: a counterexample would need log₂ n above 1,000. So the new theorem adds only graphs with more than about 10³⁰¹ vertices. That is a real change in the shape of the bound, from n log n to linear, and in this kind of mathematics the shape is the point. But no graph that could ever be stored is newly covered.

How the proof goes

How the proof goes, by the paper’s overview. Suppose two different graphs had the same edge deck. Removing lone dots leaves two connected graphs that share their edge deck, their vertex deck and their degrees, with log₂ n above 1,000. The engine is the counting method of Lovász and Müller. If a graph is not reconstructible, inclusion and exclusion over the deck force many shufflings of its vertices that carry a chosen set of edges back onto edges: at least 2 to the number of other edges, divided by 2. Now fix a spanning tree, a skeleton that reaches every vertex. Counting the shufflings that keep the tree inside the graph gives an upper bound, roughly a product of degrees along the tree. If the lower bound beats the upper bound, that is a contradiction. With high-degree hubs, the product is too large, and the tree count falls back to n log n. The paper’s new tool is a single spanning tree, built greedily in order of degree, that works at every degree scale at once, the scales doubling from about 2^(average degree / 16). With it, the paper shows that a counterexample must carry more than 0.15 m bits of a certain information, in small pieces that hang off hubs and repeat relatively rarely.

Three mechanisms then bound that information: one with polynomials, using classical tools such as Charlier polynomials and Bessel function estimates; one with symmetry groups on vertices that have identical neighbors; and one by random sampling. Together they allow less than 10⁻³⁰ × m bits, far below 0.15 m: a contradiction. We read the theorem, the overview, the preliminaries with Müller’s bound, the counterexample setup in Section 3 and the final proof, and redid the arithmetic in this episode. We did not check Sections 3 to 8 beyond that setup, where nearly all the new work is. One piece of context the paper does not cite: in 1996, Pyber showed that Nash-Williams’ condition alone cannot settle the conjecture, even for graphs with about the square root of log n edges per vertex. By its own description, the new proof also uses the vertex deck and more, so there is no contradiction, but a checker should know it. The README says a complete Lean formalization, from Mathlib alone, has been completed and will be released. Without it, there is nothing for us to read. The Cogentic paper on arXiv describes the system: an orchestrator assigns directions to parallel provers, adversarial verifiers check each draft, and checked lemmas go into a shared ledger. Its earlier results were verified by domain experts, by the authors’ account. For this paper, no human check is named.

In more detail (the paper’s §1.3 and Lemma 2.2): if G is not edge-reconstructible, Möbius inversion over edge subsets gives, for every set E₀ of edges, at least 2^(|E(G) ∖ E₀| − 1)·|Aut G| permutations σ of the vertices with E₀ ⊆ σ⁻¹(E(G)). For a rooted spanning tree T₀, the permutations that carry E(T₀) into E(G) number at most n times a product of falling factorials of degrees, (d_G(τ(u)))_(c(u)), over the vertices u with c(u) children. The new argument uses one lexicographic degree-ordered Kruskal minimum spanning tree across all dyadic degree thresholds Δ_k = 2^k ⌈2^(d̄/16)⌉, shows that a counterexample of average degree d̄ ≥ 2000 concentrates more than 0.15 m bits of “component-multiplicity entropy” on low-multiplicity non-bridge components, and rules this out with three mechanisms (Sections 4–8); Section 9 ends with 0.15 m < 10⁻³⁰ m. Pyber’s 1996 paper “Dense graphs and edge reconstruction” (Combinatorica 16, 521–525), which the paper does not cite, shows by an example that Nash-Williams’ condition by itself cannot settle the conjecture, even for graphs with about n√(½ log n) edges (we follow the zbMATH review; we could not read the paper itself).

New graphs of storable size
None. Müller’s bound (which the paper reproves as Lemma 2.2) shows that a counterexample has fewer than n log₂ n edges; with at least 1000 n edges that forces log₂ n > 1000, as the paper’s own Lemma 3.3(1) says. So for every graph with at most 2¹⁰⁰⁰ vertices (a 302-digit number) Theorem 1.1 already follows from Müller’s theorem; it is new only beyond about 10³⁰¹ vertices (our arithmetic).

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: the triangle with a tail (the “paw”) has edge deck {triangle + dot, path of 3 edges, path of 3 edges, star with 3 arms}; the 4-cycle has four paths of 3 edges. The claw and the triangle plus a lone dot each have three cards “path of 2 edges plus a dot”; two separate edges and a path of 2 edges plus a dot each have two cards “one edge plus two dots”.
  • Ours: a brute-force check over networkx’s atlas of all 1,253 graphs with up to 7 vertices finds exactly 8 pairs of non-isomorphic graphs with the same edge deck: the two pairs above, padded with lone dots, all with 2 or 3 edges. Every graph there with at least 4 edges is determined by its deck (a known fact; per McKay, Stolee checked the edge version to 12 vertices).
  • Ours: edges per vertex that Müller needs, simple form log₂ n (exact form (log₂ n! + 1)/n): 19.93 (18.49) for n = 10⁶, 39.86 (38.42) for n = 10¹², 265.75 (264.31) for n = 10⁸⁰. Lovász’s threshold is (n − 1)/4 edges per vertex.
  • Ours: log₂ n reaches 1000 at n = 2¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 1.07 × 10³⁰¹ (302 digits). With Müller’s exact form 2^(m−1) > n!, m = 1000 n is covered up to log₂ n ≈ 1001.44, n ≈ 10^301.46.
  • The paper’s Remark 1.3, recomputed by us: d̄/2 − 1 = log₂(d̄ − 1) has root d̄ ≈ 7.32 (m ≈ 3.66 n), and d̄/2 − 1 = log₂ d̄ has root d̄ = 8 (m = 4 n).
