Decidable means simple inside?

Three AI preprints claim the Boone–Higman conjecture, with higher finiteness

The claim, in plain words

Give a group by generators and rules. The word problem asks whether a given string of generators equals the identity. For many groups a computer can always tell, but in the 1950s Novikov and Boone built finitely presented groups whose word problem no computer can solve. There is a classical test: if a group sits inside a finitely presented simple group, run two searches at once. One looks for a proof that the word equals 1, the other for a proof that setting it to 1 collapses the simple group. Simplicity guarantees that one of them ends. In 1973 and 1974 William Boone and Graham Higman asked for the converse: does every finitely generated group with decidable word problem embed in a finitely presented simple group? Many families were already known, and since 2023 Belk, Bleak, Matucci, Zaremsky and others have added many more, including hyperbolic groups.

Result 250 is three OpenAI preprints of 35, 30 and 18 pages, all dated September 23, 2026. The first claims the Boone–Higman conjecture: a finitely generated group has decidable word problem exactly when it embeds in a finitely presented simple group. The second claims that the simple group can even be of type F∞, with a classifying space that has finitely many cells in each dimension. The third claims a single group of type F∞ that contains every finitely presented group.

What it doesn’t claim

An explicit or natural embedding: the construction goes through computability. The easy direction is classical. The universal group of the third paper is not simple. It maps onto the integers, and since it contains every finitely presented group, its own word problem cannot be decidable.

The formalization includes Higman’s embedding theorem and the Thompson-group theorems in the forms the papers use. On October 8, Matthew Zaremsky crossed the conjecture off his public list of open problems. No detailed public check has appeared, and no error has been reported.

How the proof goes

  1. The target. A twisted Brin–Thompson group of Belk and Zaremsky cuts and pastes pieces of a Cantor set. It is always simple and contains Thompson’s group V. Zaremsky proved in 2024 that it is also finitely presented when a finitely presented group acts faithfully and 2-transitively with finitely generated stabilizers.
  2. The action. The first paper builds that action by one general method that works for any group with decidable word problem. It makes every element a commutator and builds an algebra over the two-element field from a program that knows its own code, with a simple core. Matrices over the algebra act on vectors, and the paper kills the hidden Steinberg kernel. Simplicity then lets any nonzero vector reach any other.
  3. Type F∞. The second paper adds a combinatorial fact. Two finite families of idempotent matrices, one over the field with two elements and one over the field with three, force a homology class to be killed by both 2 and 3, so it is zero. A theorem of Belk and Zaremsky then finishes the proof.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: for the rotation group of the icosahedron, rewriting gives 7 rules and 60 normal forms. The two searches settled all 8 test words there. In a non-simple group of order 12, 3 of 8 words left both searches stuck.
  • Ours: with 2-by-2 matrices over the two-element field, two-sided row operations put all 255 nonzero vectors in one orbit. One-sided operations split them into four.
  • Ours: 158 idempotents over the two-element field and 680 over the three-element field. The class has order exactly 2 and exactly 3, and its order becomes infinite without the relations linking the copies.
