A perfect fingerprint, proved at last?

Two AI preprints claim that von Neumann algebras of lattices like SL(3,ℤ) remember the group

The claim, in plain words

Every group has a von Neumann algebra, an algebra of operators that records how the group acts on itself. When every element other than the identity has infinitely many conjugates, this algebra is a factor, with a trace that measures the size of its projections continuously. In SL₃(ℤ), the conjugates of a basic matrix keep multiplying (1, 7, 40, 204, 925 as our search radius grows), while −I in SL₂(ℤ) has no conjugate but itself. Around 1980, Connes asked whether such a factor remembers the group, for rigid groups with Kazhdan’s property (T), like SL₃(ℤ). Amenable groups of this kind give no hope: they all produce the same factor. In August 2026, counterexamples among property (T) groups showed that the answer is no in general, one from OpenAI and one by Zhou with AI help. Lattices like SL₃(ℤ) remained open.

Result 286 is two OpenAI preprints, 190 pages, dated September 23, 2026. For such groups, commensurable with lattices in products of simple groups with property (T), over the reals, the complex numbers or the p-adic numbers, the first paper classifies every finite-index bimodule into a twisted group factor of any group with infinite conjugacy classes: each one is built from finite-index subgroup isomorphisms and finite-dimensional projective representations. The second paper deduces that these factors remember everything. If a stretched copy of one twisted group factor equals another, the stretch is one, the groups are isomorphic and the twists agree. So SL₃(ℤ) and its relatives would be W*-superrigid, and their factors would have trivial fundamental group.

What it doesn’t claim

Lattices in groups without property (T), like SL₂(ℤ); fields of positive characteristic; or property (T) groups in general, where the conjecture is already known to fail.

We found no published check.

How the proof goes

  1. Bridges between algebras. Think of bimodules as bridges, each with a left and a right size. The obvious ones come from matching finite-index subgroups, such as the congruence subgroup of level 3, whose index in SL₃(ℤ) is 5,616, decorated with small projective representations.
  2. No other bridges, and whole-number sizes. The hard part, 165 pages, shows there are no other bridges, using measurements at the boundary of symmetric spaces and Bruhat–Tits buildings, rather than Popa’s deformation and rigidity methods. Then a short argument finishes: if a copy stretched by t equals the original, there is a bridge with sizes t and 1/t. Both must be whole numbers, so t is one.
