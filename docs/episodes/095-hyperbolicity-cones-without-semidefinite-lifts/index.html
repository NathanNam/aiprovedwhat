No lift at all?

A hyperbolicity cone that no semidefinite description reaches, even with extra variables

The claims, in plain words

A homogeneous polynomial p is hyperbolic in a direction e if it is nonzero at e and, for every point x, the polynomial t ↦ p(te − x) has only real roots. The points where all those roots are nonnegative form the hyperbolicity cone, which Gårding proved is always convex. For x₀² − x₁² − x₂² with e along the x₀ axis it is the ice-cream cone; for the determinant on symmetric matrices with e the identity it is the cone of positive semidefinite matrices. Güler showed that these cones carry the barrier functions of interior-point methods, so they support optimization beyond semidefinite programming.

A spectrahedron is a slice of the positive semidefinite cone: the points where a matrix depending affinely on x is positive semidefinite. A spectrahedral cone whose matrix is positive definite somewhere is a hyperbolicity cone. The generalized Lax conjecture asked for the converse, that every hyperbolicity cone is a spectrahedron; in three variables this holds, by Helton and Vinnikov with an equivalence of Lewis, Parrilo and Ramana. The weaker projected Lax conjecture of Netzer and Sanyal allows extra variables: every hyperbolicity cone should be a spectrahedral shadow, a projection of a spectrahedron. Netzer and Sanyal proved it when every nonzero boundary point is a smooth point of the polynomial, and Scheiderer, who found other convex sets that are not shadows, asked whether his methods could settle it. The difference already shows in the plane: x⁴ + y⁴ ≤ 1 is a shadow (u ≥ x², v ≥ y², u² + v² ≤ 1) but not a spectrahedron.

Result 095 is three OpenAI preprints. The first (September 24, 2026, 14 pages) writes down a polynomial of degree 20 in 23 variables, det((det X) Z − Φy(adj X)) for 4 × 4 symmetric X and Z and y in ℝ³, built from the Choi–Lam form, and claims it is hyperbolic but its cone is not a spectrahedron in any matrix size; dividing by det X gives degree 16. A companion (October 5, 10 pages) shows that this cone is nonetheless a shadow, exactly the projection of a 100 × 100 matrix inequality with 307 extra variables. The third (October 5, 29 pages) claims a hyperbolic polynomial whose closed cone has no finite semidefinite lift at all, with any number of extra variables and any real coefficients, which disproves the projected Lax conjecture and with it the generalized one.

What it doesn’t claim

The third paper’s polynomial is not written down: it is an existence argument in very high dimension, not a bound on the size of representations. With the paper’s stated choice N > 10 · 36², the degree comes to about 67 billion. The sizes in the first two papers are not claimed minimal, and no explicit or small cone without a lift is given. The first paper also records a counterexample to an intermediate step in an earlier preprint by González Nevado that claimed the conjecture was true.

How the proofs go

  1. A form with no square under it. The Choi–Lam form b(z, y) is nonnegative, but the only bilinear form whose square stays below it is zero: it vanishes at certain pairs and grows like s4 along certain curves. Writing b = zᵀQ(y)z gives a positive map Φy, and the polynomial det((det X) Z − Φy(adj X)). Along each line parallel to e its 20 roots are the eigenvalues of a symmetric 20 × 20 matrix, so they are real.
  2. No slice. Where X is positive definite, membership means Z ⪰ Φy(X⁻¹). Assuming a pencil describes the cone, the first paper rescales it, sends X⁻¹ and Z⁻¹ toward rank-one projections to get an exact identity for kernel projections of its blocks, and differentiates one of them. That produces bilinear forms whose squares fit under b, so they vanish, which would make b zero: a contradiction.
  3. But a shadow. The companion covers y-space by planes through the vertical axis; on each, Q has a Gram factor linear in the plane’s coordinates, giving a 20 × 20 pencil. A linear functional on trigonometric polynomials replaces the angle: testing against 5 basis functions gives one 100 × 100 matrix, and of its 330 coordinates 23 are the original variables, leaving 307 extra.
  4. No lift at all. The third paper allows any quadratic matrix map Q that is positive at every input and reduces the question to the set of (A, t, y) with A ⪰ 0, the range of Q(y) inside that of A, and t ≥ tr(A†Q(y)). It builds Q from moment matrices on quadratic forms in 8 variables, starting from a rank-20 seed whose rank conditions are certified by determinants over the field with 7 elements, then takes more than 12,960 copies and a quadratic form separating one vector from every limit of the moment images. A positive moment functional produces points violating the defining inequality by order ε4, while a finite lift would force feasible points within order ε16.

The numbers in the video

  • The TV screen x⁴ + y⁴ ≤ 1: on 1,440 lines through four interior points, exactly 2 of the 4 roots of 1 − x⁴ − y⁴ are real each time; the three 2 × 2 matrix inequalities match the region at 20,000 random points.
  • The Choi–Lam form: its minors, 11 zeros and the s4 curves are checked exactly with sympy.
  • The explicit polynomial: the identity p(te − x) = det(tI − D) holds at 1,000 random points (relative error below 10−11), exact root counts on 20 integer lines find all 20 roots real, and the slice criterion Z ⪰ Φy(X⁻¹) agrees at 5,000 random points.
  • The companion’s angular Gram factor reproduces Q to within 10−14 at 2,000 random angles.

Precisely, for every point x, the polynomial in t given by p(te − x) has only real roots, and where all of them are nonnegative, x lies in the hyperbolicity cone. Take x₀² − x₁² − x₂², with e pointing along the x₀ axis. The roots are x₀ ± the distance from that axis, and the cone is the ice-cream cone. For a cone the dependence is linear, and when the matrix is positive definite somewhere, its determinant is hyperbolic, so spectrahedral cones are hyperbolicity cones. Is the converse true? The region where x⁴ + y⁴ ≤ 1 is a shadow: add variables u and v with u ≥ x², v ≥ y², and u² + v² ≤ 1, each a 2 × 2 matrix condition. In our check this matches the region at 20,000 random points. But the region is not a spectrahedron. Along a line through an interior point, its quartic boundary polynomial has four roots, and on every such line exactly two are real. Helton and Vinnikov showed that for a spectrahedron, the lowest-degree polynomial vanishing on its boundary has only real roots along every line through an interior point.

4:32How the proofs go

The first paper starts from the Choi–Lam form, a polynomial b in two triples of variables, z and y, quadratic in each. It is never negative, yet the only bilinear form whose square fits under it is zero: b vanishes at certain pairs, and along certain curves it grows only like the fourth power of the parameter. We checked these identities exactly. Writing b as zᵀQ(y)z gives a matrix Q(y) that is positive for every y, and from it a positive map Φ. The polynomial is the determinant of (det X) Z − Φ(adj X). As the later papers show, along every line parallel to e its 20 roots are the eigenvalues of one symmetric 20 × 20 matrix, so they are real. On 20 random integer lines, our exact root count found all 20 real each time. Suppose some matrix pencil described the cone. Where X is positive definite, membership means that Z dominates Φ(X⁻¹). The paper rescales the pencil, pushes X⁻¹ and Z⁻¹ toward rank-one projections, and gets an exact identity for kernel projections of the pencil’s blocks. Differentiating one of them produces bilinear forms whose squares fit under b. So they all vanish, which would make b zero everywhere: a contradiction, so no pencil exists. The companion lifts the same cone. It covers the y-space by planes through the vertical axis; on each, Q has a Gram factor linear in the plane’s coordinates, giving a 20 × 20 pencil. A linear functional on trigonometric polynomials replaces the angle. Tested against 5 basis functions, it gives one matrix of size 20 × 5 = 100, and of its 330 coordinates, 23 are the original variables, leaving 307 extra ones. The third paper keeps the determinant construction but lets Q be any quadratic matrix map that is positive at every input. It reduces the question to one convex set: positive matrices A and numbers t with t at least the trace of A†Q(y). To build Q, it uses moment matrices on quadratic forms in 8 variables, a space of dimension 36, starting from a rank-20 seed matrix, with its rank conditions certified by explicit determinants over the field with 7 elements. More than 12,960 copies, and a quadratic form that separates one vector from every limit of the moment images, give Q. Finally, a positive moment functional produces points that violate the defining inequality by order ε⁴, while any finite lift would force feasible points within order ε¹⁶. That contradiction is the theorem.

7:28Where it stands

The three preprints are not peer reviewed and have not been revised. Lean covers the first paper’s theorem: its degree-20 polynomial is hyperbolic and its cone is not a spectrahedron; we have not run it. The exact lift and the headline result, no lift at all, are checked only on paper. The headline proof is closely related to Scheiderer’s local sum-of-squares obstructions. None of the papers is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, hyperbolic programming truly reaches beyond semidefinite programming: some hyperbolicity cones cannot be described exactly by semidefinite constraints, even with extra variables. No explicit cone without a lift is given, and no small one.
