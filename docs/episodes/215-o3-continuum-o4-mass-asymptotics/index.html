The claim, in plain words

Put a unit vector in n dimensions at every point of a square grid, and let neighbours prefer to point the same way, with strength β, the inverse temperature. In one dimension the memory is short: our exact calculation for three-component spins gives a correlation length of about β. In two dimensions, for n of two or more, Mermin and Wagner showed there is never long-range order, but correlations could still fade only like a power of the distance.

For arrows in the plane, n = 2, that really happens: the Kosterlitz–Thouless phase. For n of three or more, Polyakov argued in 1975 that rotations of a sphere, which do not commute, make correlations decay exponentially at every temperature, however cold. A rigorous proof at every temperature, for small n, has been open for fifty years.

Result 215 bundles five OpenAI preprints, 379 pages, dated September 23 to October 5, 2026. The first, 20 pages, claims that for every n of at least three and every temperature, spin correlations decay exponentially on any finite piece of the grid with free boundary, and in its limits. It does not claim a full transfer gap, or a formula for the correlation length. Four longer papers go further: for n = 4, a full mass gap at every temperature, with bounds of the right order when it is cold; for n = 3, the continuum limit itself, a quantum field theory in one plus one dimensions with a unique vacuum, a positive mass gap, an isolated particle and a nonzero connected four-point function; and for n = 4, the exact leading form of the mass, 32 eπ/4 − 1/2 √β e−πβ. Our algebra shows that constant matches the 1990 prediction of Hasenfratz, Maggiore and Niedermayer, converted to the lattice in the standard way. No value is claimed for the O(3) mass.

What it doesn’t claim

A formula for the correlation length in the short paper; any value of the O(3) mass; asymptotic freedom as a theorem; anything for n = 2.

How the proof goes

  1. Twist a ring. Pin the spins on two square rings and twist the inner ring by a half turn. Because rotations about two different axes produce a rotation about a third, waves at every scale can absorb the twist, and the cost falls at least as fast as 1/log N for a ring of size N.
  2. From twist to decay. An exact identity turns that cost into the chance that a cluster of equal signs crosses the ring. So crossings die out, and a count over coarse paths gives exponential decay. In our simulation the crossing chance falls to zero for three-component spins and stays at one for planar spins when cold.
  3. The longer papers. Three of them use an exact renormalization group: block spins at every scale, with the coupling drifting as predicted. The exact constant comes from an auxiliary model solved by the Bethe ansatz and matched back to the lattice.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: 32 eπ/4 − 1/2 = 42.569, equal to the 1990 prediction m/ΛMS = √(32/(πe)) with the standard lattice conversion; 1D chain correlation lengths (O(3): 0.86, 1.61, 4.48, 9.49 at β = 1, 2, 5, 10).
  • Ours: O(4) correlation lengths by cluster simulation, 4.7, 7.7 and 14.0 at β = 1.8, 2.0 and 2.2, about 10% below the leading formula (5.0, 8.9, 15.9), as expected this far from the limit. The Lean line count is ours.

2:32How the proof goes

The short paper’s idea fits on one screen. Pin the spins on two square rings, and twist the inner ring by a half turn. Because rotations about two different axes produce a rotation about a third, waves at every scale can absorb the twist, and the cost falls at least as fast as 1/log N for a ring of size N. An exact identity turns that cost into the chance that a cluster of equal signs crosses the ring. So crossings die out, and a count over coarse paths turns that into exponential decay. Our simulation shows the mechanism: for n equal to three, the crossing chance falls to zero as the ring grows; for n equal to two, when cold, it stays at one. Three of the longer papers use an exact renormalization group: block spins at every scale, with the coupling drifting as predicted. The three later papers rest on the first O(4) paper, which the O(3) construction cites more than 300 times. The exact constant comes from an auxiliary model solved by the Bethe ansatz and matched back to the lattice. As an illustration, our cluster simulations of O(4) reach correlation lengths near 14, about 10% below the leading formula, which is expected so far from the limit.

3:50Where it stands

All five preprints are unrefereed and have not been revised. Only the short paper’s main theorem is formalized in Lean: about 13,000 lines, with no unproved steps and its statement pinned down; we did not build it. The other four, about 360 pages, are not formalized. None is among those OSVerify reviewed. In Scientific American, Roland Bauerschmidt said one paper, which the article called the first, seems very reasonable, and called the rest of the papers on this problem incomprehensible slop. The article does not say which paper he meant. If the short paper holds, spins with three or more components in two dimensions always forget their direction, at every temperature, as Polyakov predicted. The rest, if it holds, would build the field theory behind it; that part needs careful checking.
