No bound at all?

An AI preprint claims a negative answer to Kalton’s question on Lipschitz-free spaces

Covers result 330 in OpenAI’s catalog: its one paper. See its connections to other results and the work it cites.

The claim, in plain words

Any metric space, with a base point chosen, sits inside a Banach space, its Lipschitz-free space, where the point masses are vectors, the base point becomes zero, and the distance between two points becomes the norm of their difference. For finitely many points, the norm of a combination of masses is the cheapest way to transport the positive masses onto the negative ones, with the base point making up any difference. Our computer checks this on small spaces: the transport cost and the best 1-Lipschitz test agree in all 100 cases.

A Banach space has the approximation property if finite-rank maps approximate the identity as closely as we like on every compact set, and the bounded approximation property if those maps can share one bound on their norms. In 2004 Kalton proved that free spaces over uniformly discrete spaces, where distances never drop below a fixed gap, have the approximation property, and asked whether they also have the bounded one. Later surveys listed it as an open problem, and a paper this April called it one of the major ones.

Result 330 is one OpenAI paper, 21 pages, dated September 26 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims a countable metric space, with every distance at least 1, whose real Lipschitz-free space has the approximation property but not the bounded one: the norms of good approximating maps cannot share any finite bound. A corollary gives an equivalent norm on ℓ₁ without the metric approximation property.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: that the approximation property itself is new, since Kalton proved it; that every uniformly discrete space fails, since bounded ones are fine; or an explicit space, since the construction is random and huge. An earlier paper found the same gap for discrete spaces with no guaranteed minimum distance.

How the proof goes

  1. Glue blocks at one point. Any bounded block with all distances at least 1 has a free space like ℓ₁, with good maps of norm at most twice its diameter. The trick is to glue infinitely many blocks at one point, block p defeating every map of norm p. For each finite set of blocks the bound is finite, which gives the approximation property, but the bounds grow without limit: 910, 3,920, 10,230, and so on.
  2. Inside a block: coloured graphs and anchors. Inside each block are huge random regular graphs with random colours that look locally like coloured trees, and each vertex is wired to an anchor shared by all vertices with the same capped distances to the colours. An operator of norm at most p that nearly fixes every anchor would give signed measures weighted toward points with similar labels. Tests built from records of the longest matched walks then force an average of at least 11p, while the slightly adjusted operator allows only 2p: a contradiction. Our computer checks the toy versions: a small block keeps every distance at least 1, and a key walk lemma holds in about 24,000 checks.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: on small finite spaces, the free norm as a transport cost equals its dual (best 1-Lipschitz test) in 100 of 100 cases.
  • Ours: the paper’s budgets for the first blocks are 910, 3,920, 10,230, …, finite for each set of blocks but unbounded.
  • The paper’s closing contradiction: an average test of at least 11p against an upper bound of 2p.
  • Ours: a toy block keeps every distance at least 1; the walk lemma holds in 23,920 checks with 0 failures.
