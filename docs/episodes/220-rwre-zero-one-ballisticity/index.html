Escape, but never speed up?

Three preprints claim zero–one laws for escape directions and the ballisticity conjecture for random walks in random environments

The claim, in plain words

At each site of a grid, draw a set of step probabilities, independently from site to site, and let a walker follow them: a random walk in a random environment. In one dimension it can do strange things. Our simulated walk has an average push of +0.38 per step to the right, always escapes to the right, and yet its speed tends to zero: rare stretches of left-pushing sites trap it, and in one dimension it cannot go around them. In two dimensions, with independent sites and sideways steps added, the same two kinds of sites give a steady speed of about 0.16.

Two old questions follow. Is escape in a fixed direction a zero-or-one event? Zerner and Merkl proved it in two dimensions. And does escaping in a direction force a positive speed in that direction? That is the ballisticity conjecture.

Result 220 bundles three OpenAI preprints, 127 pages, dated September 23 and October 5, 2026. The first claims the zero–one law in every dimension at least three, for independent environments where every step has positive probability. The second claims the ballisticity conjecture as Fribergh and Kious stated it: in dimension two and up, for independent environments with step probabilities bounded below, almost sure escape in a direction implies a deterministic velocity with a positive component in that direction. In dimension three and up, any positive chance of escape is then enough, and the walk escapes, with probability one, in each direction at an acute angle to its velocity, and in no other. The third paper extends the zero–one law to environments with step probabilities bounded below whose dependence stops at a fixed range.

What it doesn’t claim

Sznitman’s condition (T), slowdown estimates, a central limit theorem, or a formula for the velocity. Mixing environments, where Bramson, Zeitouni and Zerner found counterexamples, are excluded.

How the proof goes

  1. Regeneration. Times when the walker reaches a new record and never falls back. After such a time it sees only fresh sites, so the future is independent of the past.
  2. Zero–one. Suppose escape both ways had positive probability. Line up paths escaping up and down so they face each other, and count at how many scales they meet. An entropy bound says at most about J/log J of the first J scales; a martingale bound says at least J/log log J. For large J the lower bound overtakes the upper: they cannot both hold.
  3. Ballisticity. Transience gives regeneration pieces of finite length but not finite average duration. The paper reduces speed to one estimate, that environments where climbing is polynomially hard are super-polynomially rare, and proves it by contradiction, comparing two walks in the same environment.

2:20How the proof goes

Both start from regeneration: times when the walker reaches a new record and never falls back. After such a time it sees only fresh, unexplored sites, so the future is independent of the past. For the zero-one law, suppose escape both ways had positive probability. Line up paths escaping up and paths escaping down so they face each other, and count at how many scales they meet. An entropy bound says at most about J/log J of the first J scales; a martingale bound says at least J/log log J. One divides J by log J, the other only by log log J, so for large J the lower bound overtakes the upper: they cannot both hold. For ballisticity, the whole difficulty is that transience gives regeneration pieces of finite length but not finite average duration. The paper reduces speed to one estimate: environments where climbing is polynomially hard must be super-polynomially rare. It proves this by contradiction, comparing two walks in the same environment, and showing that conditioning on bad environments costs so little entropy that the slowdown leads to a contradiction.

3:31Where it stands

All three preprints are unrefereed and have not been revised. The zero-one law and the ballisticity theorem, with its hemisphere corollary, are formalized in Lean against pinned statements: about 117,000 lines in all, with no unproved steps; we did not build them. The finite-range paper is not formalized. None is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we found no expert reaction or earlier proof. If they hold, a random walk in an independent random environment with step probabilities bounded below, in any dimension above one, cannot creep: if it surely escapes in a direction, it escapes at a positive speed.
