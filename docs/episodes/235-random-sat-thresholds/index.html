When does random 3-SAT break?

Four preprints on random k-SAT: another proof that thresholds exist, a square-root window, and a computable 3-SAT threshold

The claim, in plain words

Take n true-or-false variables and add random clauses, each an “or” of three variables with random signs. Each clause vetoes one eighth of all assignments; keep adding until no assignment survives. Experiments show this happens suddenly, and physicists predict it at about 4.27 clauses per variable. But does a sharp threshold density exist as n grows? Chvátal and Reed conjectured it in 1992; Friedgut proved in 1999 that the transition is sharp around some sequence of densities, but not that the sequence converges; Ding, Sly and Sun proved existence for large clause sizes. On October 5, 2026, Gaia Carenini of Cambridge made public a proof for every clause size from three up (ECCC TR26-229).

Result 235 is four OpenAI preprints, 116 pages, dated September 25 to October 5, 2026. They credit Carenini with priority for the threshold’s existence and give another proof for every clause size from three up. Two variance papers claim that the number of clauses at which the formula first becomes unsatisfiable has variance of order n, so the transition window is of order √n clauses; for 3-SAT this is a separate 10-page paper. The fourth claims that the 3-SAT threshold is a computable real number.

What it doesn’t claim

The value of any threshold, or its identification with 4.27; what happens exactly at the threshold; or a rate of convergence. “Computable” means an algorithm exists in principle: it gives no digits, and by our arithmetic from the paper’s explicit constant its error only drops below 0.1 at around 10127 variables.

How the proof goes

  1. Forced variables. A variable is forced if every surviving assignment sets it the same way; a new clause can kill everything only if it hits three forced variables, all with the wrong signs. By a route much like Carenini’s, the threshold paper erases one variable and compares the shortened clauses with full ones, to show forcing adds up slowly. That gives concentration, and a comparison of systems of different sizes shows the centres converge. Carenini reaches concentration and the limit by different steps.
  2. The window. Delete one clause and ask how long the delay lasts. If restoring it kills satisfiability, all its variables are forced, and flipping any one of them must fail. Solution sets that short clauses kill easily die fast under long ones. For 3-SAT a logarithm remained, and the separate paper removes it with a new potential.
  3. Computability. The threshold is bracketed from below by finite computations and from above by finite certificates from spin-glass theory.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: six variables, clauses added one at a time: after 26 clauses two of 64 assignments survive, after 27 one, with every variable forced; it lasts 21 more clauses until clause 49 contradicts all three of its forced values at once.
  • Ours: our solver (checked against brute force on 360 streams) repeated this for up to 300 variables, about 55,000 times for 3-SAT; the satisfiability curves steepen. The variance of the hitting time divided by n is still falling at 300 variables (12.0 down to 3.1), so small systems neither confirm nor refute the linear law.
