How much overlap?

Covering space with one convex body: the worst density is of order n log n

The claims, in plain words

Cover all of space with copies of one convex body, moved around but never turned. Unless the body tiles, the copies must overlap, and the cost is the density: the total volume of the copies per unit of space, the average number of copies over each point. Squares and hexagons tile, with density 1; for disks the best is 2π/√27, about 1.21 (Kershner; our lattice search agrees). The question is how large the density can be forced to be, for the worst convex body in n dimensions, when every body gets its best arrangement.

Rogers showed in 1957 that every convex body in dimension n ≥ 3 has a covering of density at most n log n + n log log n + 5n, with centers that may form several shifted copies of a lattice. Random centers explain the n log n: at density θ they leave a fraction e−θ of space uncovered, and enlarging the body by a factor 1 + 1/n, which multiplies its volume by less than e, is enough to fill the holes once that fraction is below about n−n. With a single lattice the bounds were weaker: Rogers’ nlog₂ log n, a constant times n² by Ordentlich, Regev and Weiss, and in 2026 n log n times a power of log log n by Li and Liu, who also found symmetric bodies whose lattice coverings need at least a constant times n log n. Naszódi had recorded the question whether some arrangement could always achieve a constant times n.

Result 092 is two OpenAI preprints dated September 23, 2026. The first (23 pages) claims that every convex body in n dimensions, n ≥ 2, has a covering by translates along one lattice with density at most an absolute constant times n log n, with no symmetry or smoothness assumed. The second (30 pages) claims that, for every large n, some centrally symmetric convex body has translative covering density above c n log n against every arrangement of centers, lattice or not. Together they pin the worst covering density at order n log n, for lattice and unrestricted coverings, over all bodies or only symmetric ones.

What it doesn’t claim

The constants are not computed, and the result concerns the worst body in each dimension, not the covering density of any particular body. Euclidean balls are not settled: the best known bounds for them are still of order n (Coxeter, Few and Rogers) and about ½ n log n (Dumer).

How the proofs go

  1. One lattice: slices. Following Li and Liu, split coordinates into a large horizontal part and a small vertical part. After an affine change, Eldan and Klartag’s theorem makes the horizontal slices of the body have volumes close to a Gaussian profile in the vertical direction. The Rogers–Schmidt mean-hole estimate, in the form of Ordentlich, Regev and Weiss, gives one horizontal lattice that leaves only an exponentially small fraction uncovered in every slice on a finite list, and one shear of a vertical lattice spreads the slices over all heights.
  2. Binary corrections and the last holes. Each vertical coordinate gets two adjacent integer choices. The paper keeps a hierarchy of weighted branches, in blocks of sizes about b, b0.9, b0.81, … down to a fixed cutoff; each block loses only a tiny fraction of the Gaussian mass, while every piece stays small enough for the mean-hole estimate. A random subset-sum sampler makes each block work from any starting point, and one shear handles all patterns at once. The last holes go by a classical lemma going back to Rogers: if the uncovered fraction δ of K + Γ satisfies δ < (1 − δ)k−n, then (1 + 1/k)K + Γ covers everything.
  3. The lower bound: a random body. A ball slightly larger than the unit ball is cut by symmetric slabs whose directions are random Poisson points on the sphere; with positive probability it keeps a substantial volume. Any low-density periodic covering, localized and rounded, becomes one of a finite list of patterns fixed before the body is drawn. A hole appears at a target point when the slabs cut it out of every nearby copy; about ec n log n well-separated targets let the bound reach order n log n. Grouped cap conditions and Janson’s inequality make each pattern’s covering probability doubly exponentially small, enough to beat the number of patterns, and a periodization principle extends the result to all coverings.

The numbers in the video

  • A numerical search over lattices finds the best lattice covering density for disks, 1.2092 = 2π/√27; squares and regular hexagons tile with density 1.
  • Random unit balls in a 3-dimensional torus (10 realizations per density) leave uncovered fractions matching e−θ, for example 0.139 at θ = 2 and 0.00031 at θ = 8.
  • The completion lemma: disks of radius 0.68 on the square grid leave 0.298 percent uncovered (exactly), the lemma allows k = 18, and the enlarged radius 0.7178 covers, since the grid needs √2/2 = 0.7071. No low-dimensional computation can test an n log n bound.

The question here is about high dimensions. Give every convex body its best arrangement. How large can the density still be forced to be, for the worst body in n dimensions? Rogers showed in 1957 that every convex body in dimension 3 and up has a covering of density at most n log n + n log log n + 5n. Random centers show where n log n comes from. If copies of total density θ are dropped at random, a fraction e^(−θ) of space stays uncovered, and our simulation in 3 dimensions matches this. To fill the last holes by enlarging the body by a factor 1 + 1/n, which multiplies its volume by less than e, it is enough for the uncovered fraction to be below about n^(−n), and that takes density about n log n. Rogers’ centers may form several shifted copies of a lattice. A single lattice is harder: Rogers’ lattice bound grew like n^(log₂ log n), Ordentlich, Regev and Weiss brought it down to a constant times n², and earlier this year Li and Liu reached n log n times a power of log log n. They also found symmetric bodies whose lattice coverings need at least a constant times n log n. Two questions remained. Can the extra log log factor for single lattices be removed? And could a cleverer arrangement, with many lattice cosets or no pattern at all, do better than n log n for every body, perhaps even a constant times n? Naszódi recorded that linear-bound question. For Euclidean balls the gap is still wide: Coxeter, Few and Rogers proved a lower bound of order n, and Dumer an upper bound of about ½ n log n.

3:37How the proofs go

The lattice proof develops a framework of Li and Liu. Split the coordinates into a large horizontal part and a much smaller vertical part. After an affine change, a theorem of Eldan and Klartag says the horizontal slices of the body have volumes close to a Gaussian profile in the vertical direction. A mean-hole estimate of Rogers and Schmidt, in the form of Ordentlich, Regev and Weiss, gives one horizontal lattice that leaves only an exponentially small fraction uncovered in every slice on a finite list. Then one shear of a vertical lattice has to spread these slices so that every height gets enough horizontal coverage. Li and Liu’s binary corrections give each vertical coordinate two adjacent integer choices. The new paper keeps a whole hierarchy of these weighted branches, in blocks of sizes about b, b^0.9, b^0.81, and so on, down to a fixed cutoff. Each block loses only a tiny fraction of the Gaussian mass, so the losses stay bounded, while every single piece stays small enough for the mean-hole estimate. A random subset-sum sampler makes each block work from any starting point, and one shear handles all the patterns at once. The last holes go by a classical lemma going back to Rogers: if the uncovered fraction is below about k^(−n), enlarging the body by a factor 1 + 1/k covers everything. In the plane, disks of radius 0.68 on the square grid leave 0.3 percent uncovered; the lemma allows k = 18, and the enlarged radius, 0.718, does cover, since the grid needs 0.707. The lower bound uses a random body: a ball slightly larger than the unit ball, cut by symmetric slabs whose directions are random Poisson points on the sphere. With positive probability it keeps a substantial volume. Any periodic covering of low density, localized to a small window and rounded, becomes one of a finite list of center patterns, fixed before the body is drawn. For each pattern, a hole appears at a target point if the random slabs cut that point out of every nearby copy. Using about e^(c n log n) well-separated targets is what lets the bound reach order n log n. Following Li and Liu, these conditions are grouped into blocks, and Janson’s inequality shows that each pattern covers the window with doubly exponentially small probability, small enough to beat the number of patterns. A periodization principle carries the result from periodic to all coverings.

6:21Where it stands

Neither preprint is peer reviewed, and neither has been revised. OpenAI’s Lean formalizations cover the single-lattice theorem and the optimal order; we have not run them. Both papers build directly on Li and Liu’s 2026 work, a preprint and a companion manuscript, which supply the horizontal and vertical framework, the binary corrections, the random slab bodies and the witness method. Neither paper is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If they hold, the worst covering density in n dimensions grows like n log n, up to constant factors, whether the centers form one lattice or not.
