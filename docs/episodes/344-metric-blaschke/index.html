The claim, in plain words

The injectivity radius of a closed manifold is how far you can travel from any point along geodesics before they stop being shortest paths. It can never exceed the diameter, the largest distance between two points. On a round sphere the two are equal: every geodesic from a point reaches the opposite point together. On flat tori, square or hexagonal, or a product of two equal spheres, our computer finds the injectivity radius falls short, to about 0.71 or 0.87 of the diameter. On an oblate spheroid, geodesics from a point on the equator meet along a whole arc instead of at one point.

Manifolds whose injectivity radius equals their diameter are named after Blaschke, who raised the question for surfaces in 1921. Besides spheres there are four other kinds of model: real, complex and quaternionic projective spaces, and the Cayley plane. The Blaschke conjecture says these are the only examples, up to scale, in the form stated in Besse’s 1978 book. Surfaces were settled by Green in 1963, and spheres and real projective spaces by about 1980, through Berger, Kazdan, Weinstein and Yang. The complex, quaternionic and Cayley cases stayed open: there were results on their topology and volume, and proofs only under extra curvature assumptions.

Result 344 is one OpenAI paper, 41 pages, dated September 23 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims the full conjecture: a closed Riemannian manifold whose injectivity radius equals its diameter is, up to scale, one of the five models. The key new step is a sharp lower bound: such a manifold has at least the volume of its model with the same diameter, with equality only for the model.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: anything about manifolds where only some points have this property, or where geodesics all close up but need not stay shortest that long, which are different problems; or a machine-checked proof, since there is no Lean.

How the proof goes

  1. A sharp volume lower bound. From the hypothesis and classical theory, every geodesic closes up with the same length. Along each closed geodesic, the paper splits the Jacobi determinant into two blocks and combines a Kazdan-type identity with Jensen’s inequality, integrating over all geodesics. This gives the volume lower bound, and equality forces a harmonic manifold, which by Szabó’s theorem is the model. Our computer reproduces the model volumes from the paper’s density formula; relative to a round sphere of the same dimension and diameter they are 8, 3, 7 and 39 times as large, for four-dimensional real projective space, the complex projective plane, the quaternionic plane and the Cayley plane.
  2. Then a matching upper bound. It then needs a matching upper bound. For complex projective spaces it comes from Yang’s 1991 work; for the two remaining planes, quaternionic and Cayley, from earlier diffeomorphism results and a volume theorem of Reznikov; and for all quaternionic projective spaces, the plane included, from a new 20-page topological argument whose final bound is a Catalan number. The two bounds together force isometry.
