Can one kind of particle provably change phase?

Two preprints claim a temperature singularity for a gas with a radial pair potential

The claim, in plain words

Put identical particles in space and let every pair interact through the same function of their distance. Can such a gas show a sharp phase transition? On lattices, rigorous transitions go back to Peierls in the 1930s; in the continuum the question resisted. In 1984 Barry Simon listed it as Problem 7 of his fifteen problems in mathematical physics: find a stable pair potential, bounded and decaying faster than r−3 by a power, for which, at large densities, the free energy is not C1 in temperature. Most earlier proofs needed props: two species (the Widom–Rowlinson model), four-body forces (Lebowitz, Mazel and Presutti), or a pair interaction tied to a fixed grid of boxes (2025). In 1975 Israel got two coexisting densities, but with a hard core that forbids a whole range of distances.

Result 228 is two OpenAI preprints, 64 pages, dated September 24, 2026. Each constructs a stable radial pair potential in three dimensions, an open interval of densities, and one inverse temperature at which the temperature derivative of the canonical free energy jumps, for every density in the interval. In the first paper the repulsion diverges at zero distance; in the second the potential is bounded and continuous, with a tail bounded by C·r−3−1/32. The second seems to meet Simon’s literal conditions, though for one interval of densities rather than all large ones; both papers cite Simon’s list, but neither names Problem 7 or claims to solve it.

What it doesn’t claim

The Lennard-Jones potential, phases identified as liquid or vapor, values for the constants, or a natural-looking potential. The potentials are engineered: by our reckoning from one paper’s stated conditions, with its example kernel, the core parameter must exceed about 300,000, putting the special shell below 10−131,428.

How the proof goes

  1. A core that allows only tetrahedra. Distances below one cost a large energy, except in a hair-thin shell at almost zero distance. No five points can all sit at shell distances from each other, so at most four particles can huddle. A counting bound from graph theory (Turán) then gives stability. Three ways to fill space compete: empty space, separated single particles, and tetrahedral clusters.
  2. A triple point. One ideal mean-field attraction of the right strength makes the three options meet at a single point, at inverse temperature 1. At fixed density, passing through that temperature switches which two phases coexist (singles and clusters when hotter, empty space and clusters when colder), releasing latent heat: the jump in the derivative.
  3. Infinitely many attractions. A real potential could round that corner off, so the papers add infinitely many ever-weaker, ever-longer-range attractions, each chosen to protect the corner from the errors of the last.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: the papers’ ideal model computed exactly: the triple point (β, h) = (1, −3/4), and at density 5p/3, where p is the packing density, a jump of 1.75p in the scaled energy. Five points need a distance ratio of at least √(12/7) ≈ 1.309 (a triangular bipyramid), far above the shell’s 1.001.
  • Ours: a cartoon Monte Carlo with 32 particles and a schematic bounded potential; on cooling, the particles gather into four-particle clusters. At that size the change is smooth, as it must be. The core-parameter bound is our arithmetic from one paper’s conditions.
