The claims, in plain words

The edit distance between two strings is the least number of single-symbol insertions, deletions and substitutions that turn one into the other. Because an insertion or deletion shifts everything after it, the distance depends on the best alignment: cutting the first symbol of a word and pasting it at the end costs at most two edits, though it can change most positions. Many algorithms would be simpler with an ℓ1 distance, a sum of absolute differences of coordinates, instead. The question is the least distortion D such that some map into ℓ1 keeps every edit distance within a factor D, after rescaling, for all strings of length at most d.

Ostrovsky and Rabani embedded the binary strings of length d with distortion exp(O(√(log d · log log d))), and noted extensions to other alphabets and lengths. Lower bounds grew from close to 3/2 (Andoni, Deza, Gupta, Indyk and Raskhodnikova) to order √(log d / log log d) (Khot and Naor) and order log d (Krauthgamer and Rabani), leaving a large gap.

Result 099 is three OpenAI preprints dated September 27, 2026: the main paper (15 pages) and two companions, on finite circles, binary codes and histogram embeddings (33 pages) and on tree constructions (23 pages). The main paper claims that for absolute constants c and C, every large d and every finite alphabet with at least two symbols, the least ℓ1 distortion lies between exp(c √(log d · log log d)) and exp(C √(log d · log log d)). So the Ostrovsky–Rabani exponent is sharp up to constant factors, and binary strings of one common length already force the lower bound. The companions give four further lower-bound constructions, from circles and from trees, and a complete histogram embedding for the upper bound.

What it doesn’t claim

Only the exponent is determined: the paper does not specify the constants c and C, and the distortion itself could still improve by a factor that grows with d, as long as the exponent keeps its order. Nothing is claimed about computing the embedding quickly. The main paper takes Ostrovsky and Rabani’s fixed-length theorem as an input and extends it to all alphabets and lengths.

How the lower bound goes

  1. Rows, payloads and markers. Words are built level by level. A word is a long list of rows; each row holds a payload of lower-level words in fixed slots, each slot with its own tag so that different slots cannot match, followed by a marker of a fresh symbol as long as the payload. The lower-level words depend on states in many components; in each, a distinguished step cycles the state with a prime period, with different primes for different components. Advancing every component at once shifts the row list by one, which costs only the deletion and insertion of one row.
  2. The distinguished step costs. Advancing only the distinguished component changes it in many separately tagged slots. Pairing row i with row i leaves those slots apart; any other pairing, by a row shift shorter than the word, leaves many of the other prime-period components apart. An alignment that spreads one source payload across several target rows skips a whole marker in between, and the skipped markers are disjoint. So every alignment pays, and relative to the word length the guaranteed separation shrinks by a factor of 24 per level.
  3. The ℓ1 side. A finite ℓ1 metric is a nonnegative sum of cut metrics, and each cut is expanded in Fourier characters on the product of the cycles. A character involving few components is caught by the simultaneous step: with distinct prime denominators its phase change is never a whole number, and with few denominators it stays well away from one. A character involving many components is caught by the average of the single-component steps. After k levels, any ℓ1 map must distort the words by at least (h/24)k/(16w) = exp(Ω(k log k)), with h = 10k, while the logarithm of the word length is O(k² log k). A delimiter code makes the words binary, and choosing k of order √(log d / log log d) gives the exponent √(log d · log log d).

The numbers in the video

  • Kitten to sitting takes 3 edits. A cyclic shift of a random 1,000-bit word changes 508 positions, at an edit cost of 2.
  • The exact least ℓ1 distortion of tiny sets, by linear programming over cut metrics: 8/7 for the 8 binary words of length 3 (127 cuts), 5/4 for the 16 words of length 4 (32,767 cuts), and 4/3 for the 15 words of length at most 3 (16,383 cuts).
  • For the primes 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11, none of the 2,309 nonzero characters has a whole-number phase change; for example 1/2 + 2/3 + 4/5 = 59/30.
  • The bar chart compares log log d with √(log d · log log d), constants set to 1: 2.6 and 6.0 at d = 106, 4.0 and 14.9 at d = 1024, 5.4 and 35.4 at d = 10100.

5:32Where it stands

The three preprints are not peer reviewed and have not been revised. Lean covers the two-sided bound and the four further lower-bound constructions; we have not run it. They are not among the papers OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, no embedding of edit distance into ℓ₁ can beat Ostrovsky and Rabani’s exponent by more than a constant factor: √(log d · log log d) is the right exponent. The best constants in that exponent remain open.
