Simple, amenable, finitely presented?

An AI preprint claims an infinite group with all three properties

The claim, in plain words

Three properties of a group:

  • Finitely presented: finitely many generators and rules.
  • Simple: no normal subgroups except the trivial ones.
  • Amenable: there are finite sets that barely move.

Finite simple groups have all three, but trivially. Infinite examples had only two at a time. Thompson’s group V (1965) is finitely presented and simple, but it contains free groups, so it is not amenable. Groups built from one-dimensional cut-and-shift dynamics are simple (Matui) and amenable (Juschenko and Monod, 2012), but they are never finitely presented. The question of all three at once was on record by 2008, and Juschenko and Monod named it the next problem. In 2021 de Cornulier wrote that, as far as he knew, there was not even a plausible candidate.

Result 253 is one OpenAI preprint, 58 pages, dated September 23, 2026. It claims an infinite group that is finitely presented, simple and amenable. The group rearranges pieces of several unit squares. The squares are cut along lines whose positions, and two of whose slopes, come from the golden ratio. The group is generated by cycling three identical pieces.

What it doesn’t claim

  • An explicit list of rules. The relations are all words up to some length that are trivial in the group, and that length is never given. With the Lean version’s 206 squares, the paper’s list of generators alone has about 354 billion entries, by our count.
  • The stronger finiteness condition called type F∞.
  • Anything about other two-dimensional groups in general.

Last checked October 19, 2026. The preprint has not been revised. In Lean, the whole statement is formalized for 206 squares: infinite, finitely presented, simple and amenable. That is about 85,000 lines with no unproved steps (we did not build it). OSVerify has not reviewed the paper. On October 8, Matthew Zaremsky crossed the question off his list of open problems. We found no detailed public check, and no error has been reported.

How the proof goes

  1. Simple. After cutting, any piece fits into any piece of larger area, so small rearrangements can be built from cycles of three pieces. The alternating group on five letters is perfect. So a normal subgroup containing one nontrivial element contains small copies of it, and moving those copies around produces every generator.
  2. Amenable. For any finitely many moves, the paper builds random partitions of the squares into polygon cells. Each move just slides the cells, and the random law barely notices the slide. In two dimensions independent random choices are not enough, so the paper correlates them.
  3. Finitely presented. The group’s Schur multiplier is finitely generated. The paper builds a finitely presented central cover, using steep lines whose golden-ratio conjugate slope is tiny. Finitely many more relations then kill the central kernel.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: shifting an n×n square changes a fraction 2/n of it (0.031 at n = 64). A ball in a free group always changes by more than its own size.
  • Ours: with independent random bits, a one-step slide becomes undetectable like 1/N in one dimension, but stays near 0.84 in two.
  • Ours: under the Lean’s constants the steep slope is at least about 439,000, and its conjugate is under 0.000002 in size.
  • Ours: a central cover of order 120 becomes the simple group of order 60 by adding one relation.

2:03How the proof goes

Simple: after cutting, any piece fits into any piece of larger area, so any small rearrangement can be built from cycles of three pieces. Because the alternating group on 5 letters is perfect, a normal subgroup with one nontrivial element contains small copies of it, and moving those copies around produces every generator. Our computer checked the key identity: a 3-cycle is a commutator of two even permutations of 5 things. Amenable: for any finitely many moves, the paper builds random partitions of the squares into polygon cells that each move just slides, with a law that barely notices the slide. In two dimensions, independent random choices are not enough; our toy: in one dimension their detectability falls like 1/N, in two it stays near 0.84. The paper correlates them. Finitely presented: the paper shows the group’s Schur multiplier is finitely generated, and builds a finitely presented central cover, using steep lines whose golden-ratio conjugate slope is tiny. Under the Lean version’s constants, that slope must be at least about 439,000, and its conjugate under 0.000002 in size. Finitely many more relations then kill the central kernel. Our toy of that last step: a group of order 120 becomes the simple group of order 60 by adding one relation.
