GO02 (Specials; Google Cogentic; see also GO01, 142 and 3), November 2026

Factoring with no coin flips, below exponential time?

A 59-page paper from Google’s Cogentic agent claims that every composite number can be factored deterministically, with no unproven hypothesis, in exp(3 (ln N)^(3/4)) bit operations, and that a non-square modulo any prime can be found deterministically in L[1/2] time. The Lean proof it mentions has not been released, and no human check is named.

The question

This is GO02, the second of our Specials on papers from Google’s Cogentic. On November 25, 2026, Google Research added six math papers to its public GitHub repository. Each README says that the paper and a Lean formalization were entirely obtained by Cogentic, an agent system built on Gemini 4, with no human interaction other than the initial prompt. The Lean files have not been released. Google is not affiliated with this series. GO01 covered the first paper, a factoring method that flips coins. This paper claims factoring with no coin flips at all, in less than exponential time, and a fast way, with no coins, to find a number that is not a square modulo a prime. So what is the question?

Every whole number splits into primes, and checking a split is easy. Finding one is hard. With coin flips allowed, factoring has had a proven time below exponential since Dixon, in 1981; Lenstra and Pomerance sharpened it in 1992. Without coins, the picture is much worse. Trial division can take up to the square root of N steps. Pollard and Strassen, in the 1970s, brought that down to the fourth root, and David Harvey, in 2021, to the fifth root; later work shaved off only factors smaller than any power of N. All of these are still exponential in the number of digits. And even assuming the generalized Riemann hypothesis, the paper says, no deterministic method below exponential time was known.

The paper’s route starts from a classic trick. Take 77, 7 × 11. 30 is a multiple of 6 and of 10, one less than each prime. Raise 2 to the 15th power, half of 30, modulo 77: you get 43. 43 is one more than a multiple of 7, but one less than a multiple of 11. So the greatest common divisor of 42 and 77 is 7: a factor. It works because 2 is a square modulo 7 but not modulo 11. Try 4, a square modulo both, and you get 1: no split. So the method needs two things: a multiple of every p − 1, and a witness that is a square for one factor but not another, like 2 here. For a single prime, the witness is a non-square, a quadratic nonresidue. Half of the numbers from 1 to p − 1 are nonresidues, so coin flips find one quickly, and for every prime below a million, the smallest is at most 43. But without coins or unproven hypotheses, the best proven bound comes from Burgess, in 1957: a scan up to about p^0.15. Assuming the generalized Riemann hypothesis, a scan up to 2 (log p)² is enough, by Ankeny and Bach.

The claim, in plain words

The claim. The paper has 59 pages and no author line; a footnote credits Cogentic. It states three theorems, counted in bit operations on a multitape Turing machine, with no randomness, no unproven hypotheses about smooth numbers, and no Riemann hypothesis. Theorem 1.3 is the slow part: given an odd composite N that is not a perfect power, find a multiple M of every p − 1, or a factor, in about e^(3 (log N)^(3/4)). Theorem 1.2 is the fast part: given N and any such M, split N in time just above L[1/2], times the length of M; and for any odd prime p, find a quadratic nonresidue in time just above L[1/2]. Theorem 1.1 chains the two: every composite number is factored, with no coins, in about e^(3 (log N)^(3/4)). Every constant is effective, the paper says, though none is given as a number. If it holds, it beats every published deterministic factoring bound by far, with or without the Riemann hypothesis, and, apart from an unreviewed claim we come back to, it is the first proven nonresidue search below a power of p.

In the paper’s words (p. 4). Theorem 1.1: an explicit deterministic algorithm A_factor that, for every composite N ≥ 4, outputs a divisor 1 < d < N within C_fac·exp(3 (ln N)^(3/4)) bit operations, which is L_N[3/4, o(1)]. Theorem 1.2: (a) for a composite N ≥ 4 that is not a perfect power and an even M̃ with p − 1 dividing M̃ for every prime p dividing N, a divisor within C₁·exp(C_split (ln N)^(1/2) ln ln(N + 16))·(ln M̃)(ln N)²; (b) for an odd prime p, a z in 1, …, p − 1 with Legendre symbol −1 within C₂·exp(C_split (ln p)^(1/2) ln ln(p + 16)). Theorem 1.3: for an odd composite non-perfect-power N ≥ 9, either a divisor or such an M̃ with ln M̃ ≤ exp((ln N)^0.65), within C_exp·exp(3 (ln N)^(3/4)). Model: elementary bit operations with schoolbook arithmetic, valid on a multitape Turing machine and a word-RAM; “zero randomness, zero unproven smoothness hypotheses, and zero Riemann-hypothesis assumptions”, with effective constants (no numerical value is given for C_split, C_fac or C_exp).

What would be new, and what is not claimed

How fast is that? Take a 617-digit number, the size of an RSA-2048 key, and, as an illustration only, drop the hidden constants. The new formula gives about 10^301 operations. Trial division, 10^308. Harvey’s fifth root, 10^123. The coin-flipping bound of Lenstra and Pomerance, 10^44, and the number field sieve, the practical method, about 10^35. By these formulas, the new bound beats trial division only beyond about 563 digits, and Harvey’s fifth root beyond about 22,000 digits. In the algorithm itself, by our reading, the first step is trial division up to a limit past the square root of N for every number below about 8,400 digits. So for those, trial division finds the factor. For nonresidues, with an illustrative constant of 1, the new bound overtakes Burgess only beyond about 1,200 digits, though the algorithm as written simply scans upward for every prime below about 28,000 digits. And the proof’s main argument for splitting applies only to N above a threshold of at least e^(2^80). What is not claimed: a practical algorithm, anything faster than coin-flipping methods, any threat to RSA, or the size beyond which the bounds take hold.

How the proof goes

How the proof goes, by the paper’s overview. Part one finds the witness. The classic idea is Vinogradov’s trick: if every number up to some Y is a square modulo q, so is every product of such numbers. But numbers with one large prime factor can be non-squares, and past a point they outweigh the rest. That is where the old bound stalls. The paper does not try to bound the smallest nonresidue among ordinary whole numbers. Instead, it takes −1 and K small primes, with K on the order of the square root of log q, and works in the quadratic number fields made from square roots of their products: 2^K − 1 fields, and the fields made from pairs. The algorithm tests the images of units and of short elements in all these fields. If every one were a square, the paper shows, the square pattern extends consistently, so a Walsh–Hadamard transform of certain counts is never negative. That boosts the smooth numbers by a factor of 2^K, more than a new sixteenth-moment Burgess bound for quadratic fields allows. So some tested element must be a witness, or N splits on the way.

Part two finds the multiple of every p − 1. It builds pairs of polynomials that share a root modulo N, as in the number field sieve. For each prime ell near a bound B, it searches small changes to the pair until the relations among small primes are complete; everything cancels except a power of ell that equals one modulo N. Its proof that most such primes have a good pair uses Landau and Page’s theorem and Montgomery’s zero-density estimates, and avoids Siegel’s theorem, whose constants cannot be computed. Then a counting argument: these primes generate a subgroup of small index modulo every prime factor, so multiplying in all small prime powers gives M. The search tries about e^(4 log 2 · (log N)^(3/4)), pairs, and 4 log 2 is 2.77, below 3. We checked that arithmetic and the counting lemma, and read the theorems, the overview and the final assembly. We did not check the other lemmas, which fill most of the 59 pages. The README says a Lean formalization, from Mathlib alone, is complete and will be released. Cogentic’s paper on arXiv describes an orchestrator, parallel provers and adversarial verifiers; its earlier results were verified by domain experts, by the authors’ account. For this paper, no human check is named.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: 77 = 7 × 11; 2^30 ≡ 3^30 ≡ 1 (mod 77), so M = 30 (a multiple of 6 and 10; found by brute force, not by the paper’s method); 2^15 ≡ 43 (mod 77), 43 ≡ 1 (mod 7) and ≡ −1 (mod 11), gcd(42, 77) = 7; 4^15 ≡ 1 (mod 77), no split. The squares mod 11 are 1, 3, 4, 5, 9.
  • Ours: for all 78,497 odd primes below 10^6, the least quadratic nonresidue averages 3.646 and is at most 43 (at p = 366,791).
  • Ours, illustration only (constants and lower-order terms dropped), operations for a 617-digit number as powers of ten: exp(3 (ln N)^(3/4)) 301.3; trial division N^(1/2) 308.3; Pollard–Strassen N^(1/4) 154.1; Harvey N^(1/5) 123.3; Lenstra–Pomerance L[1/2, 1] 44.1; number field sieve L[1/3, 1.923] 35.2.
  • Ours: the formula exp(3 (ln N)^(3/4)) is below N^(1/2), N^(1/4), N^(1/5) once (ln N)^(1/4) exceeds 6, 12, 15: beyond about 563, 9,006 and 21,986 digits.
  • Ours, nonresidues: a scan to p^(1/(4√e)) = p^0.1516 (Burgess) at 617 digits is about 10^93; exp((ln p)^(1/2) ln ln p) with an illustrative constant 1 (the paper gives none) is about 10^119; the second is smaller beyond about 1,181 digits. Under GRH, 2 (ln p)² is about 4.0 million at that size.
  • Our reading of the algorithm (not a claim of the paper): Step 1 of A_exp (p. 56) trial-divides N up to min(K_idx, N − 1), with K_idx = B·2^⌈(k_N+1)⌈log₂(6L₀)⌉/e(N;9,25)⌉ (p. 39). By our computation K_idx ≥ √N for every bit length below 28,071 bits (about 8,450 digits; the condition is not monotone: it fails from there, holds again roughly between 31,500 and 32,755 bits, and fails above), so for those numbers this trial division finds a factor. The guard d ≥ 10 of Step 2 first holds at about 3,109 bits (936 digits); below that Step 2 does nothing and Step 3 falls back to trial division.
  • From the paper: the proof of Theorem 1.2(a) uses its main argument for N ≥ N_thr = max(2^64, exp(2^(2 m_thr))) with m_thr ≥ 40 (pp. 27–28), so N_thr ≥ e^(2^80), a number with more than 5.3e+23 digits; smaller N are covered by the constant. Theorem 1.2(b) uses its main branch only when ln p ≥ 2^16 (p. 29), about 28,462 digits; otherwise the algorithm scans 2, 3, … The cost of Theorem 1.3: 16·B·L₀^8·U^4·A^2 = exp((4 ln 2 + o(1)) (ln N)^(3/4)) calls, and 4 ln 2 = 2.7726 < 3 because e^3 > 16 (p. 56).
