Log squared, everywhere?

A claimed randomized k-server policy with competitive ratio O(log² k) on every metric space

The claims, in plain words

In the k-server problem, k servers stand at points of a metric space. Requests arrive one at a time, each must be served by moving some server to the requested point, and the cost is the total distance moved. An online policy decides without seeing the future and is compared with the offline optimum, which knows every request in advance; the worst-case ratio is the competitive ratio. Manasse, McGeoch and Sleator (1988) showed that no deterministic policy does better than k and conjectured that k is achievable; Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou proved 2k − 1 with the work function algorithm, and a September 2026 preprint by Coester, Koutsoupias and Zbysiński, cited by the paper, claims k.

Randomization helps when the requests are fixed in advance. For paging, where all distances are equal, the randomized marking algorithm is within twice the harmonic number Hk ≈ log k, and order log k is optimal. That suggested the randomized k-server conjecture: O(log k) on every metric. In 2023 Bubeck, Coester and Rabani disproved it, with metrics that force order log² k. Upper bounds of order log² k were known on hierarchically separated trees (Bubeck, Cohen, Lee, Lee and Mądry), but on general metrics only with an extra factor depending on the number of points or on the spread of distances.

Result 110 is two OpenAI preprints, the existence theorem (71 pages) and a uniform algorithm (16 pages), both dated September 24, 2026. The first claims an absolute constant C such that, for every k ≥ 2, every metric space with at least k + 1 points and every starting position, one randomized policy has expected cost at most C (log(k + 1))² times the optimum plus an additive term, which is zero when the servers start at distinct points. It covers infinite and unbounded spaces and makes no efficiency claim. The second gives a uniform algorithm on finite rational metrics, with polynomial preprocessing and per-request work polynomial in the input size and the log of the request count, at the price of an additive constant that may be enormous.

What it doesn’t claim

It does not say every metric needs log² k: on paging the optimal order is log k, and order log k is the lower bound that holds on every metric. The claim settles the worst case: at most order log² k on every metric, and that much on some. The constant C is not computed, and the uniform algorithm’s additive constant is not claimed to be small.

How the proof goes

  1. A hidden comparator. First let the policy know the probability law of the requests. Fix one offline optimum for each possible input and use only its conditional distribution given the requests so far, never its realized future. A lazy-simulation lemma turns any such causal trajectory into a policy that moves one server per request and pays no more. At the end, independent episodes remove the additive term, and minimax and compactness arguments give one policy for all inputs on any metric.
  2. Partitions and a fixed tree. Partitions of the metric at geometrically shrinking scales, updated as requests arrive, map each point to a leaf of a fixed tree labelled by its cells. Heavy balls protect concentrated requests and other requests use chronological rosters in tiers. A ball-mass telescope sums the ratios of nested ball masses across scales into a single logarithm, so the comparator’s movement on the tree is at most order log k times its true movement.
  3. Allocation on the tree. From the comparator’s expected server counts mv below each vertex, an allocation theorem keeps fractional amounts qv ≤ C0 mv that always cover the request and move only order log k times as much as the comparator moves on the tree. Unused mass may park at internal vertices. This is the other logarithm.
  4. Back to the metric. Parked mass is represented by anchor points in the metric, whose travel is paid separately, and balanced rounding turns fractional amounts into exactly k servers with only a constant loss. Altogether the expected cost is at most an absolute constant times (log(k + 1))² times the optimum, plus an additive term.

The numbers in the video

  • Points 0, 1 and 100 on a line, servers at 0 and 100, requests alternating 1, 0, 1, 0, …: always moving the nearest server costs 10,000 over 10,000 requests, while the offline optimum pays 99.
  • Paging with k + 1 pages and random requests: randomized marking caused 1.50, 2.29, 2.95 and 3.59 times as many page faults as Belady’s offline optimum for k = 2, 5, 10 and 20, close to Hk = 1.50, 2.28, 2.93 and 3.60.
  • The lazy-simulation lemma held in 300 of 300 random trajectories, and the ball-mass telescope in 200 of 200 random measures.
  • For k = 106: log(k + 1) ≈ 13.8 and its square ≈ 191, ignoring the unspecified constant.

Together with the lower bound of Bubeck, Coester and Rabani, this would settle the worst-case order: log² k, not log k. For a million servers, and ignoring the unspecified constant, log k is about 14 and its square about 191, against a million for the deterministic bound. 3:26How the proof goes

The proof first lets the policy know the probability law of the requests. Then it can follow a hidden comparator: fix one offline optimum for each possible input, and use only its conditional distribution given the requests so far, never its realized future. A lazy simulation lemma turns any such causal trajectory into a policy that moves one server per request and pays no more. At the end, minimax and compactness arguments replace the known law by one policy for all inputs. In our check, the lazy policy never paid more than the simulated trajectory, in 300 random trials. Next, partitions of the metric at geometrically shrinking scales, updated as requests arrive, map each point to a leaf of a fixed tree, labelled by its cells. The comparator’s posterior gives expected server counts below every vertex. An allocation theorem keeps fractional server amounts on the tree, at most a constant times those counts and always covering the request, while moving only order log k times as much as the comparator moves on the tree. Unused mass may park at internal vertices. That is the first logarithm. The second logarithm comes from the partitions: the comparator’s movement on the tree must be at most order log k times its true movement. Heavy balls protect concentrated requests, and other requests use chronological rosters in tiers. The key estimate telescopes ratios of ball masses across scales, so the losses add up to a single logarithm instead of one per scale. In our check, this ball-mass telescope held for 200 random measures. Finally, mass parked at a vertex is represented by an anchor point in the metric, and balanced rounding turns fractional amounts into exactly k servers, losing only a constant factor. Independent episodes remove the additive term, and compactness over all finite histories gives one policy on any metric, finite or not. Altogether: an absolute constant times log² k.

5:34Where it stands

The preprints are not peer reviewed and have not been revised. The Lean formalizations cover both theorems; we have not run them. Neither paper is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, the worst case of randomized k-server is settled up to constants: at most order log² k on every metric, and that much on some.
