Exactly enough?

Schrödinger convergence at the exact Sobolev endpoint

The claim, in plain words

Run the free Schrödinger equation from initial data f for a short time t. Does the solution return to f at almost every point as t goes to zero? Carleson asked this in 1980. For square-integrable data it returns on average, but pointwise convergence can fail on a set of positive measure; the question is how much smoothness, measured by the Sobolev space Hs, guarantees it.

The obstruction is speed. Each frequency travels at a speed proportional to its size: in one dimension, a packet at frequency N with spread √N moves at speed 2N and stays together for a time of about 1/N, long enough to sweep across a whole unit interval. Carleson proved that s = 1/4 suffices in one dimension, and Dahlberg and Kenig showed that nothing less does. In dimension n, Bourgain’s 2016 counterexample showed that s must be at least n/(2(n+1)), which is 1/3 in the plane; Du, Guth and Li proved convergence for every s above 1/3 in the plane, and Du and Zhang for every s above n/(2(n+1)) in higher dimensions. Equality itself was open.

Result 080 is two OpenAI preprints, both dated September 24, 2026: 83 pages for the plane and 96 pages for dimensions three and up. They claim the endpoint: for every f in Hn/(2(n+1))(ℝn), the solution converges to f at almost every point. The evolution is defined as a limit of Gaussian-smoothed solutions, taken before t goes to zero, on one full-measure set of points and for every time between 0 and 1, and the limit is the value of f at its Lebesgue points. The key estimate bounds the integral over any unit ball of the supremum in time of the solution by the Hs norm at the exact exponent.

What it doesn’t claim

The planar paper does not claim the stronger endpoint maximal estimate with local L3 output. Below the endpoint, Bourgain’s counterexample already rules out convergence, so if the papers hold, convergence for every f in Hs(ℝn) holds exactly when s ≥ n/(2(n+1)).

How the proof goes

  1. A transverse power saving. The planar proof works with packets of width L lasting time L2, and a measure models the area of a time graph that picks where the supremum is attained. Multilinear restriction and Du and Zhang’s refined fractal estimate reduce a saturated transverse configuration to weighted incidences between points and tubes; information profiles across scales have slopes forced to 0 or 1 by a robust projection theorem, and polynomial partitioning excludes the last jump.
  2. A bilinear frame estimate. For packets with separated frequency groups, positive masks remove large pieces of a tight frequency frame, charged to lost energy, and a quartic potential with the curvature identity of the parabola controls changes in the spatial smoothing scale. The estimate is uniform in the number of scales; with flat-frequency and rich-tube reductions it gives a distribution bound with an exponent above 2 and no growing loss.
  3. Square summation at the endpoint. All frequencies are linearized on one time graph, and a binary time tree separates heavier children from lighter siblings. Spatial localization gives decay in relative depth and the exponent above 2 gives a convexity deficit, so the Sobolev square norm is kept with no small loss. In higher dimensions, a transverse frame estimate and a geometric induction to polynomial walls, whose relevant pieces are flat plates, replace the planar steps.

0:00Carleson’s problem

The free Schrödinger equation moves a wave without friction, and each frequency travels at a speed proportional to its size. Start from initial data f and run the evolution for a short time t. In 1980, Carleson asked when the solution returns to f at almost every point as t goes to zero. For square-integrable data it returns on average, but pointwise convergence can fail on a set of positive measure. The question is how much smoothness, measured by the Sobolev space Hˢ, guarantees convergence everywhere except on a set of measure zero. The obstruction is speed. In one dimension, a packet at frequency N with frequency spread √N moves at speed 2N, and it stays together for a time of about 1/N, long enough to sweep across a whole unit interval. So the supremum over time is about √N on that interval, while the Hˢ norm is about N^(s+1/4). In our computation, the ratio grows like N^(1/4−s): it stays bounded exactly when s is at least 1/4. Carleson proved that 1/4 suffices in one dimension, and Dahlberg and Kenig showed that nothing less does. In higher dimensions, Sjölin and Vega independently proved convergence above 1/2. In the plane, the threshold came down through work of Bourgain, of Moyua, Vargas and Vega, of Tao and Vargas, and of Tao, to Lee’s 3/8. In 2016, Bourgain built a counterexample showing that n/(2(n+1)) derivatives are needed in dimension n, which is 1/3 in the plane. Du, Guth and Li proved convergence above 1/3 in the plane, and Du and Zhang proved it above n/(2(n+1)) in every higher dimension. In one dimension the same formula gives Carleson’s 1/4, where the endpoint holds. In higher dimensions, equality itself was open.

Result 080 is two OpenAI preprints, both dated September 24: 83 pages for the plane, and 96 pages for dimensions three and up. They claim the endpoint: for every f in H^(n/(2(n+1))), the solution converges to f at almost every point. The evolution is defined as a limit of Gaussian-smoothed solutions, taken before t goes to zero, on one full-measure set of points and for every time between zero and one, and the limit is the value of f at its Lebesgue points. The key estimate bounds the integral, over any unit ball, of the supremum in time of the solution by the Hˢ norm, at the exact exponent. The higher-dimensional paper uses lemmas and the time-tree mechanism of the planar paper, though not its planar conclusion. It also notes that Singh and Singh Parmar found a mismatch in a published packet estimate, from work on Falconer’s distance problem, and proved a corrected version in ambient dimension two; the paper proves the version it needs in every dimension. The planar paper does not claim the stronger endpoint estimate with local L³ output.

3:09Three ingredients

The planar proof works with packets of width L that last for time L², and a measure models the area of a time graph that picks where the supremum is attained. First, a power saving for transverse packets. Multilinear restriction and the refined fractal estimate of Du and Zhang reduce a saturated configuration to weighted incidences between points and tubes. Information profiles across scales then have slopes forced to zero or one by a robust projection theorem of Weikun He; a further argument rules out unboundedly many alternations, and polynomial partitioning excludes the last jump. Second, a bilinear estimate for packets with separated frequency groups and general smooth profiles. Positive masks remove large pieces of a tight frequency frame, charging them to the lost energy, and a quartic potential, with the curvature identity of the parabola, controls changes in the spatial smoothing scale. The estimate is uniform in the number of scales, which the long, nearly flat frequency configurations require. With flat-frequency and rich-tube reductions, this gives a distribution bound with an exponent above two and no growing loss. Third, square summation at the endpoint. All frequencies are linearized on the same time graph, and a binary time tree separates heavier children from their lighter siblings. Spatial localization gives decay in the relative depth of the tree, and the exponent above two gives a convexity deficit that sums the lighter masses across frequency scales, so the Sobolev square norm is kept with no small loss. In higher dimensions, a frame estimate for transverse packets and a geometric induction that reduces to polynomial walls whose relevant pieces are flat plates replace the planar steps, with the same time tree.

5:03Where it stands

Neither preprint is peer reviewed, and neither has been revised. There is no full Lean proof: uncatalogued Lean code checks only the planar paper’s final step, taking its main estimate as given. The higher-dimensional paper relies on lemmas from the planar one. Outside inputs include multilinear restriction, the fractal estimate of Du and Zhang, a projection theorem of Weikun He, polynomial partitioning and decoupling. Neither paper is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If they hold, Carleson’s problem has its exact Sobolev answer in every dimension: convergence for every f in Hˢ holds exactly when s is at least n/(2(n+1)), endpoint included, as Carleson found in one dimension. The stronger endpoint estimate with local L³ output in the plane is not claimed.
