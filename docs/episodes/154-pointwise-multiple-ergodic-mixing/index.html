Mixing is enough?

A claim that, for an invertible mixing transformation, multiple ergodic averages converge at almost every starting point, for every number of functions

The claim, in plain words

Take a transformation T that shuffles a space while preserving probability, start at a point x and follow its orbit. At step k, look at the points Tkx, T2kx, …, Tnkx, evaluate bounded functions f1, …, fn there, multiply, and average over k from 1 to N. These are multiple ergodic averages. If T is mixing (any two events become nearly independent when far enough apart in time), one might expect the averages to approach the product of the integrals of the functions. The question is whether that happens at almost every single starting point, not just with an error that is small on average over starting points.

As the paper recounts, these averages became central to ergodic Ramsey theory through Furstenberg’s proof of Szemerédi’s theorem. Host and Kra proved convergence in mean square for every number of functions on every system, and Ziegler gave another proof. “The distinction between norm and pointwise convergence is substantial.” Bourgain’s double recurrence theorem handles two functions pointwise; longer products needed extra structure: K-systems, two classes of weakly mixing systems, and, by Huang, Shao and Ye, ergodic measure-distal systems. The paper calls no question open and makes no priority claim.

Result 154 is an OpenAI preprint (25 pages, October 4, 2026), listed with three companion preprints of the same date. Its Theorem 1.1: if T is invertible and mixing, then for every n ≥ 2 and every fixed tuple of bounded functions, the averages converge almost everywhere to the product of the integrals, as N runs through all positive integers. “The probability space need not be standard, and no rate of mixing is required.” The exceptional null set may depend on the system and the tuple. The companions claim the cases of three and four functions for mixing systems; this paper extends the argument to every number.

What it doesn’t claim, and what it rests on

The theorem says nothing about systems that are only weakly mixing or ergodic, about commuting transformations or polynomial times, and it gives no rate of convergence; the proof is by contradiction and not effective.

It builds on three results in the same release, which it says are “not reproduced here”: two of its listed companions (fourfold averages, 36 pages; triple averages at distinct slopes, 43 pages) and result 145 (mixing of all orders). Those two companions rely in turn on the third listed companion (triple averages for mixing transformations, 61 pages) and on result 86, an L3 bound for the trilinear Hilbert transform (93 pages). OSVerify’s review of result 86 reported one major issue, in its Lemma 8.6, with a proposed repair; the distinct-slopes companion imports that lemma by number. Counting result 145, the three companions and result 86, the papers involved total about 300 pages.

Flagged for expert review. Along the way (Lemma 2.5), the paper derives from a companion’s estimate that “on any standard invertible probability-preserving system, averages of at most three bounded functions at distinct positive integer slopes converge almost everywhere along all positive integer lengths”, mixing or not. That is a strong claim of its own, which the catalog does not headline; the proof’s small-slot step uses it.

How the proof goes

  1. Record a failure as a law. Suppose the averages fail to approach the product on a set of positive measure. Then a centered average rises above a fixed level at arbitrarily late lengths. An adversary picks, for each starting point, one of those bad lengths; averaging the configuration of n points up to the selected lengths (on a suspension flow) and passing to a limit along an ultrafilter gives a selected law on n copies of a system, carrying a positive correlation. Averages of at most three slots converge anyway, by the flagged three-function claim, and so do those of the structured, distal part, so selection cannot change their laws.
  2. Channels. A length chosen from the starting point can bias other coordinates. The paper sorts them into three groups and asks that the original coordinate be independent of any two groups, though not necessarily of all three: a channel. The key lemma is that selecting again keeps a channel a channel. Mixing of all orders makes the relevant vectors nearly orthogonal outside a graph of bounded degree, and a tensor packing theorem says that a subspace of moderately growing dimension captures a substantial part of only a small fraction of such a family. The rest is bounded by the chosen dimension alone, and those bounds are summable over doubling lengths, so no rate of mixing is needed.
  3. A smallest counterexample, and a square. Take a nonzero bad correlation with the fewest slots; given the structured part, laws of at most three slots are products, so it uses at least four, and up to three functions are already done. Among those, take one with the most output slots, and saturate the system: conditioning on the structured part, alone or with one slot, cannot be improved by any extension. Selecting once more and regrouping the other slots into three groups gives a square of states whose chosen row and column both have the old law, for different reasons. Saturation gives an exact formula for their correlation, and a random vector almost surely orthogonal to an independent copy of itself must be zero. So the correlation survives with one more output slot, a contradiction.

The numbers in the video

  • A coin-flipping shift (mixing, and a K-system, so already covered before this paper), three functions reading the flips: the average along one orbit approaches the product of the integrals, 1/16 (our run, N up to 105).
  • Median distance from the product over 12 random starts, for two to six functions: 1.2 × 10−3 to 8.5 × 10−3 at N = 103, and 3.8 × 10−5 to 3.7 × 10−4 at N = 106.
  • Our adversary (our reading of the paper’s selectors) picks the worst length in each doubling range: its best average shrinks from about 0.12 at lengths 16 to 31 to about 0.0013 at lengths 262,144 to 524,287.
  • An irrational rotation (not mixing), with f1 = cos 4πx and f2 = cos 2πx: the averages settle to ½ cos 2πx, which depends on the start, while the product of the integrals is 0.
  • Three fair signs U, V, W and their product: the product is independent of any two signs, yet fixed by all three.
  • Pictures cannot prove almost-everywhere convergence; the paper itself contains no computations.
