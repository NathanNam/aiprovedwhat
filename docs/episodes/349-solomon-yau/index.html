The next smallest?

An AI preprint claims the Solomon–Yau least-volume conjecture in every dimension

The claim, in plain words

A minimal hypersurface of a round sphere has zero mean curvature: every small piece balances its bending, like a soap film. The simplest is an equator, a great sphere. Next come Clifford products, one round sphere times another, with radii squared k/m and (m − k)/m, where our computer checks that the mean curvature vanishes. In the three-sphere this is the Clifford torus, with area 2π², against 4π for the equator. In every dimension the smallest of these products is the balanced one, which our computer confirms up to dimension 200; its volume over the equator’s is π/2 for surfaces, about 1.54 one dimension up, and exactly 3/2 in dimension four.

The question is whether the Clifford products are the next step up: does every closed minimal hypersurface other than an equator have at least the smallest Clifford volume? It is Problem 31 in Yau’s 1992 problem list, where Yau notes that Bruce Solomon thought so, and it is also called the Solomon–Yau conjecture. For surfaces in the three-sphere, Marques and Neves proved it in 2012, in their work on the Willmore conjecture. In higher dimensions only special cases were known: rotationally symmetric or antipodally symmetric hypersurfaces, and non-embedded ones, whose volume is at least twice the equator’s, already above the Clifford value. In 2025 Perdomo wrote that it had been fully verified only for surfaces.

Result 349 is one OpenAI paper, 32 pages, dated September 23 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims the conjecture in every dimension m from two up: a closed connected minimal immersion into the unit sphere whose image is not an equator has volume at least that of the smallest minimal Clifford product, counting covering multiplicity. Along the way it proves an average-curvature inequality conjectured by Perdomo, and that Morse index m + 3 forces a Clifford product; a preprint by Anass Nifa, which the paper cites, also gives proofs of those two steps.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: that only Clifford products reach the bound, since the paper states no equality case; the separate eigenvalue and Chern conjectures; or a machine-checked proof. Surfaces in the three-sphere were already known; the new part is dimension three and up.

How the proof goes

  1. Narrowing down a counterexample. First, a counterexample would have to be embedded, since non-embedded hypersurfaces are already too large. Comparing a two-point curvature maximum with a solution of a Poisson equation gives the inequality: the average squared curvature is at least m. Balancing arguments then show that only Clifford products have Morse index exactly m + 3, and our computer checks that every minimal Clifford product up to dimension 10 does, against index one for the equator. So a counterexample has index at least m + 4.
  2. Min-max below the candidate. Next comes min-max. A family built from Lorentz boosts applied to the signed distance from the cone over a candidate hypersurface, sliced by the unit sphere, sweeps out the sphere; on the Clifford torus our computer checks that the unboosted slices’ area peaks only at the center. The extra unstable direction pushes the family’s maximum below the candidate’s volume, and Marques and Neves min-max theory then yields a smaller minimal hypersurface. An induction on dimension, using cones of low density, keeps everything smooth, even in dimension seven and above. A least counterexample would have a smaller one, a contradiction.
