Square-root law?

Three AI preprints claim Yau’s nodal bound on surfaces, and counterexamples in higher dimensions

The claim, in plain words

Vibrate a surface at one of its resonant frequencies and some points stay still: the nodal set, where an eigenfunction of the Laplacian vanishes. On a flat torus of side 2π, our computer finds that the nodal length of sine m x times sine n y is exactly 4π(m + n), so its ratio to the square root of the eigenvalue always lies between 4π and 4π√2. On the round sphere, the same ratio for the standard spherical harmonics runs from about four to about 2π. Higher frequencies make longer nodal sets, growing like the square root of the eigenvalue.

Yau conjectured that this is always the rate: on a closed smooth manifold with a fixed metric, the size of the nodal set lies between two constants times the square root of the eigenvalue. Donnelly and Fefferman proved both bounds for real-analytic metrics in 1988. For smooth metrics, Logunov proved the lower bound in every dimension, after Brüning on surfaces, but the upper bound stayed open. On surfaces the best known was a power just below 3/4, by Logunov and Malinnikova, and in higher dimensions only Logunov’s unspecified power above one half.

Result 350 is three OpenAI papers, all dated September 23 and made public October 6, 2026. The first, 26 pages, claims the upper bound on every smooth closed surface, which together with the known lower bound completes Yau’s conjecture there. The second, 60 pages, claims it fails in dimensions three and four: arbitrarily close to the round three-sphere there is a fixed smooth metric with a sequence of eigenfunctions whose nodal areas outgrow any constant times the square root of the eigenvalue, and likewise on a sphere times a torus. The third, 34 pages, claims a fixed metric in dimension five, on a four-sphere times a circle, where nodal measure beats the square root by a fixed positive power.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: failure for round or real-analytic metrics, where the known theorems stand; any rate in dimensions three and four; or a sizable power, since the exponent is never computed, and the one its proof gives is, by our reading of the constants, below 2 in 10 million.

How the proof goes

  1. Surfaces: average the growth. On surfaces, the proof chops the surface into squares the size of a wavelength. A local theorem of Roy-Fortin bounds the nodal length in each square by how fast the eigenfunction grows there, so it is enough to bound the average growth. The paper shows that, where growth is large, subtracting an affine tilt halves the scale-adjusted growth on average at each subdivision, by an argument special to two dimensions.
  2. Higher dimensions: ripples that add up. For the counterexamples, start from an explicit wave on the round sphere whose nodal set is a fan of half great spheres; our computer checks that its size grows at the square-root rate, with ratio tending to 4π. Then corrugate the wave’s envelope in two directions: a ripple can be tiny in height while its slope stays large, so nodal sheets pile up. Change the metric so the corrugated wave becomes an exact eigenfunction, make the eigenvalue simple so the wave survives small changes, and nest infinitely many such steps into one smooth metric.
