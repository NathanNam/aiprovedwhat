How sensitive can it be?

The claim, in plain words

Take the Boolean cube: all strings of n pluses and minuses. A polynomial threshold function of degree d labels each corner by the sign of a real polynomial of degree at most d; degree one gives a halfspace, such as majority. Its average sensitivity, also called total influence, is the expected number of coordinates that change the label when flipped one at a time, at a random corner. Majority has average sensitivity about √(2/π)·√n. Taking the sign of a degree-d polynomial in the number of pluses, with one root in each of the d gaps between levels closest to the middle, gives about d√n, as long as d is at most about √n.

In 1994 Gotsman and Linial conjectured that these symmetric functions are the extreme ones. That exact form was refuted by Chapman, and independently by Kim, Maldonado and Wellens. The asymptotic form, average sensitivity at most a constant times d√n, stayed open. Harsha, Klivans and Meka, and Diakonikolas, Raghavendra, Servedio and Tan, proved bounds growing more slowly than n for each fixed degree; Kane reached √n up to factors that grow with d.

Result 127 is one OpenAI preprint (13 pages, September 25, 2026). It claims that every polynomial threshold function of degree at most d on n variables has average sensitivity at most 8d√n, for every n and every d from 1 to n. The polynomial may vanish on the cube, with the sign of zero taken as +1, and no regularity is assumed; the constant is absolute and d may grow with n. With the symmetric examples, this pins the largest possible average sensitivity at order d√n whenever d is at most √n; beyond that the trivial bound n takes over. The paper also derives a noise-sensitivity bound of at most a constant times d√η at noise rate η, and an agnostic learning algorithm under the uniform distribution.

What it doesn’t claim

The paper makes no claim that the constant 8 is the best possible; the symmetric examples sit near 0.8, ten times lower. It does not determine which functions are exactly extremal; the original symmetric guess is already known to fail. And the bound adds something only when n exceeds 64d², since average sensitivity is never more than n.

How the proof goes

  1. A one-sided coupling lemma. If U and V have the same distribution, with variance σ², and V − U is never more than a, then E(U − V)² ≤ 8aσ, even though U − V may be large. The function g(t) = ½(t − m)|t − m|, a primitive of the distance to the mean m, has increments of at least a quarter of the squared gap; equal laws make its expected change zero, so its total change is twice its upward change, and each upward step is at most a times the distance to the mean, which averages to at most σ.
  2. A weighted grading. Multiply the polynomials of degree at most k by the square root of a positive weight and orthogonalize: the layers have dimension C(n, k). Give the k-th layer the eigenvalue k − n/2. With the constant weight this operator is −½ times the cube’s adjacency matrix; for any weight its squared Hilbert–Schmidt norm is n·2ⁿ/4, and multiplying by a coordinate links each layer only to itself and its neighbours. Together these show that each edge entry has size at most ½, and that the total shortfall on edges is at most twice the squared mass on the diagonal.
  3. Edges from the anticommutator. For a sign function h, the anticommutator with multiplication by h kills every entry where h differs and doubles, up to sign, every entry where h agrees, so the number of edges where h agrees is at most twice its squared norm. With h equal to f times the full parity, those edges are exactly the sensitive edges of f.
  4. The binomial coupling. Take the weight to be |p|, after a tiny shift that removes zeros without changing signs. Multiplying p by parity moves its Fourier support to degrees at least n − d, so the blocks of h between layers r and s vanish when r + s < n − d. Their squared norms form a law on pairs of layers with binomial marginals; with U = s and V = n − r, the vanishing says V − U ≤ d, and the anticommutator’s squared norm over 2ⁿ is E(U − V)². The lemma with σ = √n/2 bounds this by 4d√n, and the edge count gives 8d√n.

The numbers in the video

  • The symmetric examples, for n = 25 to 1,600 and d = 1, 2, 5, have average sensitivity between 0.69 and 0.81 times d√n, close to √(2/π) ≈ 0.798 when d is small compared with √n.
  • The worst coupling of two Binomial(100, ½) variables with V − U at most a, found by linear programming, has expected squared gap 15.0, 28.2, 49.6 and 76.9 for a = 1, 2, 4, 8, against the lemma’s bounds 40, 80, 160 and 320.
  • On cubes of up to 7 dimensions, with 12 random polynomials for each (n, d), rebuilding the weighted layers confirms the key identities, the binomial coupling and the chain I(f) ≤ 2‖T‖²/2ⁿ ≤ 8d√n; the largest average sensitivities were 1.50 to 2.94, against bounds of 16 to 58.8.

3:07How the proof goes

The proof has two parts. The first is a probability lemma. Let U and V have the same distribution, with variance σ², and suppose V − U is never more than a. Then the expected squared gap between them is at most 8aσ, even though U − V may be large. The trick is a primitive of the distance to the mean, ½(t − m)|t − m|, whose increments dominate a quarter of the squared gap; equal laws make its expected change zero, so its total change is twice its upward change, and each upward step is at most a times the distance to the mean, which averages to at most σ. In our check, the worst coupling of two binomials with 100 trials and V − U at most one gives an expected squared gap of about 15, against a bound of 40. The second part is an operator on functions on the cube. Take the polynomials of degree at most k, multiply them by the square root of a positive weight, and orthogonalize: you get layers of dimension C(n, k). Give the k-th layer the eigenvalue k − n/2. With the constant weight, this operator is minus one half times the adjacency matrix of the cube. For any weight, its squared Hilbert–Schmidt norm is fixed, at n·2ⁿ/4, and multiplying by a coordinate only links each layer to itself and its immediate neighbours. These two facts show that each edge entry has size at most 1/2, and that the total shortfall on edges is at most twice the squared mass on the diagonal. For a sign function h, the anticommutator of this operator with multiplication by h kills every entry between corners where h differs, and doubles, up to sign, every entry where h agrees, including the diagonal and those edges. So the number of edges where h agrees is at most twice its squared norm. Now multiply f by the full parity: the edges where the new function agrees are exactly the sensitive edges of f. Take the weight to be the absolute value of the polynomial, after a tiny shift that removes zeros without changing any sign. Multiplying the polynomial by parity moves its Fourier support to degrees at least n − d. Since h times the weight is exactly this product, the blocks of multiplication by h between layers r and s vanish whenever r + s is less than n − d. The squared norms of the remaining blocks form a probability law on pairs of layers, with both marginals binomial, with n trials and probability one half. Reflect one index: U is the index s, and V is n − r. They have the same law, and the vanishing blocks say that V − U is at most d. The anticommutator’s blocks are those of h, scaled by r + s − n, so its squared norm over 2ⁿ is exactly the expected squared gap between U and V. The lemma, with σ = √n/2, bounds that gap by 4d√n, and the edge count turns this into 8d√n. In our check, we rebuilt the weighted layers on cubes of up to 7 dimensions, with random polynomials, and checked the key identities, the binomial coupling and the whole chain of inequalities: all held.

6:29Where it stands

The preprint is not peer reviewed and has not been revised. The Lean formalization covers the main theorem; we have not run it. The paper is not among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, the asymptotic Gotsman–Linial conjecture is settled: polynomial threshold functions of degree d have average sensitivity at most a constant times d√n, with an absolute constant, by a short argument. The paper does not determine which functions are exactly extremal; the original symmetric guess is already known to fail.
