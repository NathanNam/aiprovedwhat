The claims, in plain words

A matroid captures the idea of independence, like forests in a graph or linearly independent vectors. Its maximal independent sets, the bases, all have the same size. Two matroids on the same ground set can share bases. When no vertex is isolated, the perfect matchings of a bipartite graph are exactly the common bases of two partition matroids, each allowing at most one edge at every vertex on one side. Edmonds showed how to find a largest common independent set in polynomial time, using only an oracle that answers whether a set is independent.

Counting is harder. A fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS) gets within any relative error ε with probability at least 1 − δ, in time polynomial in the input size, 1/ε and log(1/δ). Anari, Liu, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant gave one for the bases of a single matroid, using log-concave polynomials; Jerrum, Sinclair and Vigoda’s permanent algorithm covers bipartite perfect matchings. For two arbitrary matroids given by independence oracles, Anari, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant gave a deterministic approximation within a factor 2O(r), exponential in the rank r. An FPRAS for two oracle matroids was recorded as an open problem in Liu’s dissertation, and Cryan, Guo and Mousa asked for a rapidly mixing chain.

Result 114 is two OpenAI preprints. The first, 26 pages, dated September 23, 2026, claims an FPRAS for the number of common bases of any two matroids of the same rank, given by independence oracles: on every execution, the numbers of oracle calls and bit operations are polynomial in the ground-set size, the input length, 1/ε and log(1/δ), and a zero count gives zero. It also covers common independent sets of a prescribed size, of any size, or of maximum size, even when the ranks differ. The second, 42 pages, dated October 5, 2026, extends this, together with a companion contingency-table preprint, to common integer bases of two polymatroids of equal total rank given by rank-value oracles, with capacities written in binary and each integer vector counted once.

What it doesn’t claim

The count is approximate and randomized, not exact. The guarantee is in the oracle model: the matroids are accessed only through independence queries, and the bounds count both the queries and the bit operations around them. The polymatroid extension relies on a separate preprint and is not part of the Lean formalization.

How the proof goes

  1. Two copies of the ground set. Following Anari, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant, put the first matroid on one copy and the dual of the second on the other, K = M1 ⊕ M2*. A transversal chooses one element from every pair; the transversal that takes the first copy on a set A and the second copy elsewhere is a basis of K exactly when A is a common basis.
  2. Weights and a chain. Every transversal gets weight qd, where d is its rank deficiency. At q = 1 the total is 2n; as q shrinks, it approaches the number of common bases. The algorithm lowers q in small steps and estimates each ratio by sampling. The chain also visits defective states, with one empty pair and one doubly occupied pair, whose total weights are balanced by learned multipliers, as in the permanent algorithm; it moves by one-element exchanges with Metropolis acceptance.
  3. The algebraic engine. By a theorem of Brändén and Huh, for 0 < q ≤ 1 a matroid’s homogeneous multivariate Tutte polynomial is Lorentzian, so every quadratic obtained from it by derivatives and nonnegative substitutions has at most one positive eigenvalue. Applied to the paired matroid and its dual, this gives inequalities between defect totals, such as 4·cij·cji ≤ c02, and a transport inequality comparing states that differ in which slot is missing.
  4. From inequalities to an algorithm. The chain’s visits to transversals mix rapidly, and a separate variance bound covers the defect indicators and ratio observables. Fresh restarts along the schedule give warm starts, even though the multipliers are learned from earlier observations, and a bit-level implementation bounds the work on every execution.

The numbers in the video

  • A 3-by-3 bipartite graph had 3 common bases, matching its permanent; two random binary matroids of rank 4 on 9 elements shared 8 bases.
  • For the paired reduction, all 512 subsets of the 9-element example agreed.
  • The total transversal weight fell from 512 at q = 1 to 142.25, 22.24, 9.20 and 8.01 at q = 0.5, 0.1, 0.01 and 1/10,000, against 8 common bases.
  • The defect inequality held for every pair of the 9-element example, with largest ratios 4·cij·cji/c02 of 0.25, 0.39 and 0.59 at q = 1, 0.3 and 0.05.
  • All 57 derivative Hessians for the graphic matroid of the complete graph K4, at three values of q, had at most one positive eigenvalue.

The proof starts from a reduction of Anari, Oveis Gharan and Vinzant. Make two copies of the ground set, put the first matroid on one copy and the dual of the second on the other, and choose one element from every pair: a transversal. The transversal that takes the first copy on a set A and the second copy elsewhere is a basis of this direct sum exactly when A is a common basis. In our check, this held for all 512 subsets of a 9-element example. Every transversal gets weight q^(rank deficiency). At q = 1 the total is 2^n; as q shrinks, it approaches the number of common bases. The algorithm lowers q in small steps and estimates each ratio by sampling. The chain also visits defective states, with one empty pair and one doubly occupied pair, whose total weights are balanced by learned multipliers, as in the permanent algorithm, and it moves by one-element exchanges. Applied to the paired matroid and its dual, this gives inequalities between defect totals, such as 4·c_ij·c_ji ≤ c_0², and a transport inequality comparing states that differ in which slot is missing. These bounds control what the algorithm actually measures. The chain’s visits to transversals mix rapidly, and a separate variance bound covers the defect indicators and ratio observables. 5:15Where it stands

The preprints are not peer reviewed and have not been revised. The Lean formalization covers the matroid FPRAS with its oracle and bit bounds, but not the common-independent-set results or the polymatroid extension; we have not run it. Neither paper is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, common bases of any two matroids can be counted approximately in randomized polynomial time, answering a problem left open after the single-matroid breakthrough.
