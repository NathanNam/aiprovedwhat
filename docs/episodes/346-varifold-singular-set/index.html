Smooth almost everywhere?

Two AI preprints claim the singular-set conjectures for stationary varifolds

The claim, in plain words

A varifold is a generalized surface: it can branch, cross itself, and count some sheets more than once. It is stationary if no small push changes its area to first order, a weaker condition than being stable or area-minimizing. Three rays meeting at 120 degrees are stationary; our computer checks this, and that an unbalanced junction fails. Two planes crossing along a line are stationary too, and so is a Y-shaped junction times a line; for these two-dimensional examples the singular points form a line, one dimension lower. In 1972, Allard showed that the smooth points of a stationary integral varifold are open and dense.

The question is how big the singular set can be. Points whose tangent cones include no plane were already known to form a set of dimension at most m − 1, and points of density one are smooth by Allard. The hard points are where the varifold looks like one plane counted several times. Is the singular set at least of zero m-dimensional size? De Lellis called this perhaps the hardest open problem in the area, and Brena, Decio and De Lellis recorded both measure zero and dimension at most m − 1 as conjectures, posted in 2025. There were partial results, and the one-dimensional case was classical. For area-minimizing currents, Almgren had proved the stronger bound m − 2.

Result 346 is two OpenAI papers. The first, 77 pages, dated September 23, claims the measure-zero conjecture: in any open subset of Euclidean space, in every dimension and codimension, a stationary integral varifold is smooth except on a set of zero m-dimensional measure, and the same holds on round spheres. The second, 35 pages, dated October 5 and building on the first, claims the dimension bound: in Euclidean space the singular set has Hausdorff dimension at most m − 1, which is stronger. Both were made public October 6, 2026. The bound is sharp, because two crossing planes reach it.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: general curved ambient spaces, where examples by De Lellis, Hirsch, Lihn and Spolaor show the geometry matters; varifolds with nonzero but bounded mean curvature, where the statement can fail; explicit constants; or a machine-checked proof. The only related Lean file formalizes one final covering step, with everything hard assumed.

An AI audit of the first paper found no gap, but said it only skimmed the core of the signed-excess proof. De Lellis, who co-wrote the paper recording both conjectures, is a member of the group that advised OpenAI on the release; we found no statement from him on these papers, no specialist comment and no reported error. How the proof goes

  1. Keep the sign. The first paper’s engine is a signed excess estimate. Near a plane counted several times, compare the extra area with the tilt. For a single tilted sheet, our computer finds that twice the extra area minus the tilt is tiny and positive, about a quarter of the slope to the fourth power, while a hole makes it large and negative. Keeping that sign, the paper shows that twice the extra area minus the tilt is at most the excess times a factor smaller than any power of one over its logarithm, by induction on the number of sheets.
  2. From fits to regularity. It then fits smooth minimal graphs at every scale. A failed fit would force separated sheets with fewer layers, which are rare near a typical point, so failures fill a vanishing share. The fits glue into a smooth center, and a frequency argument forces the varifold onto it, so almost every point is regular. The second paper assumes the singular set has dimension above m − 1, picks an exponent s in between, and keeps a whole measure of bad points while blowing up at prescribed scales. They survive because the integral of t to the power s − m converges exactly when s exceeds m − 1, and a measure-valued dimension reduction then gives a contradiction.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: three rays at 120 degrees have first variation about 1e-12 (stationary); an unbalanced junction at 100, 130, 130 degrees does not.
  • Ours: densities of cones: a plane 1, a Y-junction times a line 1.5, the tetrahedral cone 1.824520, two crossing planes 2.
  • Ours: box-counting dimensions: Cantor set 0.631, segment 1, Sierpinski triangle 1.585, Sierpinski carpet 1.893, filled square 2.
  • Ours: for a single sheet tilted with slope s, twice the extra area minus the tilt is about s⁴/4 (2.475e-5 at s = 0.1); a hole makes it negative.
