AN08 (Specials; Beyond OpenAI; see also AN03 and 108), November 2026

VP ≠ VNP over every field?

A paper credited to Claude (Anthropic) claims that arithmetic circuits for the permanent and the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial need more than m^(c·(log m)^(1/3)) gates, which would prove Valiant’s conjecture over every field. Its Lean proof passes Anthropic’s own checks; no human mathematician has checked it, and it does not settle P vs NP.

The question

This is AN08. Before we start: Anthropic makes Claude, which helps make this series. This claim is Anthropic’s own: we hold it to the same standards as any lab’s, using only public sources. On November 23, 2026, Anthropic posted a paper and a Lean proof on GitHub, called VP ≠ VNP over every field. The author line reads Claude, Anthropic. The README says the result was discovered by an internal research Claude model, that the paper and the Lean were both written by AI models, and that no human mathematician has checked the proof. It claims to settle Leslie Valiant’s conjecture from 1979, the algebraic cousin of P versus NP. It does not settle P versus NP itself. So what is the question?

An arithmetic circuit builds a polynomial from its variables using only addition and multiplication. Write it as a list of gates, each one a variable, a constant, or the sum or the product of two earlier gates. Our example: x, y, x plus y, and that times itself. Four gates, and the last one is x plus y, squared. Multiplying it out first and adding up the pieces, our way, takes nine gates. The size of a circuit is its number of gates, and the question is how few gates can do the job. A circuit must produce the polynomial itself, not just the right values on zeros and ones. That is what separates it from the Boolean circuits of P versus NP.

Two polynomials sit at the center. For a 2 by 2 matrix, the determinant is ad − bc, and the permanent is ad + bc. For 3 by 3, both have the same six terms, and the determinant puts minus signs on three of them. Gaussian elimination computes determinants fast: for a 20 by 20 matrix of numbers, about 5,000 operations in our count. Nothing like it is known for the permanent. Multiplying out all its terms would take about 49 quintillion operations; Ryser’s formula cuts that to a few hundred million, but it still grows like 2^m. In 1979, Valiant defined two classes. VP holds families of polynomials, of polynomial degree, with circuits of polynomial size. VNP holds sums, over all strings of bits, of such polynomials. The permanent is complete for VNP, except in characteristic two, where it equals the determinant. The Hamiltonian cycle polynomial, a sum over the permutations that form one single cycle, is complete over every field. Valiant conjectured that neither has circuits of polynomial size.

The claim, in plain words

The claim. The paper has 70 pages. Its main theorem: there is an absolute constant c above zero such that, over every field, for all large enough m, every circuit for the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial of an m by m matrix, and every circuit for the permanent outside characteristic two, needs more than m^(c·(log m)^(1/3)) gates. The threshold for large enough depends only on the characteristic, and the paper gives no value for it. That bound grows faster than any fixed power of m, so neither family is in VP: VP is not VNP, over every field. A second theorem says the same for circuits that only approximate, in a limit, so VNP is not even in the closure of VP. A Lean file states all of this from Mathlib alone: circuits, both polynomials, both classes, and the four main statements.

In the paper’s notation (Theorem 1.1, p. 2): there is an absolute constant c > 0 such that for every p, zero or prime, there is m₀ such that over every field of characteristic p and for every m ≥ m₀, every circuit for HC_m, and every circuit for PERM_m if p ≠ 2, has more than m^(c (log m)^(1/3)) gates, with log = log₂. Equivalently 2^Ω((log m)^(4/3)). Corollary 1.2: VP ⊊ VNP over every field. Theorem 1.4: the same for approximating circuits (Definition 1.3, over Laurent series), so VNP ⊄ VP-bar. Statement.lean states four propositions (MainTheorem, MainTheoremApprox, Separation, SeparationClosure); Challenge.lean and Solution.lean add a fifth theorem, the bound for the real permanent (Theorem L.1). A lower bound “for an explicit polynomial” matters: counting arguments have long shown that most polynomials need large circuits, but not for any specific one like these.

What would be new, and what is not claimed

What would be new. For general circuits, with no limit on depth, the paper says the best earlier lower bound for an explicit polynomial was that of Strassen, and of Baur and Strassen, of order n log d. For the permanent, that is about m² log m. Stronger bounds were known only for restricted circuits. For example, Limaye, Srinivasan and Tavenas proved superpolynomial bounds, first published in 2021, for circuits of any constant depth, and Forbes extended them to all fields. This claim covers every circuit. But look how slowly it grows. In our chart, with an illustrative constant of one, it passes m² at m equal to 256, but m³ only beyond about 134 million. The constant the proof gives is far smaller: at most 1/2,560,000. The best known upper bounds grow exponentially; in the paper’s words, there is still plenty of room to tighten the bounds. What is not claimed: not P versus NP, and nothing about Boolean circuits. Nor that the exponent 1/3 is optimal.

How the proof goes

How the proof goes, by the paper’s overview. It turns circuits into geometry. A circuit with L gates splits the polynomial’s table of coefficients into pieces of rank at most L. Functions called spectral points, after Strassen’s work on tensors, turn ranks into distances, so a small circuit forces an inequality between certain distances. Then the proof splits into two cases, by a property of the field: a flat case, where Euclidean geometry is available, and a contracting case. The paper proves that at least one case holds, but not which, even for the real numbers. In the flat case, it places the points of a network of wires in Euclidean space, and that gives the bound for a polynomial that the permanent can express. The contracting case is indirect. A small circuit for the permanent would speed up a whole class of computations; a diagonal argument then gives a value V equal to 1 + V, which is impossible. The flat case gives the weaker bound, the one in the theorem. The permanent enters only by substitution, through Valiant’s completeness. No such reduction is known for the determinant, and that, the paper says, is where the proof would fail for it.

Two details from the paper: the spectral points it uses are known to exist only by Zorn’s lemma (p. 3), and it says it does not know which of the two hypotheses holds, even over the real numbers, nor that they are mutually exclusive (p. 6). The flat case gives 2^(c (log m)^(4/3)); the contracting case gives the stronger 2^(c (log m)²/log log m), with constants depending on an integer for which the proof gives no value, which is why Theorem 1.1 has no value for m₀ (p. 8).

The Lean development, and what we compared

The Lean development has 324 files and about 73,000 lines, in our text count. Our text search finds sorry only in the challenge file, which states the theorems without proofs for comparison, and no axiom declarations. Anthropic’s automated check on the merge commit passed: a full build, then the comparator, which matched the five theorems to the challenge file, with Lean’s own kernel and the independent kernel nanoda both accepting. We read its log; we did not build or run anything. We also compared the statement file with the paper, by reading. Circuits count variables and constants as gates, as the paper does. The polynomials and the bound match. VP and VNP match Valiant’s definitions as the paper states them. Approximation uses Laurent series constants, as in the paper’s definition. We did not read the 73,000 lines. And the README warns that no machine checks that the statement file says what is claimed; the paper says that was judged only by AI readers.

Our reading of Statement.lean against the paper (not a machine check): Computes is the paper’s circuit model (§1.3), counting variables and constants as gates; perm is written as Σ Π x(σ(i), i), the paper’s sum with σ replaced by its inverse, as Appendix Q notes; hc sums over permutations that are a single cycle on all points; Beyond uses log₂; MainTheorem has one constant c for all characteristics and m₀ depending only on the characteristic, as in Theorem 1.1; VP bounds variables, degree and circuit size by n^a + a; VNP sums g over all bit vectors with g in VP (the number of bits is bounded through g’s variable count); Approximates uses Laurent series constants, as in Definition 1.3. We did not read the 72,932 lines of proof, and we took Mathlib’s definitions at their standard meaning.

Where it stands

Public
On GitHub, anthropics/formal-math, folder vp-vnp (pull request #51, merged November 23, 2026, 13:43 PT; merge commit 11b6c7e), and on Hexagon as 2611.00954v1 (November 23; “Contributed by Anthropic”). Not on arXiv. We found no announcement on anthropic.com.
Peer reviewed
No.
Anthropic’s own Lean checks
The repository’s CI run on the merge commit passed: a build from scratch (sorry warnings only from Challenge.lean), then Comparator in a sandbox, with only propext, Quot.sound and Classical.choice permitted; its log reads “nanoda kernel accepts the solution”, “Lean default kernel accepts the solution” and “Your solution is okay!” (22:05 UTC). We read the log; we did not build or run anything.
Written proofs complete on paper
No, by the paper’s own account: four printed proofs that describe machines and programs “can be verified only in the Lean development”, so “the contracting case, and with it every main result, rests on Lean at these points” (p. 17). Remark N.6, the consequence for determinantal complexity, is the one numbered statement not formalized.
Checked by a human
No, by Anthropic’s account: the README says the paper and the Lean were “written by AI models, and no human mathematician has checked the proof”; Appendix Q says whether the statements say what is meant “was judged only by AI readers”. Hexagon’s listing labels the text “A mix of human-written and AI-generated text” and “Human understanding: some parts”, and its provenance note says Anthropic researchers worked with Claude to improve the presentation.
Expert review; outside rerun
None found as of November 23, 2026, about 17:00 PT (we looked at Scott Aaronson’s and the Computational Complexity blogs, Gil Kalai’s blog and the Lean Zulip public archive).

Last checked November 23, 2026, about 17:00 PT. Not built or run by us.

Also November 23, 2026 (not in the video): OpenAI added its own claimed proof that VP ≠ VNP over every field to github.com/openai/math, as result 379, “VP and VNP are different” (30 pages; OpenAI’s history file says the result was obtained on November 6, 2026). It claims a lower bound of exp(Ω((log n)^(5/4))) on border circuits for an explicit VNP family, carried over to the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial over every field and to the permanent outside characteristic two. By OpenAI’s scope note, its Lean proof covers the exact separation of VP and VNP, not the border bound. Its bibliography cites Anthropic’s 3-SAT paper (AN03) and OpenAI’s cubic bound (108), but not this paper. Not peer reviewed; not built or run by us.

Later, November 23, 2026, 6:40 pm PT: the complexity theorist Emanuele Viola wrote on his blog that the paper’s human-readable part “is very short and mostly points to the Lean formalization”. He does not review the proof.

Later, November 24, 2026 (not in the video): OpenAI says new papers in result 379 subsume its November 23 paper. “Paths, trees and grids separate Valiant’s hierarchy” claims VF ≠ VBP ≠ VP ≠ VNP over every field, and “Circuit lower bounds for Hamiltonian cycle and the permanent” claims exp(c(log N)²/(log log N)²) gates for the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial over every field and the permanent outside characteristic two, also for circuits that approximate only the degree-N coefficients. By OpenAI’s scope note, Lean covers the three separations and superpolynomial exact bounds for both polynomials, not this rate. These papers cite Anthropic’s 3-SAT paper (AN03), not this one. Not peer reviewed; not built or run by us.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: (x+y)² as a circuit of 4 gates (x, y, x+y, its square) and, multiplied out, 9 gates; gates counted as in the Lean file’s Computes (variables and constants count).
  • Ours: for a 2 by 2 matrix det = ad − bc, perm = ad + bc; for 3 by 3 the same 6 terms, 3 with minus signs in the determinant. Examples: [[1,2],[3,4]] has det -2 and perm 10; [[1,2,3],[4,5,6],[7,8,9]] has det 0 and perm 450.
  • Ours, a random 20 by 20 integer matrix: Gaussian elimination (exact fractions, with division) took 5,150 arithmetic operations; multiplying out all 20! terms would take about 4.9 × 10^19; an upper count for Ryser’s formula is 420,478,574 (an exact count of the plain formula, 210,763,774).
  • Ours: the Hamiltonian cycle polynomial has (m − 1)! terms (checked by brute force for m = 2 to 8: 1, 2, 6, 24, 120, 720, 5040).
  • Ours, illustrative c = 1 only (the paper gives no value of m0): log₂ of m^((log₂ m)^(1/3)) is (log₂ m)^(4/3), which passes 2·log₂ m at m = 256 and 3·log₂ m at m = 2^27 = 134,217,728. The constant the proof gives is at most 1/1600² = 1/2,560,000 (the flat-case bound of Theorem E.11, p. 37, and the proof of Theorem L.1, p. 60); with it, the bound passes m² only when log₂ m exceeds about 1.3 × 10^20.
  • Ours, text only: 324 Lean files, 72,932 lines; sorry only in Challenge.lean; no axiom declarations or native_decide; imports from outside the folder only Mathlib (commit 0df444a360ea, the v4.33.1 tag); Lean v4.33.1.
