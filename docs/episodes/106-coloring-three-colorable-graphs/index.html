The claim, in plain words

A proper coloring gives adjacent vertices different colors. If a graph can be colored with three colors, one color class holds at least a third of the vertices, and no two of them are adjacent: it is an independent set. Deciding whether a graph is three-colorable is NP-complete. But suppose it is promised to be: can we color it with four colors, or ten, or find an independent set of 1% of the vertices?

Algorithms do poorly: Bansal, Huang and Lee’s method uses O(n0.19539) colors, improving Kawarabayashi, Thorup and Yoneda, and newer randomized preprints report somewhat smaller exponents. On the hardness side, Khanna, Linial and Safra showed that four colors are NP-hard, Barto, Bulín, Krokhin and Opršal five, and Fei, Minzer and Wang’s recent theorem on 4-to-1 games, through reductions of Guruswami and Sandeep and of Krokhin, Opršal, Wrochna and Živný, gives any fixed number. Independent sets are a stronger question: small independent sets force many colors, but adding isolated vertices keeps the colors while making the independent sets huge. Earlier results reached it from conjectures (Dinur, Mossel and Regev; Braverman, Khot, Lifshitz and Minzer), for almost three-colorable graphs with density 1/9 (Dinur, Khot, Perkins and Safra), or with an eight-coloring promise (Fei, Minzer and Wang).

Result 106 is one OpenAI preprint (21 pages) dated September 24, 2026. For every fixed δ below 1/3 it gives a deterministic polynomial-time reduction from 3-SAT to graphs: satisfiable formulas give three-colorable graphs, and unsatisfiable ones give graphs in which every independent set has fewer than δ times the number of vertices. Since a graph colored with c colors has an independent set of at least a c-th of its vertices, coloring three-colorable graphs with any fixed number of colors is NP-hard.

What is new

The coloring consequence was already known through Fei, Minzer and Wang, and also follows from result 105. The new part is the independent-set bound under the full three-coloring promise, proved from ordinary label cover with no condition on the sizes of its projection fibers.

How the proof goes

  1. Phases on a circle. Start from label cover with perfect completeness and tiny value on unsatisfiable formulas. Build chains of question tuples, replacing one question at a time. A vertex is a point, drawn by a finite random experiment, that assigns a phase on the circle to every possible answer of a tuple; projections link points at distance zero, and an edge joins x and y when x is within 1/8 of y shifted by half a turn.
  2. Completeness. If the formula is satisfiable, evaluating every point at the satisfying answer gives each vertex one phase, and adjacent vertices get phases at least 3/8 apart, so the three arcs [0, 1/3), [1/3, 2/3), [2/3, 1) color the graph properly.
  3. Few coordinates matter. An independent set gives bounded functions on the phase spaces that flip sign under the half turn and share a Lipschitz bound. Austin’s junta theorem, a continuous counterpart of Friedgut’s, approximates them using a bounded number of answer coordinates, however many there are. Coordinate lists are chosen for whole stretches of a chain, with random rotations at every layer; two separated stretches sharing an answer would satisfy a label cover test in between, which low label cover value makes rare.
  4. A density bound. An alignment lemma (finite minimax, Ramsey-type homogenization, random arrays) assigns each listed label, on most sampled sets of layers, one index shared by all its occurrences. Coefficients sampled at finitely many levels with geometrically decreasing probabilities, and a bound on influential coordinates, give a small mean square and hence a small independent set. The random experiment is expanded exactly into unweighted vertices, so the reduction is deterministic.

The numbers in the video

  • A random 15-vertex graph with a planted three-coloring (42 edges): chromatic number 3, color classes of 5, largest independent set 6.
  • K4 plus isolated vertices keeps 4 colors while its independence ratio rises: 0.700, 0.970, 0.997.
  • The completeness rule on 20,000 random pairs of phases: 5,071 edges, every one joining phases at least 3/8 apart, all properly colored by the three arcs.
  • n0.19539, ignoring the constant: about 15 for a million vertices, 57 for a billion.

3:12How the proof goes

The proof starts from label cover with perfect completeness and tiny value on unsatisfiable formulas, from the PCP theorem and parallel repetition. It builds chains of question tuples, replacing one question at a time. A vertex of the graph is a point, drawn by a finite random experiment, that assigns a phase on the circle to every possible answer of a tuple. Projections between answers link points at distance zero, and an edge joins x and y when x is within 1/8 of y shifted by half a turn. If the formula is satisfiable, evaluating every point at the satisfying answer gives each vertex one phase; adjacent vertices then get phases at least 3/8 apart, so three equal arcs of the circle color the graph properly. In our test of this rule on random phases, every edge got two different colors. For soundness, an independent set gives bounded functions on the phase spaces that flip sign under the half turn and obey a common Lipschitz bound. Austin’s junta theorem, a continuous counterpart of Friedgut’s, approximates such functions using a bounded number of answer coordinates, however many there are. The proof chooses such coordinate lists for whole stretches of a chain, with independent random rotations at every layer. If two separated stretches share an answer, that answer satisfies a label cover test in between, so low label cover value makes this rare. Two further steps turn this into a density bound. An alignment lemma, built from minimax, Ramsey-type homogenization and random arrays, lets each listed label, on most sampled sets of layers, be assigned one index shared by all its occurrences. Then coefficients are sampled at finitely many levels between zero and one, with geometrically decreasing probabilities, and a bound on influential coordinates gives a small mean square, hence a small independent set. Finally the random experiment is expanded exactly into unweighted vertices, so the reduction is deterministic.

5:18Where it stands

The preprint is not peer reviewed and has not been revised since its release. The Lean formalization covers the main theorem and proves its own inputs, with a Friedgut-type junta theorem in place of Austin’s; we have not run it. It is not among the papers OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. The gap between the algorithms’ growing number of colors and hardness for every fixed number remains wide. If it holds, then unless P equals NP, even knowing that a graph is three-colorable does not make it easy to find an independent set holding any fixed fraction of its vertices.
