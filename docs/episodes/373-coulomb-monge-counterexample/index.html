Electrons that must flip a coin?

An AI preprint claims the three-marginal Coulomb transport problem need not have an optimal map

The claim, in plain words

When electrons repel each other very strongly, chemistry asks a transport question: given the density of three electrons, how should their positions be correlated to make the average Coulomb repulsion as small as possible? Each electron must follow the same density, a third of the total, and the cost is one over distance, summed over pairs. The Monge ansatz assumes the best arrangement is deterministic: where one electron sits fixes where the other two sit. On a line our computer works it out for a uniform density: the electrons stay a third of the mass apart, and every such co-motion triple costs exactly 15/2.

In one dimension, maps were proved optimal in 2015. In higher dimensions, with three or more electrons, the question stayed open: a 2017 paper called it still an open question, and a January 2026 paper wrote that it is not known, except in one dimension. Some particular maps were shown to fail, and examples where no map is optimal were found for other costs, but not for the Coulomb cost itself.

Result 373 is one OpenAI paper, 14 pages, dated September 25 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims a smooth, compactly supported density in three dimensions for which the three-electron Coulomb problem has an optimal arrangement, but no deterministic one: every pair of measure-preserving maps costs strictly more. Yet maps can come arbitrarily close, so the two best values agree. The paper extends this to every inverse power of distance in dimension two and up.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: real atoms or ground states; this is one constructed density, and the strictly correlated energy is the same with or without maps. It is the first Coulomb example, as far as we found, where no map at all is optimal; the closest ones for other costs came in 2019.

How the proof goes

  1. Five blobs and two kinds of optimal triple. The density is five tiny blobs: a centre holding a third of the mass, and four blobs on two perpendicular axes holding a sixth each. A straight triple, centre plus two opposite blobs, costs one plus one plus a half, 5/2, with no net force on the middle electron; a bent triple costs about 2.71. Local calculus, after subtracting a large quadratic, gives two families of candidate triples, one per axis; our computer finds the quadratic must be strong enough: above about 2.65 is needed, and even ten fails. A five-part potential then forces every optimal arrangement onto those two families.
  2. Why a map cannot do it. A map would have to pick one family for each central point, sending a third of the mass into a target that holds only a sixth. Our computer tries several selection rules: each one overloads its target by exactly a factor of two. So the optimum must flip a coin between the two axes. Splitting the centre and the outer blobs into ever finer cells gives maps whose cost approaches the minimum; in our toy the gap falls from 6 × 10⁻⁵ to the level of our rounding error, but the limit is the coin flip, not a map.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: for a uniform density on a line, every co-motion triple of three electrons costs exactly 15/2.
  • Ours: a straight triple through the centre costs 5/2 with no net force on the middle electron; the bent one costs 2 + 1/√2 ≈ 2.71.
  • Ours: every deterministic selection rule we tried sends twice as much mass as its target can hold.
  • Lean: 73 files and 8,472 lines in the closure of the proof (97 files, 10,668 lines in the full module; counted by us; not run by us).
