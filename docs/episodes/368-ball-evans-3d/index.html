Smooth any 3-D deformation?

Two AI preprints claim the three-dimensional Ball–Evans approximation problem for Sobolev homeomorphisms

The claim, in plain words

In the mathematics of elastic solids, a deformation is a one-to-one map, and its energy depends on how much it stretches. Mathematicians measure that with Sobolev classes, W¹ᵖ: the p-th power of the stretching must have a finite total. Such a map can still be wild. Our computer checks a deformation of a ball that stretches infinitely at its centre, yet has finite energy for every p below six.

In 2001, John Ball asked, crediting Lawrence Evans: can every such deformation be approximated, in this energy, by one-to-one maps that are piecewise affine? Later work asked for smooth ones too. The obvious tools fail. Our computer interpolates a twist on a triangle mesh and finds ten triangles flipped inside out at every mesh size, and averaging two one-to-one maps, z and minus z, gives zero. In the plane the answer is yes, from Iwaniec, Kovalev and Onninen, and from Hencl and Pratelli. In four or more dimensions it fails for small p. A 2025 survey by Hencl calls any positive result in higher dimensions, especially three, the main open problem in the area.

Result 368 is two OpenAI papers, both dated September 24 and made public October 6, 2026: 62 pages for p from one to two, and 130 pages for p above two. Together they claim that for any bounded domains in three dimensions and any finite p from one up, every W¹ᵖ homeomorphism from one onto the other is a limit, in W¹ᵖ, of smooth one-to-one maps onto exactly the same target, with no assumptions on the boundary, the inverse or the Jacobian.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: convergence of the inverses or of elastic energies, matching boundary values, or anything in four dimensions and up. Ball’s original question asked for piecewise affine maps, and neither paper states that as a theorem. And smoothing piecewise affine one-to-one maps, in three and four dimensions, had already been done, by Campbell, D’Onofrio and Vítek in 2025.

How the proof goes

  1. Two stages, and the case p = 2. Both papers first build a locally bi-Lipschitz one-to-one map onto the target that is close in energy, then smooth it; a degree argument keeps each smooth map one-to-one and onto. For p up to two, the paper charges each crossing of test curves, and at p = 2 test surfaces, with a target mesh to a length or area budget, turns those budgets into an actual map, and rebuilds the derivative piece by piece. At p = 2, filling a face from its edges breaks down: our computer shows the cost growing without bound as the face’s core shrinks, so the paper needs a sharp disk estimate instead.
  2. Above p = 2: walls, guards and small spheres. For p above two, the paper builds an exact carrier map and a convex quantizer, then places walls pinned by guards. A misplaced wall costs something on many small spheres, and the sphere estimate works only when p is above two, where its factor shrinks with the sphere: at radius 1/1000, our computer finds one tenth at p = 3, while the same formula gives one at p = 2 and ten at p = 3/2. Finally, uniform convexity turns nearly sharp energy bounds into convergence of the derivatives.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: the map x|x|^(−1/2) stretches infinitely at the centre of a ball, yet has finite W¹ᵖ energy for every p below 6.
  • Ours: interpolating a twist with Jacobian exactly 1 on a triangle mesh flips 10 triangles at every mesh size.
  • Ours: filling a face from its edges at p = 2 costs ln(1/α) — 2.30, 4.61, 9.21 as the core shrinks 10, 100, 10⁴ times.
  • Ours: the factor R^(1−2/p) at R = 1/1000 is 10, 1, 0.25 and 0.1 for p = 3/2, 2, 5/2, 3; the papers' sphere estimate is stated for p above 2.
