Every element tame, yet infinite?

A preprint claims a division ring in which every element satisfies a polynomial over the center, yet two elements generate an infinite-dimensional ring, answering Kurosh’s 1941 question negatively

The claim, in plain words

The quaternions are a number system in which multiplication does not commute, yet every nonzero element can be divided by: a division ring. Take them with rational coefficients: then every quaternion satisfies a quadratic equation with rational coefficients; for example q = 1 + 2i + 3j + 4k satisfies q² − 2q + 30 = 0. In any division ring of finite dimension over its center, every element satisfies a polynomial over the center, with a ceiling on the degrees; and the words in two elements span a space that grows and then stops at the dimension.

Alexander Kurosh asked about the converse in 1941, in connection with Burnside’s problem on groups: if every element of a division ring satisfies some polynomial over the center, must finitely many elements always generate something finite-dimensional? For algebras that are not division rings the answer is no (Golod, 1964), but those finitely generated, infinite-dimensional examples rely on nilpotent elements, which a division ring cannot have. When the degrees are bounded, the answer is yes, by theorems of Jacobson and Kaplansky.

Result 201 is one OpenAI preprint, A Counterexample to Kurosh’s Division-Ring Problem (45 pages, dated September 23, 2026). Its Theorem 1.1: there is a countable division ring D of characteristic zero, with center F, and x, y in D such that D = F[x, y] = F(x, y), D is infinite-dimensional over F, and every element of D is algebraic over F. Every element has its own polynomial, but their degrees can have no ceiling.

What it doesn’t claim

Anything in positive characteristic. An explicit example: the ring is built with an ultrafilter and with choices at every stage, and no formula for x and y is given. A named center: “The construction does not prescribe the center” (p.2); by our reading it contains every algebraic number, and much more.

How the proof goes

  1. A schedule. Build an infinite sequence of finite-dimensional division algebras, each generated by a pair. List every noncommutative polynomial in x and y and, at each stage, force the next one on the list to satisfy a polynomial while keeping all the earlier ones.
  2. Glue the stages. The algebras need not sit inside one another, so the paper glues them with an ultraproduct, a kind of limit by vote in which whatever holds at almost every stage holds in the limit. In each stage the span of the words stops growing at that stage’s dimension, but the dimensions grow without bound, so in the limit the span never stops growing. A short descent argument moves this from a central subfield to the actual center. This takes about two and a half pages.
  3. The lifting theorem. About 36 pages. The free letters are replaced by series of matrices so that old identities survive; the paper studies how eigenvalues respond to small changes, shows the leading errors satisfy cyclic relations among rotations of words, and cancels them on a ring of eleven blocks, one letter on arrows that jump one or two places and the other on arrows that jump three or four. The data are realized in a division algebra of at least eleven times the degree, and a final shift fuses the eleven blocks into one, ready for the next step. By our reading nine or ten blocks would meet the conditions the proofs use too; eleven is the paper’s choice.

1:43The claim

Result 201 is one OpenAI preprint, 45 pages, dated September 23, 2026. It claims a countable division ring of characteristic zero in which every element satisfies a polynomial over the center, yet two elements, x and y, generate the whole ring over that center, and the ring is infinite-dimensional over it. So for division rings, the answer to Kurosh’s question is no. Every element has its own polynomial, but by the theorems just mentioned, their degrees can have no ceiling. Not claimed: anything in positive characteristic. Nor an explicit example: the ring is built with an ultrafilter and with choices at every stage, and no formula for x and y is given. Nor a named center: the paper does not prescribe it, and by our reading it contains every algebraic number, and much more.

2:40How the proof goes

The plan: build an infinite sequence of finite-dimensional division algebras, each generated by a pair. List every noncommutative polynomial in x and y, a list that never ends but reaches every item, and at each stage force the next one on the list to satisfy a polynomial, while keeping all the earlier ones. The algebras need not sit inside one another, so the paper glues them with an ultraproduct, a kind of limit by vote, in which whatever holds at almost every stage holds in the limit. In each stage, the span of the words in x and y stops growing at that stage’s dimension. But the dimensions grow without bound, so in the limit the span never stops growing. A short descent argument then moves this from a central subfield to the actual center. That part takes about 2½ pages. The real work is the lifting theorem, about 36 pages. In one step, the free letters are replaced by series of matrices, so that old identities survive. The paper studies how the eigenvalues of the chosen expression respond to small changes, shows that the leading errors satisfy cyclic relations among rotations of words, and cancels them on a ring of eleven blocks, with one letter on arrows that jump one or two places, and the other on arrows that jump three or four. The matrix data are then realized inside a division algebra of at least eleven times the degree, and a final shift fuses the eleven blocks into one, restoring the conditions for the next step. Our checks on the finite pieces agree: the cycle test, the eleven-block count, and the rank count of the last step. By our reading, nine or ten blocks would meet the stated conditions too; eleven is the paper’s choice.
