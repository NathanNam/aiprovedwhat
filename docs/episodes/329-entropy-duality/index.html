Covering is not self-dual?

An AI preprint claims counterexamples to Pietsch’s duality conjecture for metric entropy

Covers result 329 in OpenAI’s catalog: its one paper.

The claim, in plain words

How many translates of a body L does it take to cover a body K? That count, on a logarithmic scale, is the metric entropy of K relative to L. Every symmetric convex body has a polar, its dual shape: the cube’s polar is the diamond, and the round ball is its own. Duality asks whether covering K by L costs about as much as covering the polar of L by the polar of K, with the roles swapped. In the plane, our computer finds that the unit square scaled up 2.5 times needs 9 copies of the unit square, and the dual covering needs 9 too.

Pietsch conjectured in the early 1970s, in the language of operators, that the two entropies agree up to universal constants, in every dimension. Volume arguments show the covering numbers agree to within a factor exponential in the dimension, so the hard case is small entropy in high dimension. A 2004 paper stated the geometric form as a conjecture and proved it when one body is a Euclidean ball; partial results followed, but the general question stayed open, and two experts voiced cautious doubts.

Result 329 is one OpenAI paper, 15 pages, dated September 24 and made public October 6, 2026. It claims the conjecture is false: for any proposed constants a and b, there is a dimension and a symmetric convex body K such that covering K by the unit cube costs more than b times the entropy on the dual side, even when the dual covering body is shrunk by the factor a. The covering body can be the cube itself.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: a counterexample in any fixed dimension, since the dimension must grow with the constants; any explicit body, since one step is random; or anything against weaker versions with logarithmic losses. The ellipsoid case stays proved.

How the proof goes

  1. A matrix: rows apart, columns compressible. The body is built from a matrix. Its rows, pairwise separated, force many cubes on the primal side. Its columns are profiles of graph distance that decay logarithmically, and a compression argument shows every signed combination of columns can be approximated from a list far shorter than the number of rows, which makes the dual side cheap. Our computer checks the logarithmic profile: at cutoff 8 it falls from 1 through 1/2 at distance 2 to 0.
  2. Rows from finite fields. The rows come from finite fields: symmetric forms over a huge prime field, with random directions defining the partitions. A random choice works: for each pair of rows, deleting a small fraction of directions keeps the two far apart. In a toy over the field of 5 elements, our computer finds 60 of 124 row differences unseparated before deletion and none after, though at this tiny size the deletion is far from small. The sizes are astronomical: with both constants equal to 1, the paper’s parameters need a dimension of about 10^10^10^10^480.
