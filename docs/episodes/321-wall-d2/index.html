Looks 2D, isn’t?

An AI preprint claims a counterexample to Wall’s D(2) problem

The claim, in plain words

A finite cell complex of dimension 3 can sometimes be squashed, up to homotopy, to dimension 2. In 1965 C. T. C. Wall asked when. If the complex already looks 2-dimensional to homology, with nothing above degree 2 in its universal cover and no third cohomology for any twisted coefficients, must it be homotopy equivalent to a finite 2-complex? That is the D(2) problem.

It sits next to the Eilenberg–Ganea and Whitehead conjectures, and most work on it has been for finite fundamental groups, where it ties to deep questions about group presentations. In 2021 Mannan and Popiel wrote that confirming one main kind of counterexample, an Euler characteristic gap, would require a fundamentally novel obstruction. Before October 6, recent papers still listed the problem as open.

Result 321 is one OpenAI paper, 10 pages, dated and made public October 6, 2026. It claims a finite connected 3-dimensional complex that passes Wall’s homological tests, but has no finite 2-dimensional model. Its fundamental group is infinite: by our reading, two finite groups of orders 30 and 78 glued together, with an extra loop.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: anything for finite fundamental groups, where the problem stays open, or anything about the Eilenberg–Ganea or Whitehead conjectures. The stabilized version, adding enough spheres, was already known to work.

How the proof goes

  1. A twisted coefficient system. The complex is built by a finite plus construction, which kills a perfect subgroup; perfection comes from the coprime numbers 15 and 26. Then the paper chooses a 1-dimensional twisted coefficient system, sending one element to −1 and another to a cube root of unity. Our computer recomputes the paper’s 4 × 5 Fox matrix entry by entry, confirms its key minor, −4(1 − ζ²), and finds that the obstruction needs both the sign and the cube root: either alone does nothing.
  2. A tower that cannot stop. The new ingredient is an obstruction, proved by a tower argument in the style of Howie. Any finite 2-complex with this fundamental group would have twisted second homology that cannot vanish, because some element of finite order sees the coefficient twist. But a 2-dimensional model would inherit the complex’s own vanishing twisted homology. Our computer checks this pattern on small complexes, from the projective plane up to presentations of the two finite groups: whenever the twist sees torsion, the twisted second homology is at least 1.
