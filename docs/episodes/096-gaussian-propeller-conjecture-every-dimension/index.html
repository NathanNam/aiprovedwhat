The claim, in plain words

Scatter points by the standard Gaussian distribution and cut space into a few pieces. For each piece, average the position vectors of all the points but count only those inside the piece, without dividing by the piece’s probability: this is the piece’s Gaussian first moment, z(A) = ∫A x dγ(x), an arrow toward where its mass sits. Add up the squared lengths of the arrows. Two half-planes give 1/π, about 0.318; three 120° sectors of the plane, the propeller, give 9/(8π), about 0.358; four equal quarters fall back to 1/π, and more equal sectors do worse.

The propeller conjecture says that no partition does better, with any number of pieces in any dimension. It arose in Khot and Naor’s 2009 study of kernel clustering, where this best total governs approximation and Unique Games hardness; they showed that a best partition can be taken to be made of cones and found the best value for three cells. Heilman, Jagannath and Naor proved the conjecture in three dimensions in 2013 with a computer-assisted argument, and Heilman later gave a conditional route for three or four cells in any dimension.

Result 096 is one OpenAI preprint dated September 24, 2026 (26 pages). It claims that for every dimension and every finite measurable partition, the squared Gaussian first moments add up to at most 9/(8π), with equality for the propeller, crossed with the remaining directions, in every dimension from 2 up. A corollary bounds the expected maximum of Gaussian scores: E max⟨vi, g⟩ ≤ 3/(2√(2π)) · (Σ‖vi − v̄‖²)1/2. The paper also derives a kernel-clustering consequence: with Khot and Naor’s analysis and a separate OpenAI preprint that claims to prove the Unique Games Conjecture (result 102), for each fixed k ≥ 3 no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm can beat Khot and Naor’s loss factor (8π/9)(1 − 1/k) unless P = NP.

What it doesn’t claim

The theorem gives the best value; it does not claim the propeller is the only partition attaining it. The upper bound uses two outside theorems, the three-dimensional result of Heilman, Jagannath and Naor and Ehrhard’s Gaussian inequality, and the clustering consequence depends on the separate Unique Games preprint.

How the proof goes

  1. Scores instead of shapes. By Khot and Naor’s duality, the square root of the best total equals the largest expected maximum of Gaussian scores ⟨vi, g⟩ over vectors whose squared lengths add up to one. Suppose some partition beats 9/(8π) and take a best one with the fewest cells. Merging two cells changes the total by twice the inner product of their moments, so every pair of moments is obtuse; each cell is the cone where its own score is largest, and the m moments span m − 1 dimensions. With four or fewer cells that is at most three dimensions, ruled out by the 2013 theorem, so at least five cells remain.
  2. Probability against length. Rescale so that the squared moment lengths ri² add up to one, like the cell probabilities Pi. Each Pi is at most 1/2, since the scores add up to zero. Comparing a cell with the half-space of the same probability, and for five cells using spherical rearrangement, gives lower bounds such as Pi ≥ 0.415 ri + 0.15 ri². Deleting one score must lower the expected maximum by a definite amount, while Ehrhard’s inequality, applied to translates of the deleted cell’s cone, limits how far it can drop.
  3. Two competitors. Take the cell with the shortest moment and project the other moments away from it. Over that cell each residual score integrates to zero; bounding the joint density of two of them gives √det Cov(Yj, Yk) ≤ (3/π) t (1 + aj)(1 + ak), with t the shortest normalized length.
  4. The contradiction. Because the probabilities add up to one, the third-longest moment is forced to be too long compared with the shortest, against the determinant bound; the final margin is 1.5(2.6 − 0.12)² − 10(3/π)² ≈ 0.1067. An appendix checks every numerical comparison with exact rational enclosures.

The numbers in the video

  • Equal planar sectors give k sin²(π/k)/(2π): 0.318, 0.358, 0.318, 0.275, 0.239 for k = 2 to 6; in three dimensions octants give 0.239, the six cones of a cube 0.293 and the four cones of a regular tetrahedron 0.353.
  • A search with five score vectors in four dimensions from 60 random starts (each point to its largest score, each vector reset to its cell’s moment): 15 runs end with three cells in a plane within 0.00001 of 9/(8π); 44 stall with four cells, 43 of them at the tetrahedron’s value 0.3532; one ends with five cells at 0.338. Recomputed by quadrature over directions, none exceeds 9/(8π).
  • The Gaussian-maxima corollary holds on 300 random lists of vectors (largest ratio 0.9994 by Monte Carlo).

3:41How the proof goes

The proof first trades shapes for vectors. By duality, the square root of the best total equals the largest expected maximum of Gaussian scores, the inner products of a Gaussian point with vectors whose squared lengths add up to 1. Suppose some partition beats 9/(8π), and take a best one with the fewest cells. Merging two cells changes the total by twice the inner product of their moments, so every pair of moments makes an obtuse angle. Each cell is the cone where its own score is largest, and the m moments span exactly m − 1 dimensions. With 4 or fewer cells that is at most 3 dimensions, which the 2013 theorem rules out. So at least 5 cells remain. Rescale so that the squared moment lengths add up to 1, like the cell probabilities. Each probability is at most 1/2, since the scores add up to zero. And each cell needs enough probability for its moment length. Comparing a cell with the half-space of the same probability gives one lower bound, and spherical rearrangement sharpens it for 5 cells. A second bound comes from deleting one score. The expected maximum must drop by a definite amount, while Ehrhard’s inequality, applied to translates of the deleted cell’s cone, limits how far it can drop. Next, take the cell with the shortest moment, and project the other moments away from it. Over that cell, each residual score integrates to zero; bounding the joint density of two of them gives an inequality between their covariance determinant and the shortest length. Finally, the probabilities must add up to 1. That forces the third-longest moment to be too long compared with the shortest one, against the determinant bound, with a final margin of about 0.107. 5:35Where it stands

The preprint is not peer reviewed and has not been revised. The Lean formalization covers the full bound and the propeller’s attainment; we have not run it. The hardness consequence rests on the separate Unique Games preprint and is not formalized. It is not among the papers OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If it holds, no partition of Gaussian space, in any dimension, does better than the propeller. And if the Unique Games preprint also holds, then for any fixed k of 3 or more, no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm can beat Khot and Naor’s factor for kernel clustering, (8π/9)(1 − 1/k), unless P equals NP.
