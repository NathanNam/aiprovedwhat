The claims, in plain words

A contingency table is a grid of nonnegative whole numbers with prescribed row sums and column sums. Statisticians use such tables to test whether two classifications are independent, which raises two basic questions: how many tables share the same margins, and how can one be drawn uniformly at random? The 2-by-2 tables with every margin 2 are already instructive: there are exactly 3, the integer points of a segment, and only the two ends are vertices. With margins written in binary the count can be enormous: the 3-by-3 tables with every margin 1,000,000 number about 1.25 × 1023.

Exact counting is #P-complete even with two rows (Dyer, Kannan and Mount). Approximation had partial answers: Dyer and Greenhill handled two rows, Cryan and Dyer any fixed number of rows, and geometric methods of Dyer, Kannan and Mount, and later Morris, handled large margins. For sparse margins, Arman, Gao and Wormald gave exact uniform generation, and in 2021 they reported that no polynomial-time approximately uniform sampler for arbitrary margins was known. A cap on every cell connects the problem to perfect matchings and network flows; Cryan, Dyer and Randall identified the unrestricted capped case as an open problem in 2010.

Result 115 is two OpenAI preprints dated September 24, 2026. The first, 31 pages, claims an exactly uniform sampler for tables with any margins, with no positivity, balance, sparsity or fixed-dimension assumption: it stops with probability one, and its expected number of bit operations is polynomial in both dimensions and the number of bits in the margins. A second algorithm has a polynomial time bound on every execution and outputs a table within 2−k of uniform in total variation, for any requested k. The second, 47 pages, claims a fully polynomial randomized approximation scheme (FPRAS) for tables with an individual upper bound on every cell, where a zero bound forbids the cell: on every execution the bit operations are polynomial in the input length, 1/ε and log(1/δ), and an empty set of tables gives zero. It also yields approximate counting and sampling of bounded integer flows in networks.

What it doesn’t claim

The exact sampler is polynomial in expectation, not on every run: a rare correction branch does exhaustive work. Its guarantees are for tables without cell bounds; with cell bounds, the claim is approximate counting (and, through it, approximate sampling). The authors note that their exponents are deliberately large: these are complexity guarantees, not practical running times.

How the proofs go

  1. Small cells and a padded rectangle. Cells in a row or column with a small margin become explicit coordinates; the remaining cells form a complete rectangle, padded so that filling it in is always a dense problem. Each small cell carries a row value xs and a column value qs; states where they agree are transversals, and the chain also allows a surplus of one at one cell and a deficit of one at another. Each state is weighted by its number of padded completions. Sampling a state and then a completion, and accepting only a transversal whose large entries are all at least the padding, gives a uniform table once the padding is subtracted.
  2. Why the chain mixes. The proof reveals the small-cell values one at a time and compares neighboring fibers. Quadratic forms indexed by pairs of unit removals have at most one positive eigenvalue, a property central to the theory of Lorentzian polynomials, and the property survives summing out a balanced pair. A weighted transport between neighboring fibers gives a Poincaré inequality, which bounds the mixing time.
  3. From approximate to exact. Completions of the dense rectangle are drawn approximately on a finite scaled grid; a log-concave density appears only in the analysis, and in an ordinary run large ranges are never enumerated. For exactness, the residual-mixture idea of Göbel, Liu, Manurangsi and Pappik: with probability δ the algorithm enters a correction branch, lists all tables, computes the bounded sampler’s exact output law pk, and draws from r(z) = (1/M − (1 − δ)pk(z))/δ, so that (1 − δ)pk + δr is exactly uniform. The branch is exponentially expensive but exponentially rare.
  4. Counting with caps. Each cell is tightened to the range it can actually take, and cells are split at a threshold. Large cells are handled through a maximum-capacity spanning forest and soft penalties, hardened along a schedule while ratios are estimated by sampling; the final softened count is within a factor 1 + ε/32 of the true count. Each small cell with bound b becomes b labelled pairs, giving transversals and defect states as in the common-bases result (episode 114), with learned multipliers as in the permanent algorithm.

The numbers in the video

  • The 3-by-3 tables with every margin 3 number 55; MacMahon’s formula, checked by direct counting up to margin 12, gives 125,000,750,001,875,002,250,001 when every margin is 1,000,000.
  • Padding every entry of a small example (rows 3, 2; columns 2, 2, 1; 5 tables) by 2 gave 33 tables, exactly 5 of which had every entry at least 2, matching the 5 originals.
  • A slightly biased law on the 55 tables, mixed with its residual at probability 2−12, was exactly uniform in exact arithmetic.
  • Seven labelled pairs gave six transversals; weighting each by 1/∏C(bs, xs) gave a total of 2, exactly the number of tables. Separately, with 0/1 bounds and unit margins, direct counts equalled the permanents.

The sampler separates small numbers from large ones. Cells in a row or column with a small margin become explicit coordinates; the remaining cells form a complete rectangle, which is padded so that filling it in is always a dense problem. Each small cell carries two bounded values, one for its row and one for its column. Completions of the dense rectangle are drawn approximately on a finite scaled grid; a log-concave density appears only in the analysis, and in an ordinary run large ranges are never enumerated. With tiny probability it enters a correction branch, enumerates all tables, computes the bounded sampler’s exact output law, and draws from the leftover mass. The branch is exponentially expensive but exponentially rare, so the expected cost stays polynomial. 6:01Where it stands

The preprints are not peer reviewed and have not been revised. OpenAI’s Lean formalization covers the bounded sampler, the exact sampler and the counting scheme, as statements about a fixed randomized machine, but not the network-flow consequences; we have not run it. Neither paper is among those OSVerify reviewed, and we have seen no expert review. If they hold, tables with any margins can be sampled exactly in polynomial expected time, and tables with caps on individual cells can be counted approximately in polynomial time, without the fixed number of rows, large margins or sparse margins that earlier methods needed.
