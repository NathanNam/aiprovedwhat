A condensate, proved at last?

Five AI preprints claim Bose–Einstein condensation for the interacting hard-sphere gas, and the quantum depletion law

The claim, in plain words

Cool a gas of bosons enough and a macroscopic fraction of the particles piles into a single quantum state. Bose–Einstein condensation was predicted in 1925 and seen in rubidium in 1995. For an ideal gas with no interactions, Einstein computed it exactly: below a critical temperature, the condensed fraction is 1 − (T/Tc)3/2. Condensation means long-range order: the one-particle density matrix does not decay to zero at large distances.

For real, interacting particles in continuous space at fixed density, this had never been proved. The only interacting case known in the thermodynamic limit was hard-core bosons on a lattice at half filling. A 2025 survey by Solovej called ground-state condensation one of the major open problems, and a 2005 book by Lieb, Seiringer, Solovej and Yngvason wrote that the problem remained open after more than 75 years.

Result 267 is five OpenAI preprints, 279 pages, dated September 24 to October 5, 2026. The headline paper claims condensation for the exact canonical Gibbs state of the three-dimensional hard-sphere gas. For each sphere size and small enough density there is a fixed positive temperature, independent of the volume, at which a positive fraction of particles occupies the lowest state in the thermodynamic limit. Companions claim condensation for every ground state of dilute hard spheres, and for bounded repulsive potentials at small temperatures. Two more claim the quantum depletion law at zero temperature: to leading order, the fraction of particles kicked out of the condensate by interactions is 8/(3√π) times √(ρa³), taking the infinite volume first.

What it doesn’t claim

The temperature is tiny. Its formula is explicit up to constants the paper never computes; at ρa³ = 10⁻⁶, if that is dilute enough, it is below 10⁻⁷ of the ideal critical temperature (our arithmetic). Not claimed: condensation all the way up to the transition, the shift of the critical temperature, two dimensions, attractive or helium-like interactions, or depletion at positive temperature.

How the proofs go

  1. Cells and random routes. The proofs divide space into cells holding many particles. They show that a particle here and a particle far away see the same surroundings, by shifting a chain of particles one cell each along random lattice routes that rarely meet. This forces occupation of the constant orbital.
  2. Hard core and depletion. The hard core is handled by local energy bounds and careful particle insertions. The depletion law comes from a Bogoliubov analysis inside large boxes, with the path comparison controlling differences between boxes.
