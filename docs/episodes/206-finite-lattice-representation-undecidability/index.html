The claim, in plain words

An algebra, in the most general sense, is a set with some operations on it, any number, of any finite arity. A congruence is a way of gluing elements together that respects all the operations, and the congruences of an algebra, ordered by inclusion, always form a lattice. Three points with no operations at all have five congruences, arranged in a diamond. In 1963 Grätzer and Schmidt showed that every algebraic lattice arises this way, but the algebra may have to be infinite.

The finite version stayed open: is every finite lattice the congruence lattice of some finite algebra? Pálfy and Pudlák showed in 1980 that this is the same as asking whether every finite lattice appears as an interval of subgroups, between a subgroup H and a finite group G. (Our computer checks one half of this link on 10 small examples: the congruences of G acting on the cosets of H match the interval exactly.) By 2012, every lattice with at most seven elements was known to work, except possibly one.

Result 206 bundles two OpenAI preprints, both dated September 24, 2026, that answer no. A negative solution to the finite lattice representation problem (87 pages) proves that some finite lattice is not an interval of subgroups in any finite group, and gets the answer through Pálfy and Pudlák. Finite congruence lattices: characterization and undecidability (180 pages) builds its own family of counterexamples and claims more: no algorithm can decide, from a lattice’s table, whether it is the congruence lattice of a finite algebra, or a full interval of subgroups. It also settles that last seven-element case: the lattice is the interval between a group of order 6 and a group of 262,080 elements, and our computer rebuilt that interval and found exactly the seven subgroups the paper names.

What it doesn’t claim

A counterexample of known size. Both papers prove one exists; the smallest has more than seven elements, and the longer paper pins down its exact size only through an uncomputed Busy Beaver number. The shorter paper’s lattice is shown not to be a subgroup interval; the congruence-lattice answer follows only through the global Pálfy–Pudlák equivalence.

How the proof goes

  1. Into finite groups. Both counterexample proofs translate lattices into finite groups and use the classification of finite simple groups.
  2. The shorter paper. Lattices with long chains of small test pieces force, in any group realizing them, the same simple group at both ends of a long enough chain (Theorem 3.2). A four-dimensional cube of subgroups and a detector then force a gluing of homomorphisms that cannot exist (Theorem 10.10).
  3. The longer paper: yes is checkable. A colored graph whose existence is equivalent to a representation (Theorem 2.2) is a finite certificate for yes; searching graph sizes one by one finds every yes, but may run forever on a no (Corollary 2.3).
  4. No is not. Integer equations are encoded in lattices, with numbers stored as sizes of permutation blocks and products checked with a squaring trick (Lemma 15.2). A decider would bound the smallest solution of every solvable equation, which would solve Hilbert’s tenth problem, known to be unsolvable (Theorem 15.3).
