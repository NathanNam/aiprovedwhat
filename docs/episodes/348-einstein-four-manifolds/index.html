Only three shapes?

Three AI preprints claim the nonnegative-curvature Einstein classification in dimension four

The claim, in plain words

An Einstein manifold is one whose Ricci curvature, the total bending of the planes through each direction, is the same everywhere and in every direction. In four dimensions, with positive curvature of this kind, three classic examples are the round four-sphere, the complex projective plane with its Fubini–Study metric, and a product of two equal round two-spheres. Normalize the Ricci curvature to three, and our computer checks their sectional curvatures, the bending of each two-dimensional plane: exactly one on the sphere, between one half and two on the projective plane, and between zero and three on the product, where mixed planes are flat.

A well-known conjecture, recorded by DaGang Yang in 2000 and since called folklore, says that if every plane bends positively, only the sphere and the projective plane occur, up to quotients; with nonnegative bending, the product joins them. Partial results needed extra hypotheses: pinched curvature, starting with Berger; a positive intersection form, by Gursky and LeBrun; or extra symmetry. In September 2026, days before this release, Liang Cheng settled the positive case when the Euler characteristic is at most three, and Gursky and Malchiodi under another topological condition, with a key step found with help from ChatGPT. Neither condition covers the topology of the product of two spheres.

Result 348 is three OpenAI papers, made public October 6, 2026. The first, 59 pages, dated September 23 but citing those September papers, claims the positive case: a closed Einstein four-manifold with positive sectional curvature is, after scaling, the round sphere, the projective plane, or real projective four-space. The second, 48 pages, claims that if the curvature is only nonnegative and some plane is flat, the universal cover is the product of two equal spheres; so with nonnegative curvature, the universal cover is one of the three models. The third, 13 pages, assumes the second and claims a gap: a closed, simply connected four-manifold with nonnegative curvature that is close enough to Einstein, in an integral sense, is diffeomorphic to one of the three.

What it doesn’t claim

Not claimed: Einstein metrics whose curvature changes sign, which exist on many four-manifolds; isometry, or an explicit threshold, in the gap theorem; or the Hopf conjecture about all metrics on the product of two spheres.

The positive-curvature theorem only (docs)

Last checked October 19, 2026. All three papers are unrefereed and unchanged since release. The positive-curvature theorem is formalized in Lean, 402 files and about 208,000 lines, not run by us, one outside project re-ran it successfully and another lists it as certified. The nonnegative classification and the gap theorem, the catalog’s headline, have no Lean, and the gap theorem depends on the zero-plane paper. An AI audit found no gap in the three. We found no specialist comment and no reported error. OSVerify has not reviewed it.

How the proof goes

  1. Two Weyl blocks. The proof splits the curvature into two Weyl blocks. Our computer checks that the smallest sectional curvature is one minus half the sum of how far below zero each block reaches, so positive curvature means that sum stays below two. Curvature integrals give the Euler characteristic and the signature. If both blocks were nonzero, a volume comparison along geodesics, with integrality, would leave only one case with nonzero signature, Euler characteristic five and signature one, which a separate lemma excludes. Our own search finds the same lone survivor.
  2. Forcing the model. Weighted inequalities, certified by exact polynomial computations, then force one Weyl block to vanish, so the manifold is half conformally flat, and a volume bound helps pin down the model. In the zero-plane paper the same argument runs at the boundary, where both blocks become parallel and the manifold splits as a product. The gap theorem runs Ricci flow for a short time and takes limits. For a product of two spheres with radii in ratio ρ, our computer finds the gap theorem’s integral is 16π²(ρ − 1/ρ)².

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: at Ricci curvature 3, sectional curvature is exactly 1 on the round 4-sphere, between 1/2 and 2 on the complex projective plane, and between 0 and 3 on the product of two equal 2-spheres.
  • Ours: Euler characteristic and signature are (2, 0), (3, 1) and (4, 0) for the three models; the product is outside both September 2026 conditions.
  • Ours: the smallest sectional curvature equals 1 − (x + y)/2 in terms of the two Weyl blocks (sampled on 60 random cases).
  • Ours: for S²(r₁) × S²(r₂), the trace-free Ricci integral is 16π²(ρ − 1/ρ)² with ρ = r₂/r₁.
  • Lean: 402 files and 207,815 lines in the import closure of the positive-curvature comparator (counted by us; not run by us).
