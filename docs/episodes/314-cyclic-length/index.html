Heights lost?

An AI preprint claims the chromatic fixed-point loss of a finite p-group equals its cyclic length

The claim, in plain words

Smith theory, from the 1940s, says that when a finite p-group acts on a finite complex that looks like a point in mod p homology, the fixed points look like a point too. Chromatic homotopy theory replaces mod p homology by a ladder of Morava K-theories, K(0), K(1), K(2) and so on, each one a finer lens, called a height. The chromatic version of Smith’s question: if the fixed points of a subgroup H are invisible at one height, at which height must the fixed points of the whole group G be invisible? The number of heights lost is the loss from H to G.

In 2015 Balmer and Sanders showed that these losses describe the topology of a space of primes, and guessed that the loss is the number of prime-order steps from H to G. In 2017, Barthel, Hausmann, Naumann, Nikolaus, Noel and Stapleton showed that for abelian groups it is the number of generators of the quotient, so a cyclic group of order p² loses one height, not two. For nonabelian groups, Kuhn and Lloyd found in 2020 that a reflection subgroup of the dihedral group of order 8 loses two heights, although the easy lower bound gives one, and expressed cautious hope that in general the loss is the length of the shortest chain from H to G, each step normal in the next with a cyclic quotient: the cyclic length. In 2023 Balderrama and Kuhn asked whether the loss for a reflection in the dihedral group of order 16 is two or three.

Result 314 is one OpenAI paper, 30 pages, dated September 24, 2026, and made public October 6. It claims that for every prime, every finite p-group G, every subgroup H and every height, the loss from H to G is exactly the cyclic length. So the loss does not depend on the height, and for the reflection in the dihedral group of order 16, the formula gives three. Combined with Balmer and Sanders’ earlier reduction, it gives the finite-height inclusion test for the space of primes of every finite group.

What is new, and what isn’t claimed

Only the lower bound is new, beyond abelian groups and Kuhn and Lloyd’s family; the upper bound, one height per cyclic step, was already known. The spectra that show the bound is sharp are proved to exist by contradiction, not built. Not claimed: compact Lie groups, or the parts of the space of primes that Balmer and Sanders had already settled.

How the proof goes

  1. A profinite group from the chromatic data. For the lower bound, the paper builds a profinite group out of which finite groups the height-local sphere can see, and integrates over finite groupoids to get two arithmetic facts about it. One says that the number of homomorphisms into any finite group, divided by that group’s order, has no p in its denominator.
  2. Group theory, then every height. Pure group theory then shows that such a group is built from at most as many copies of the p-adic integers as the height, so its finite quotients have short cyclic chains. Nilpotence and ambidexterity carry the bound from rational information to every height, and a Balmer–Sanders-style argument finishes: a prime breaking the bound would split an irreducible closed set in two.
