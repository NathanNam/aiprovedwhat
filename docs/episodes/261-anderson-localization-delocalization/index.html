Trapped, or free?

Two AI preprints claim localization in two dimensions and free states in three and up for the Anderson model

The claim, in plain words

An electron moving through a perfect crystal travels freely. In 1958 Philip Anderson argued that random impurities can trap it: its waves stop spreading and stay localized. The model is a lattice, a hopping term between neighbours, and an independent random energy at every site, uniform between −h and h. In one dimension any disorder traps everything. Physicists predicted in 1979 that in two dimensions every amount of disorder eventually traps, while in three dimensions weak disorder leaves some states free.

In spectral terms, trapping is usually read as pure-point spectrum, a complete set of eigenfunctions, and freedom as absolutely continuous spectrum. Localization at strong disorder and near band edges was proved in the 1980s and 1990s. But Barry Simon’s 2000 list of problems called proving purely absolutely continuous spectrum on some energy range in three dimensions “the big kahuna”, and two-dimensional localization at weak disorder was also open. Two papers from September 2026 still called these problems open.

Result 261 is two OpenAI preprints, both dated September 23, 2026. The first, 78 pages, claims that in two dimensions, for every positive disorder strength, the spectrum is almost surely pure point, with eigenvalues dense in the whole spectrum. The second, 93 pages, claims that in every dimension 3 or more, for small enough disorder, the spectrum is almost surely purely absolutely continuous on a fixed interval, with nonzero weight there. At the very bottom of the spectrum, below the band, the same disorder gives pure point, so both kinds coexist.

What it doesn’t claim

The two-dimensional paper does not claim dynamical localization or exponential decay of eigenfunctions. The higher-dimensional paper’s interval is a thin sliver just above the bottom of the band, a few hundredths of a percent of it or less. Its disorder threshold is at most about 0.002 from four dimensions up, and no value is given in three. It claims nothing about the mobility edge, diffusion, or the band centre.

How the proofs go

  1. Two dimensions. The proof plants rare resonant cells inside the true randomness and controls them through a hierarchy of comparisons on finite tori. The planar step is a capacity argument: in the plane, a logarithmic profile costs almost nothing, which forces the transport energy to zero. It ends with a fractional-moment bound and a rank-one perturbation argument of Simon and Wolff.
  2. Three dimensions and up. The proof writes each uniform potential as infinitely many coin flips and reveals them one binary digit at a time. It tracks three controls on the resolvent and sets aside failed local trials as pending sites to be cleared later. It ends with a finite-volume certificate that becomes absolutely continuous spectrum; its threshold uses boxes at least 10⁴⁸¹ sites on a side.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: in three dimensions, a particle released at one site keeps spreading at disorder h = 2 and freezes at h = 12.
  • Ours: our computer cannot see the two-dimensional result. At disorder 1, eigenvectors spread over more sites as the box grows, from about 80 to over 2,300, and the level statistics look like free states. The predicted trapping happens only on scales far beyond any box we can diagonalize.
