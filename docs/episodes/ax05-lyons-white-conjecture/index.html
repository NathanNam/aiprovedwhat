Faster random clocks, farther away? The Lyons–White conjecture

Colin Defant and Ken Ono, of Axiom Math, with AxiomProver: on dihedral groups, even powers never get worse; every other power above 1 can.

The claim, in plain words

This is AX05. Take a regular pentagon with labelled corners. Its rotations and reflections form the dihedral group D_5, so the pentagon can sit in ten states. Give each move a random clock, running at the same rate as its inverse’s; when a clock rings, the machine makes that move. Over time, every state becomes equally likely. To measure how far the walk is from that balance, take each state’s gap from 1/10, raise it to a power p, add them up, and take the p-th root. Now speed up some clocks. Can the walk end up farther from balance?

The paper is Proof of the Lyons–White Conjecture, by Colin Defant and Ken Ono, both of Axiom Math: thirteen pages on the arXiv, version one of August 27, 2026. A group and a power p are called rate-monotonic if raising symmetric clock rates never increases the distance, at any fixed time. Theorem A: D_n with p = 2m is rate-monotonic, for all positive integers m and n. Theorem B extends this to inversion extensions of finite abelian groups, including dicyclic and generalized quaternion groups. Theorem C: for every real p greater than one that is not an even integer, some dihedral group is not rate-monotonic. The appendix says the proofs were generated through human-AI collaboration, with AxiomProver.

What it doesn’t claim

What is not claimed. Not that every walk misbehaves at 4.001: only some dihedral group, some rates and some time. Not p = 1 in Theorem C; for that case the paper cites Lyons and White’s own example on D_5. Not every finite group: Lyons and White found examples at exactly four on the symmetric group S_5. And not the paper’s broader conjecture, for groups with an abelian subgroup of index two, which is left open. The word conjecture is the paper’s; Lyons and White reported what their searches found.

How the proof goes

  1. History. Russell Lyons and Graham White studied this in a paper first posted in 2021. They proved that faster clocks never hurt when p is two or infinity, on every finite group. On dihedral groups, their computer searches found walks where faster is farther, for p from one to just below two, between two and four, and from 4.001 to 5.995; but none at exactly four, in groups up to D_131, and none at six. They wrote that they had no explanation for four or six. Our own example, from a computer search: on the pentagon, raise one reflection’s clock from two to twelve. With p = 1, the faster walk is up to about seven percent farther from balance, around time one; with p = 4, it is nearer at every time we computed.
  2. Even powers. How the proof goes, in the authors’ account. For even powers, the rotations supply frequencies, and Fourier analysis reduces the key calculations to two by two matrices, whose positivity bounds one set of Fourier coefficients by another. Then the even power does the work: a sum of 2m-th powers is a sum of squares of m-th powers, so Parseval’s identity applies, and the products can be bounded term by term. For a reflection’s clock, differentiating the distance with Duhamel’s formula shows it never grows; rotations are handled more directly. The paper points to a close analogue in the Hardy–Littlewood majorant problem.
  3. Other powers. For a p that is not even, the paper builds the bad walk from a negative Fourier coefficient. With p between 2m and 2m + 2, and K equal to 2m + 3, a cosine integral obeys a recurrence with factors (p − 2l)/(p + 2l), and only the last factor is negative. At 4.001, the factors are positive, tiny, then negative; at exactly four, in our arithmetic, one factor is zero. A small two-frequency perturbation, adjusted and scaled into a genuine walk, then drifts farther when a reflection’s clock speeds up.

The numbers in the video

  • Ours: on the pentagon (D_5, 10 states), slower rates 2, 2, 1 on the reflections r³b, r²b, rb and 1.4 on the rotations r², r³; the faster walk raises r³b to 12. With p = 1 the faster walk is up to 6.9% farther from uniform (near t = 1; farther for t from 0.83 to 2.2); with p = 2 and p = 4 it is nearer at every time we computed (t from 0.05 to 3). Found by a computer search and rounded.
  • Ours (a sample, not a proof): 238 random walks on D_3 to D_9 with one inverse pair sped up; for p = 2, 4, 6 the largest relative increase was at rounding level (below 10⁻¹⁰).
  • Ours: the Lemma 3.1 cosine sum for p = 3 (K = 5) at n = 11 is -0.135; the recurrence factors are 1/5 and −1/7. At p = 4.001 (K = 7) they are 0.333, 0.000125 and -0.200; at p = 4 the same three factors are 1/3, 0 and −1/5.
  • Ours (keyword scan, not an audit): Axiom’s repository has 81 Lean files (16,030 lines); sorry appears only in the 3 challenge statements.
