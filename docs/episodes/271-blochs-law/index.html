A law for magnets, proved at last?

The claim, in plain words

The simplest quantum model of a magnet is Heisenberg’s ferromagnet: spins on a cubic lattice, each coupled to its neighbours so that they prefer to point the same way. The equations treat every direction alike, so the magnet has to choose one spontaneously. In one and two dimensions this cannot happen at any positive temperature; in three, it should. In 1976 Dyson, Lieb and Simon announced a proof, but it contained an error. Their method works for the antiferromagnet with larger spins, where neighbours point opposite ways, but fails for the ferromagnet. The problem stayed on open-problem lists, Simon’s in 1984 and Lieb’s in 1999, and in 2025 Seiringer called it “the famous open problem”. There is also a textbook formula: in 1930 Bloch predicted that at low temperature spin waves reduce the magnetization below its maximum by an amount growing like T3/2.

Result 271 is four OpenAI preprints, 176 pages, dated September 24 and October 5, 2026. The first claims spontaneous magnetization: for nearest-neighbour Heisenberg ferromagnets in every dimension from three up and every spin, at low enough temperature, there is an equilibrium state magnetized to at least a quarter of the maximum. It uses its own formulation of the problem; a companion proves the averaged two-point version in three dimensions. The second claims Bloch’s law with its exact coefficient in three dimensions, for every spin and any finite-range ferromagnetic couplings that connect the whole lattice, taking the infinite volume first, then zero field, then low temperature. A third derives the next term for nearest neighbours, Dyson’s lattice correction at T5/2; in Dyson’s units its coefficient is exactly Dyson’s. A fourth shows that in a large box with no field, the magnetization has a fixed length and a uniformly random direction.

What it doesn’t claim

Dyson’s interaction term or anything beyond the first correction, an explicit temperature range, the Curie temperature, or Bloch’s law outside three dimensions. The order of limits matters: on a 3×3×3 cube at temperature 0.1, free spin waves lose almost nothing; in infinite volume they lose about 1% of the maximum (our calculation).

How the proofs go

  1. Loops around imaginary time. The proofs split each spin into slots and write the thermal state as loops winding around a circle of imaginary time. Every loop touching the edge of a box is pinned up, which costs only surface energy. The question becomes whether a loop deep inside reaches the edge, and that is a random walk among sparse obstacles, which escapes in three dimensions.
  2. Where Bloch’s coefficient comes from. A return of the walk after n windings contributes in proportion to n−3/2, and summing over windings gives ζ(3/2), the number at the heart of Bloch’s coefficient. The first winding alone gives 38% of it.

The numbers in the video
